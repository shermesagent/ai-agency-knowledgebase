# Magnifica Humanitas

## Core Idea
*Magnifica Humanitas* is Pope Leo XIV's first papal encyclical, published on May 25, 2026 — the 135th anniversary of *Rerum Novarum*, Pope Leo XIII's encyclical on labor during the industrial revolution. It is the first papal encyclical devoted entirely to artificial intelligence. The core concept is **"disarming technology"** — not slowing AI development, but preventing it from becoming a form of power that dominates human existence. The encyclical reframes AI from a technical question ("what can it do?") to a human question ("who controls it, with what interests, and according to what idea of the human being?").

## Why It Matters
This is arguably the most significant institutional articulation of Superagency principles to date. The Catholic Church — the world's oldest continuous institution — has formally engaged with AI not as something to bless or condemn, but as a force reshaping power, work, truth, and human dignity. The encyclical matters because:
- It moves AI governance from tech-policy margins to the center of how humanity thinks about its future.
- It provides a moral vocabulary ("disarming technology," "digital Babylon," "construction site") that transcends any single faith tradition.
- It explicitly connects the industrial revolution's labor crisis to the digital revolution's agency crisis.
- It challenges the AI industry's power concentration while affirming AI's potential for human flourishing.

## Core Arguments

### 1. "Disarming Technology"
The central concept. Disarming AI means breaking "the equation between technical power and the right to govern." It means preventing AI from becoming an instrument of economic, political, or military domination by a select few. This is not a call for Luddism — the encyclical explicitly affirms technology's place in human creativity. It's a call to organize AI around human dignity rather than power concentration.

### 2. Power Concentration Is the Central Risk
"A few players concentrate digital infrastructure, data, and computing capacity," which "affects information, economics, and even democracy." The encyclical states that it's not enough to merely regulate technology — "It must be taken away from monopolies, made transparent and open to challenge — that is, made 'habitable' by a plurality of actors."

### 3. Work as Dignity, Not Just Productivity
AI risks a "social calamity" of technological unemployment when innovation is driven primarily by cost-cutting. Beyond job loss: AI that reduces workers to "measurable, controllable, and replaceable functions" creates a "social, political, and profoundly human issue." The encyclical concerns itself with automated surveillance, task fragmentation, and loss of autonomy — not just employment statistics.

### 4. Truth Infrastructure
The problem isn't just fake news but systems that select what's visible based on engagement rather than truth. "Truth does not disappear, but it becomes dependent on opaque systems that influence opinions, perceptions, and collective choices." The solution: "train people capable of recognizing these mechanisms."

### 5. The "Construction Site" Metaphor
The encyclical's closing image: AI is not a closed system or predetermined future but "a process still under construction." The question is not what AI can do, but who controls it, with what interests, and according to what idea of the human being.

### 6. No Lethal Autonomy
A clear bright line: "It is not acceptable to entrust lethal or irreversible decisions to artificial systems. Moral responsibility cannot be delegated, nor can it be dissolved in automated chains."

## Practical Examples
- The Vatican-Anthropic alliance: Anthropic cofounder Christopher Olah spoke at the encyclical's presentation, signaling institutional engagement between moral authority and AI safety research.
- Constitutional AI: Anthropic's approach of training models with explicit principles mirrors the encyclical's call for embedding values in technology design.
- The "disarming technology" audit: applying the encyclical's framework to any AI system — who controls it? Does it concentrate or distribute power? Is it transparent?

## Risks / Limits
- The Vatican-Anthropic alliance risks mutual legitimation — the Church gets relevance, Anthropic gets moral cover — without producing meaningful governance mechanisms.
- The encyclical's power-critique challenges every major AI company's business model; implementation requires political will, not just moral clarity.
- "Disarming technology" is a powerful metaphor but translating it into policy, code, and institutional design is the hard part.
- The encyclical's audience is primarily Catholic; its arguments need translation for secular, multi-faith, and non-religious contexts.
