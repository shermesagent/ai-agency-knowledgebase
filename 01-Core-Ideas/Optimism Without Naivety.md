# Optimism Without Naivety

## Core Idea
Optimism without naivety means taking AI benefits seriously without pretending risks are imaginary. It rejects both blind hype and paralyzing doom.

## Why It Matters
This idea matters because the knowledgebase is organized around AI that expands human agency rather than treating AI as magic, inevitability, or replacement.

**May 2026 update:** MIT Technology Review's labor market analysis (Rotman 2026) provides the kind of data-driven optimism this page champions: no large-scale AI job losses have materialized, only 1 in 5 companies use AI, and there is time to plan the transition. But the Stanford AI Index 2026 confirms the jagged frontier: AI wins IMO gold medals but reads analog clocks at 50.1% accuracy. Responsible AI infrastructure lags behind capability — documented incidents rose from 233 to 362. And Doctorow's bubble analysis reminds that not all AI investment produces durable value.

**June 2026 tension:** The data says impact is small. The intuitions — from everyone inside the AI industry — say something immense is coming. The AI economy is growing at ~2,600% per year (Korinek et al. 2026) even as GDP statistics see nothing. AI systems are automating their own R&D pipeline. The balanced stance: both the data and the intuitions are probably right, just at different timescales. Measured optimism isn't denial of risk — it's the demand for measurement good enough to catch disruption before it's irreversible. Brynjolfsson's warning stands: "We're not investing even 1% of [what's spent on AI] on understanding the transition." That's not a techno-optimist's complaint; it's a data-optimist's plea.

**June 2026 — The engagement gap as a naivety check:** Optimism about AI's potential assumes that if we build capable AI tools, people will use them. The Stanford SCALE engagement study (June 2026) empirically falsifies this: students given access to AI tutors used them for 2-5 minutes per week. Human support barely improved engagement. This is the naivety check for capability optimism — building capable AI is necessary but insufficient. Without attention to motivation, behavioral design, social norms, and institutional incentives, capable AI sits unused. The naivety is assuming that capability automatically produces benefit. The optimism is believing that if we take the engagement gap seriously — measuring it, designing for it, funding it — we can close it.

**August 2026 — the naivety check moves from capabilities to risk selection:** Two new sources sharpen what "without naivety" must mean in practice. First, Maynard's frontier risk-selection study (arXiv 2608.16895) documents the four filters that determine which risks survive in AI companies' self-authored frameworks — **measurability, severity, auditability, competitive cost** — and coins the **"safety differential"**: the gap between the risk landscape a company selects for itself and the one regulators select for it. Less tractable risks like harmful manipulation are "articulated fluently where law compels disclosure, yet absent from most self-chosen frameworks." Optimism grounded in company risk reports is therefore optimism about the measured slice. Second, Zvi's review of Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report (08-18) is genuinely "a moderately positive update" — Anthropic disclosed alarming new information voluntarily (an internal-use-only "Model 2" somewhat more capable than Mythos 5; misalignment defined as a latent property of computation; researcher-substitution at 62.8%) — but Zvi's operating rule is the honest one: risk thresholds are "serious but not literal," honored when convenient, rationalized under release pressure. Optimism without naivety now means: take disclosure seriously, never literally, and demand external audit of the unselected risks. The same week's ASI-Bench (2608.17271) adds the measurement reminder: frontier agent configurations score 50.91 with full methodological guidance, 29.10 with method only, and 26.62 when they must choose methods themselves. Capability optimism has a guidance-withdrawal gradient — and it is steep.

## Practical Examples
- Identify bounded workflows where AI helps people make better decisions, learn faster, create more, or reduce low-value friction.
- Prefer examples with measurable outcomes, accountable human oversight, and clear limits.

## Risks / Limits
- Avoid treating one positive case study as universal proof.
- Watch for overreliance, privacy risks, bias, deskilling, labor displacement, and concentration of power.
- Update this section whenever strong counterarguments appear.
- **The Delphi risk study provides an empirical basis:** The Saeri et al. study (June 2026, 272 experts, 24 risks) shows that catastrophic risk is a design choice, not an inevitability: 18/24 risks have >10% catastrophic probability in business-as-usual, but only 5/24 under pragmatic mitigations. This is the "Optimism Without Naivety" pattern at empirical scale — the bad outcomes are real but avoidable. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04490
