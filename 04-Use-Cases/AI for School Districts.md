# AI for School Districts

## Core Idea
School districts can use AI for teacher support, tutoring pilots, curriculum alignment, translation, family communication, analytics, and operational efficiency — but only inside clear educational goals, privacy rules, and human review.

## Why It Matters
Districts are where AI adoption becomes institutional. A classroom teacher can experiment with prompts, but districts decide policy, procurement, student-data boundaries, assessment norms, parent communication, professional development, and evaluation. If districts wait too long, informal use fills the vacuum; if they adopt too quickly, they can normalize surveillance, weak pedagogy, or vendor lock-in.

The agency goal is to help students and teachers do more meaningful learning work, not to automate schooling.

## Best Supporting Sources
- [AI Guidance for Schools Toolkit](https://www.teachai.org/toolkit-users), TeachAI — practical resource for system leaders developing or updating AI guidance and policy aligned to educational goals and compliance.
- [A Guide to AI in Schools: Perspectives for the Perplexed](https://tsl.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/GuideToAIInSchools.pdf), MIT Teaching Systems Lab, 2025 — educator-facing guide for ethical, instructional, and community conversations about AI.
- [2025 AI in Education: A Microsoft Special Report](https://cdn-dynmedia-1.microsoft.com/is/content/microsoftcorp/microsoft/bade/documents/products-and-services/en-us/education/2025-Microsoft-AI-in-Education-Report.pdf), Microsoft Education, 2025 — adoption-pattern report emphasizing student agency, classroom conversation, confidence, and curiosity.
- [Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Teaching and Learning](https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/documents/ai-report/ai-report.pdf), U.S. Department of Education, 2023 — broad guidance on teaching, learning, assessment, equity, privacy, and human judgment.
- [AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), NIST — useful for procurement, pilot design, risk mapping, measurement, and governance.

## Practical Examples
- Create a district AI guidance page with allowed uses, prohibited uses, disclosure norms, data privacy requirements, and parent-facing explanations.
- Run one bounded AI tutoring or feedback pilot with pre/post learning evidence, teacher logs, student reflection, and an incident record.
- Offer professional development where teachers redesign one assignment for AI-aware process, revision, and oral defense rather than only policing cheating.
- Use AI to translate family communications and draft individualized supports, with human review before sending.
- Require [[Human Review Checkpoints]] before AI-generated grades, disciplinary recommendations, special education decisions, placement recommendations, or parent communications.
- Maintain a procurement rubric: instructional fit, privacy, accessibility, evidence, bias testing, interoperability, cost, and exit plan.

## Risks / Limits
- AI policy can become symbolic if teachers lack time, examples, and support.
- Vendor reports may highlight success stories without enough local evidence; districts still need their own pilots and outcome measures.
- Privacy and surveillance risks are especially high with minors.
- Overly restrictive rules can make responsible teachers hide useful experimentation; overly permissive rules can make students and teachers absorb the risk individually.
- AI tools should strengthen teacher agency, not turn educators into passive monitors of automated instruction.
