# The Turing Trap

## Core Idea
The Turing Trap is Erik Brynjolfsson’s warning that AI progress can be misdirected when society rewards machines for imitating or replacing humans instead of augmenting what humans can do.

## Why It Matters
This is one of the most important counterweights to shallow AI optimism. [[Superagency]] depends on design and incentives: the same technical capability can either expand human productivity and judgment or concentrate returns by substituting for workers.

## Best Supporting Sources
- [The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04200), Erik Brynjolfsson, 2022 — distinguishes augmentation from substitution and warns against overvaluing human-like imitation.
- [The 5 faces of human readiness for AI adoption](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/06/ai-workplace-adoption-readiness/), David Timis / WEF, June 2026 — five employee postures and the readiness gap as a driver of augmentation vs. substitution outcomes.
- [A reality check on the AI jobs hysteria](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/26/1137855/a-reality-check-on-the-ai-jobs-hysteria/), David Rotman / MIT Technology Review, May 2026 — early entry-level decline concentrated in automation-use cases; wages rising where AI augments.
- [Microsoft Build 2026: Agent 365, ASSERT, and the Agent Control Specification](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/06/02/microsoft-build-2026-be-yourself-at-work/), June 2026 — governance infrastructure that makes augmentation vs. substitution auditable at the code level.
- [[Work]] and [[AI and Inequality]] — wiki pages where this argument has direct implications.

## Practical Examples
- Evaluate a workplace AI pilot by asking whether it complements employees’ judgment or merely removes them from the loop.
- Prefer tools that make workers more capable, transparent, and accountable over tools that hide labor displacement behind “efficiency.”
- Use task-level analysis before deciding whether AI should automate, augment, or stay out of a workflow.

## Risks / Limits
- Some automation can be beneficial when it removes unsafe, tedious, or low-value work.
- The trap is not automation itself; it is optimizing around replacement without shared prosperity, human development, and institutional accountability.

## Modern Restatement: The "Inconvenient Humans" Thesis
Cory Doctorow's June 2, 2026 column restates the Turing Trap in structural terms: AI deployment is being shaped not by what maximizes productivity but by what serves capital's preference for workers who make no demands. "AI makes no demands, requires no moral consideration, and does not attempt to germinate a culture, a cuisine, or a language in your sacred soil." The trap isn't just a design choice at the task level — it's embedded in the political economy of who controls deployment and for what purpose. Augmentation requires institutional design that favors it; without that, Brynjolfsson's warning that AI can be steered toward replacement becomes a prediction of the default trajectory.

## The Readiness Dimension: Why Augmentation Isn't Just a Technical Choice

The WEF's June 2026 "five faces of human readiness" framework reveals that augmentation vs. substitution is shaped by psychological and cultural factors, not just technical design. Two competing narratives determine whether AI tilts toward augmentation or replacement:

- **Top-down narrative:** AI as a plug-and-play solution for value creation. Leadership enthusiasm drives adoption with minimal worker input. This narrative tends toward substitution because it treats workers as interchangeable inputs to a production function.
- **Bottom-up narrative:** Employee anxiety about job loss, critical thinking erosion, and distrust of tech executives. This narrative produces resistance that can paradoxically push organizations toward more aggressive automation — if workers won't adopt AI willingly, leadership may impose it.

The trap has a readiness dimension: organizations that close the narrative gap (by validating concerns, co-designing workflows, and investing in psychological safety alongside technical training) are more likely to achieve augmentation. Those that don't default to substitution regardless of what augmentation is technically possible.

## Empirical Context: The Data So Far

MIT Technology Review's May 2026 labor market analysis (Rotman) provides the empirical backdrop: no mass AI-driven displacement has materialized, unemployment is lower in AI-exposed occupations, and wages in AI-exposed sectors are rising — suggesting employers value tacit knowledge AI can't replicate. However, Stanford Digital Economy Lab data reveals a 16% decline in entry-level jobs for 22-25-year-olds in AI-exposed fields since 2024, concentrated where AI automates rather than augments. The trap is already claiming early casualties at the entry level.

## Governance as Escape Hatch

Microsoft's June 2026 Agent 365 platform — with ASSERT (policy-driven safety evaluation) and the open Agent Control Specification — represents a governance response to the Turing Trap: making agent behavior observable, testable, and remediable at the code level rather than the policy level. When augmentation vs. substitution can be audited and governed at the infrastructure layer, the trap becomes escapable through design rather than hope. The AI-IARA framework (van Zyl, 2026) extends this logic to psychological capacities: if AI systems are audited for their effects on human Awareness, Interpretation, Intention, Action, Relational Agency, and Autonomy, augmentation becomes measurable — not just aspirational.
