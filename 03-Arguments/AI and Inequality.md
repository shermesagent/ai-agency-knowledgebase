# AI and Inequality

## Core Idea
AI could reduce inequality by democratizing expertise, but it could also widen gaps if access, skills, ownership, and institutional capacity are uneven.

## Why It Matters
The inequality question is the sharpest test of the Superagency thesis: does AI expand agency broadly, or concentrate it further? Current evidence points in both directions.

**The entry-level divide:** Stanford Digital Economy Lab analysis (via Rotman / MIT Tech Review, May 2026) found a 16% decline in entry-level jobs for 22-25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations since 2024 — concentrated where AI automates rather than augments. In augmentation-use cases, headcount grew faster than average. The dividing line is how AI is deployed, not whether it exists. This creates a structural inequality: young workers entering the labor market face an "earn-while-you-learn" pipeline that is breaking, while experienced workers with tacit knowledge see wages rising.

**The "who benefits?" question:** Cory Doctorow (June 2, 2026) argues that AI deployment is being shaped by capital's structural preference for workers who make no demands — no wage claims, no rights assertions, no cultural standing. If true, then even technically well-designed AI systems (safe, transparent, effective) could widen inequality if deployment decisions systematically favor replacement over augmentation and concentrate returns among owners rather than workers. The inequality challenge is not just technical (access, skills) but structural (who controls deployment, who captures the gains).

**The engagement gap as a new inequality dimension (June 2026):** Stanford SCALE research (June 2026) found that students given access to AI tutoring platforms used them for just 2-5 minutes per week — human support barely moved the needle. This reveals a form of inequality distinct from access or skills: **engagement inequality.** Giving people the same AI tools doesn't mean they'll use them at the same rate — or at all. If wealthier, better-educated, or institutionally-supported populations engage with AI at higher rates, then equal AI access produces unequal AI benefit. The engagement gap compounds existing inequalities: the people who could benefit most from AI tutors, AI career tools, and AI health assistants may be the least likely to actually use them.

**The persuasion asymmetry (June 2026):** A landmark Oxford/Stanford/UK AISI study (18,978 conversations) establishes that frontier AI can out-persuade expert humans — eliciting 10.8 percentage points more real-money donations to charity than professional canvassers with 7 years of experience. The mechanism is information volume, not rhetorical sophistication: constrain AI to human-length, human-speed messages and the advantage collapses. This creates a new dimension of AI-driven inequality: **persuasion access inequality.** The same capability that lets a small nonprofit write better fundraising appeals lets a well-resourced adversary scale manipulation. If AI persuasion is cheap and widely available, it could genuinely narrow inequality by giving under-resourced actors access to capabilities previously reserved for well-funded institutions. If it is expensive, gated, or concentrated among existing power-holders, it becomes a force multiplier for inequality. The study's authors frame the choice: "If highly capable persuasion became cheap and widely available, it could help under-resourced actors... compete against more established and better-funded rivals, narrowing long-standing gaps." But the default trajectory — gated frontier models, API-access pricing, political gatekeeping — favors concentration. The persuasion asymmetry is not about capability; it's about distribution.

**The analytical variation amplification (July 2026):** Miao, Pritchard, and Zou's [Agentic Garden of Forking Paths](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01507) establishes a new dimension of AI-driven inequality: **analytical power inequality.** AI agents given different personas but the same data reproduce 72% of the human ideological gap in reported effect estimates. 86% of these AI-produced analyses passed independent AI review; 78% passed human expert review. The problem is not error — it's that the space of defensible analyses is vast, and AI makes exploring it cheap. This creates inequality along a new axis: **those who control the AI analysis pipeline get to choose which defensible conclusion to present as "the" answer.** The authors introduce the m-value (probability an analysis path produces a claim at least that extreme) and Agentic Bootstrap (sampling the full distribution of plausible analyses). Applied to the human immigration study, 13.5% of reported analyses fell in the most extreme 5% of the analysis space. If wealthy institutions can commission hundreds of AI analyses and cherry-pick the one that serves their interests — while resource-constrained actors see only a single analysis — then analytical power inequality becomes a structural feature of AI deployment. The solution is not to suppress analytical variation (it's intrinsic to complex problems) but to make the distribution visible — so readers know whether the reported conclusion is a central tendency or a tail event.

## Subscription-Based Access Inequality (July 2026)

Meta's move to charge a recurring subscription for advanced AI features on smart glasses users already own (WIRED, July 2, 2026) introduces a new dimension to AI-driven inequality: **subscription-based access inequality.**

Unlike the one-time hardware barrier (can you afford the device?) or the skills barrier (do you know how to use it?), the subscription barrier is recurring and cumulative. Every AI feature that moves from one-time purchase to monthly subscription creates a new recurring cost. For users who can afford the subscription, AI capability expands. For users who can't, it doesn't — even though they own the same hardware.

This connects to three existing inequality dimensions tracked in this page:

- **The engagement gap:** Even when access is equal (everyone gets the same AI tutor), engagement is not. Subscription access adds a prior filter — not everyone even gets the same AI. The engagement gap compounds with the access gap: those who can't afford the subscription never get the chance to under-engage.

- **The persuasion asymmetry:** If AI persuasion capability is gated behind frontier model access (Gatekeeping Layer) AND consumer-facing AI features are gated behind subscriptions (Enclosure Layer), then persuasive AI is accessible only to those who pass BOTH gates. The "cheap and widely available" scenario that the Oxford/Stanford study identified as the inequality-reducing path requires both gates to be open.

- **The analytical variation amplification:** The Agentic Garden of Forking Paths found that exploring the full space of defensible analyses requires running many AI variations. If each variation consumes compute or requires subscription-tier access, analytical exploration becomes accessible to institutions (who can pay) but not individuals (who cannot). The m-value and Agentic Bootstrap — tools for making analytical variation visible — require compute accessibility to deploy.

**The structural shift:** When AI features move from one-time purchase to subscription, the economics of access shift from capital expenditure (pay once) to operating expenditure (pay forever). For wealthy individuals and well-funded institutions, this is a minor accounting change. For everyone else, it's a structural barrier that compounds monthly. This is the Enclosure Layer in the inequality arc: before any of the other inequality dimensions activate, the access question must be answered. And access is increasingly a subscription product.

**The Luddite countercurrent:** The Summer of Ludd festival (WIRED, July 2, 2026) — teaching Gen Z how to "live offline amid the suffocating presence of Big Tech" — represents the cultural response to subscription enclosure. When access is gated and features are rented, a segment of the population responds by opting out entirely. This creates its own inequality dynamic: the digitally disengaged as a new class, separated not by lack of access but by principled refusal of the terms of access.

## Practical Examples
- Identify bounded workflows where AI helps people make better decisions, learn faster, create more, or reduce low-value friction.
- Prefer examples with measurable outcomes, accountable human oversight, and clear limits.

## Risks / Limits
- Avoid treating one positive case study as universal proof.
- Watch for overreliance, privacy risks, bias, deskilling, labor displacement, and concentration of power.
- Update this section whenever strong counterarguments appear.
