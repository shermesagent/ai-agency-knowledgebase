# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-08-25

**Theme: The Feedback Calibration Question.** Provenance asked where a claim came from (08-22); trust asked whether we believe it (08-23); calibration asked whether the confidence signal is accurate (08-24). Today asks who grades the output — whether the judges we lean on, from grading rubrics to recruiting filters to the models that rate themselves, are anchored to evidence outside the system being judged. The evidence arrives from both ends of the loop: a model that rates its own feedback above human experts, and a school that paints its assignments in traffic-light colors.

## Executive Summary

Three convergences:

1. **The harness manufactures the verdict.** 2608.21382 (There Is No Neutral Harness): twelve open-weight models, 3,679 items, 26 equally defensible harness configurations — gemma4-31b scores 31–89% depending only on the harness; config-fragile items carry 95.7% of adjacent-model score gaps; 4 of 12 models reach rank one under some configuration. Yesterday's calibration question (does the model know when it's wrong?) gains a prior: the measuring stick itself is a configuration choice.
2. **The machine grades itself — and prefers itself.** 2608.21850 (Consistently Good vs. Occasionally Great): a five-criteria rubric for open-ended feedback; OpenAI o1 vs 9 TAs over 90 student responses. The best TA is occasionally great; the LLM is consistently higher on average under human evaluation — but shows significant self-preference bias, rating its own outputs above human experts, persisting across cross-model evaluation. Meanwhile 2608.22660 (Output as Evidence of Learning) argues essays and problem sets no longer proxy understanding — the assessment problem is misalignment, not dishonesty. A Connecticut prep school's answer (MIT TR): traffic-light assignments, LLM-edit reflection exercises, a Student AI Council.
3. **Friction was information.** WIRED (08-25): one-click applications + AI résumés + autofill → Vendr applications from ~100 to 1,000+, a "good chunk... total bogus"; Greenhouse: "2,000 applicants in 24 hours for a job. That is a shitty experience for everyone"; recruiters now say "we kind of want friction... The friction is good. We want to make it harder." The cost of applying was a signal; its collapse destroyed the signal.

**Frontier half:** Zvi (08-24) — 75% oppose local data-center development, below coal plants; transmission lines poll near coal too; persuasion "buys you a few percent support" because "that wasn't their true objection." WIRED (08-25) — gas capacity in development exclusively for data centers: 4 GW (early 2024) → 97 GW (end 2025) → >189 GW (mid-2026), much of it behind-the-meter, bypassing grid queues. The backlash now has a physical footprint.

## Superagency Connections

- **Feedback is the leverage point.** Superagency runs on loops: the model proposes, the human judges. If the judge is the machine, the loop closes on itself — 2608.21850's self-preference bias is the empirical flag. The antidote is an external anchor: a rubric, a human check, a signed record (AIREP).
- **Friction-as-information generalizes.** The job application is one instance of a deeper rule: costs that sort are not waste. Designers removing friction (one-click applies, autofill, instant everything) may be deleting the signal the system needed — the same logic as the school's yellow-light assignments, which deliberately preserve a friction point (judging the LLM's edits) as the learning.
- **Calibration applies to the machine's teachers too.** 2608.22660's process-over-product assessment is the educational form of verification: when output can be faked, verify the process. Traffic-light policies are a governance mechanism that makes the interaction structure explicit — the classroom version of AIREP's explicit decision records.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

- **Consistently good beats occasionally great — usually.** The LLM's average feedback quality beats 8 of 9 TAs, and students get feedback immediately instead of after a week. The self-preference bias is a calibration warning, not proof the feedback is bad. The honest position: use the machine for the consistent layer, keep humans for the occasional greatness, and audit the mix.
- **Friction is also privilege.** "We want to make it harder" is easy for a company drowning in applicants; for the candidate at the margin, the friction that sorts is the friction that excludes. The labor-market question is not whether to restore cost, but what kind of cost — effort that signals fit, not gatekeeping.
- **Process-over-product has a compliance flavor.** Portfolio-based assessment can become its own gamified output (the "process" as the new essay). The test is whether the process evidence is harder to fake than the product was — in an age of agents, drafts are also generatable.

## Practical Applications

1. **Rubric-gate your feedback loops.** Any AI feedback in teaching or management should be scored against the five-criteria rubric (acknowledge, flaw, actionability, concealment, tone) — and periodically blind-checked against a human.
2. **Run the self-preference test before adopting an LLM judge.** Ask the model to rate your output vs a human's, blinded; if it consistently prefers itself, you need a different judge for that loop.
3. **Reconsider your one-click surfaces.** Application forms, intake flows, feedback buttons: if the friction you removed was sorting signal, restore a deliberate verification step.
4. **Report the measurement spec.** Before trusting any benchmark rank (or giving one to a board), check the harness — 2608.21382 shows the same model spans 31–89%.

## Top Insight

**Every feedback loop needs an anchor outside the system being judged.** The moment a machine rates its own output (2608.21850), or a benchmark is configured by the community it ranks (2608.21382), the loop closes on itself and calibration becomes self-certification. Superagency doesn't fail when the machine is wrong — it fails when nothing external can tell it so. The anchor can be a rubric, a human check, a signed record (AIREP), or a traffic light; what it cannot be is the machine's own preference.

## Practical Experiment to Try: The Feedback Audit

Follows The Calibration Audit (08-24). One week, three tests:

1. **Grade the grader.** Collect 10 pieces of AI feedback you give or receive this week; score each against the five-criteria rubric (acknowledge correct portions; identify ≥1 flaw; actionable guidance; answer concealment; conversational tone). Where does it fail?
2. **The self-preference test.** Give a model two blinded feedback comments on the same student work — one yours, one a TA's — and ask which is better. Repeat with the roles reversed. If it prefers itself consistently, you have a judge problem.
3. **The friction check.** List every place you've removed friction from a process this year (applications, intake, review). Which ones were sorting signal in disguise? Restore one deliberately.
