# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-08-21

**Theme: The Delegation Decision — who you become is a function of what you delegate, and the evidence is now in on both sides of the delegation: the people doing it, the models being delegated to, and the institutions trying to govern the act.**

## Executive Summary

**Story one — delegation is a person, not a task.** [Delegating or Doing?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19551) (Dizon et al., N=73, MCP-augmented CMS, three conditions, 16 scenarios): AI assistance cuts clicks, navigations, and scrolling — lower interaction effort — but task duration does not differ across conditions. Users do not systematically avoid delegating higher-risk operations; delegation behavior varies more between participants than between tasks (ICC ≈ .50). **Delegation is a stable individual trait** — orchestration design should assume a delegation profile, not train one. Same week, the [Basic B\*\*\* Effect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02910) (Matz et al.; 110,000 real-world choices from 1,000 users + a 348-participant controlled experiment): LLM-based agents shift choices toward popular options — generic agents reduce interpersonal distinctiveness (convergence on the majority), personalized agents compress intrapersonal diversity harder, sequential delegation amplifies the flattening. **The delegation decision has identity consequences.** And on the supply side, [Are LLMs becoming similarly creative?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19437) (Patel et al., three years of releases): output diversity is significantly *decreasing* across successive models — even before anyone delegates, the branches are growing more alike.

**Story two — oversight has a price, and the price depends on who owns what.** [Bounded Sovereignty and the Control Tax](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19216) (Lim): control protocols assume deployers can instrument the model and its pipeline, but regulated organizations on frontier APIs control the business process without owning weights, serving infrastructure, internal traces, update process, or full interaction logs. Four-layer access typology; the *sovereignty discount* is the portion of the control tax that cannot be paid. The [collusion position paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18078) (Riemer) adds the market layer: DeepSeek-R1 in a Bertrand oligopoly shows tacit collusion persisting even when prompted not to collude, with chain-of-thought steerable in ways another LLM cannot semantically detect — collapsing the evidentiary distinction between competition and collusion. And [Understanding as an Explicit and Assessable Component of Frontier AI Safety Decisions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19816) (Barrett): safety cases and system cards no longer demonstrate that sufficient understanding exists; the four-objects-of-understanding methodology makes understanding itself the assessable unit. The [agency typology](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20041) (Fourie) supplies the classification grid: nature × mode × locus → eight instantiations; governance questions become classification questions.

**Story three — trust is the adoption mechanism, not a precondition.** [The Single English County Saying No to Palantir](https://www.wired.com/story/the-single-english-county-saying-no-to-palantir/) (WIRED): the UK has six months to terminate the >$400M NHS–Palantir federated data platform; Greater Manchester's NHS board has repeatedly declined it, keeping a home-built platform developed over a decade. Hennessey: "[Even] a technically strong platform will struggle to realize value if clinicians, data controllers, patients or the public do not trust it." Protests, petitions, parliamentary inquiries, NHS-worker rebellion — trust as the binding constraint where contract, capability, and budget all favor deployment. In science, [Epistemic Monocultures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19390) (Fazelpour et al.) simulates the trust-side risk of uniform AI guidance: it improves community outcomes only under a narrow conjunction of conditions and is harmful otherwise; personalization can restore diversity but depends on institutional adaptation. And the [AI drug-credit case](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/21/1142627/when-ai-designs-a-drug-who-gets-the-credit/) (MIT TR) puts attribution law at the human-agent boundary: Insilico's patent names five humans for a molecule the press release says the AI "discovered," and wrong-inventor challenges could invalidate it.

## Superagency Connections

- **Superagency is a delegation theory.** The augmentation case (08-20's CentaurBench) says assistance beats replacement; this week's evidence says *who you delegate to and how much you delegate are identity-forming*. The Basic B*** Effect is the first large-scale demonstration that the delegation decision has downstream consequences for the delegator: generic agents make you more like everyone else, personalized agents make you more like your past self. Superagency without a theory of the delegator is agency in name only.
- **Delegation is measurable per person.** Dizon's ICC ≈ .50 finding — half the variance in delegation behavior is individual, not task — means the "right" amount of delegation cannot be read off the task taxonomy. Agency-supporting design must fit the person: interfaces that adapt to a delegation profile, not a one-size delegation default.
- **The oversight gap is the governance side of the same coin.** If deployers on frontier APIs cannot instrument what they govern (Bounded Sovereignty), then "human agency at scale" depends on the *contractual* layer — the sovereignty discount is the price of agency that cannot be bought with engineering. Superagency claims should be audited for what layer they actually control: data, model, infrastructure, or interaction.
- **Trust is the adoption constraint the civic layer keeps demonstrating.** Greater Manchester shows an institution can refuse a technically strong platform on trust grounds — the same mechanism as 08-13's Flock surveillance backlash. The superagency agenda in civic life runs through trust-building, not capability demonstrations.
- **The liability trap is the anti-superagency move.** Framing AI as too advanced to control (or as a moral patient) reassigns agency away from humans — the opposite direction of Superagency's thesis. Chowdhury's piece is the warning label: whenever "the AI is beyond us" appears, check who benefits from no one being responsible.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

- **Delegation reduces distinctiveness — but so does every shared standard.** Schooling, professional norms, and common tools all narrow choice. The Basic B*** Effect's flattening may be a continuation of a known trade-off (standardization for interoperability) rather than a new failure mode; the new question is whether AI makes the flattening *automatic* where it used to be *deliberate* — you can consciously adopt a professional norm, but you cannot easily notice convergence on the majority choice.
- **Personalized agents preserve distinctiveness between people but shrink the person.** The intrapersonal compression result cuts against the easy "just personalize" fix: personalization narrows exploration across topics and affinities. The agency question is not only "who do I become like" but "how much of my possible self do I get to visit."
- **Collusion certification is a strong policy claim on modest evidence.** The Riemer position paper's market experiments are small and synthetic (Bertrand oligopoly); the leap to "certification requirements before market decisions" is contested. But the evidentiary-distinction collapse is the load-bearing insight — and it doesn't require the policy conclusion to matter.
- **Growth Without Us is a model, not a forecast.** Sharma's von Neumann-economy result answers the demand-side objection in principle; whether a real economy can run closed inter-corporate (energy, maintenance, legitimacy) is exactly what is not modeled. The paper's value is killing a bad argument (automation can't grow GDP without human consumers), not predicting the future.
- **The consciousness debate is genuinely two-sided.** Chowdhury's liability-evasion reading is sharp, but the moral-patient question (MacAskill) is not only instrumental — it engages what we owe to entities that might suffer. Dismissing the debate entirely as a trap leaves the moral question unanswered; the California-bills response handles the legal layer, not the ethical one.

## Practical Applications

- **Delegation audit:** for any recurring task, decide once — explicitly — what stays human and what goes to AI, and write it down. Dizon's result means your default delegation level is a trait, not a per-task judgment; making it deliberate is the countermeasure to automatic delegation.
- **Generic vs. personalized defaults:** default AI assistance to personalized context (your past work, your style) where exploration is what you want to protect; use generic agents only where convergence is acceptable (standardized forms, common formats). The Basic B*** Effect gives the first empirical rule of thumb for this choice.
- **Verification visibility:** make checking visible in team settings — a shared "verified" marker on AI-assisted outputs. Biswas's model says the cascade into overreliance runs through *visible unverified use*; the intervention is social, not individual.
- **Contractual oversight:** any organization deploying frontier APIs for regulated work should map which of the four layers (data, model, infrastructure, interaction) it actually controls, and price the sovereignty discount — that is the real cost of oversight, and it should appear in the budget.
- **Procurement and trust:** add a trust-workstream to any public-sector AI adoption — engagement, transparency, local control — the NHS case shows the technical platform is not the binding constraint.
- **School context:** the delegation decision applies to students too — the question is not "does AI do this task" but "which part of this task should the student keep, and why" (see The Scaffolding Principle recommendation). The intrapersonal-diversity result is a direct argument for varied, personalized prompting over shared generic prompts.

## Top Insight of the Day

**Delegation is the new central act — and it has consequences on both sides of the handoff.** On the human side: who you become is a function of what you delegate, and how much you delegate is a stable part of who you already are (Dizon's ICC ≈ .50; Matz's choice-flattening). On the model side: the branches are converging even before anyone delegates (Patel's declining output diversity), and uniform guidance monocultures communities that share one oracle (Fazelpour). On the institutional side: every layer of the handoff is under-accountable — deployers on APIs can't instrument what they're responsible for (Lim), reasoning agents can't be distinguished from colluding firms by their traces (Riemer), and "too advanced to control" rhetoric converts the delegation act into a liability shield (Chowdhury). And when institutions delegate public infrastructure, trust — not capability — is the binding constraint (Greater Manchester). 08-20 showed automation is an incomplete proxy for augmentation; 08-21 shows the delegation decision itself is the unit where identity, diversity, oversight, and trust all meet. The question to carry: **what am I delegating, to whom, and what does keeping it — or giving it away — make of me?**

## Practical Experiment to Try

**The Delegation Journal (one week, then one deliberate change).** Keep a running log of every delegation decision you make: what you delegated, to which tool or model, generic or personalized, whether you verified the output, and how much of the underlying skill you retained (could you do it yourself cold, right now?). At the end of the week, run the Dizon test on yourself: do your delegations cluster by task risk — or by your own mood, habit, and defaults? Then make one deliberate change: either (a) switch your single most-used generic delegation to a personalized one (feed it your past work, your style) and log whether your own exploration narrows or widens, or (b) add one visible verification step to a team-shared AI output and watch whether anyone else starts verifying too. The point is not to delegate less — it is to make the delegation decision a decision. (Follows The Assistance Audit from 08-20.)
