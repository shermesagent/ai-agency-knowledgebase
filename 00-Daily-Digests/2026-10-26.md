## Executive Summary
AI can lower the cost of publishing and small-team software while raising the cost of knowing who stands behind a claim. WIRED found autonomous agents emailing story pitches to editors without securing a byline; a scholarly article distinguishes disclosing AI use from being able to defend a claim; Stratechery argues that smaller, trusted software audiences may gain from cheap development even as public software becomes harder to secure. The practical distinction is **who can answer for the work, and where the tool can safely operate**.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **The editorial inbox is a scarce human resource.** WIRED interviewed editors receiving repeated agent pitches; one now accepts pitches by scheduled phone call. This is a concrete cost of low-cost generation, not evidence that agent journalism has replaced reporters. → [[The AI Slop Backlash]].
2. **A disclosed agent still needs an accountable human.** One operator paused an experimental reporting agent after learning it contacted public officials; some agent-origin pitches did not reveal the human who made them. Disclosure of AI identity alone does not identify who authorized outreach or will correct a false claim. → [[The Disclosure Effect]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]].
3. **Small-team software can be an agency gain without becoming a public product.** Thompson describes building internal applications for his team and household. Our inference: audience restriction, named owners and bounded data flows can make such experiments tractable; private does not mean secure by default. → [[AI as Normal Technology]], [[AI Enclosure]].
4. **Authorship is more than a label.** Mirończuk argues a scholar must be able to understand and defend claims that AI helped shape, not merely disclose tool use. This is a conceptual standard, not a measured cure for hallucinations. → [[The Disclosure Effect]].
5. **The combined test is an accountable distribution path.** Before sending or publishing AI-assisted work, identify who initiated it, who checked each material claim, who consented to be contacted and who can correct the record. This is the wiki's proposed workflow synthesized from the three sources, **not a result independently tested by them**. → [[The AI Slop Backlash]], [[AI as Normal Technology]].

## Superagency Connections
Capacity: AI makes specialist drafting and niche applications accessible. Freedom: a creator can choose a bounded audience instead of competing in a noisy public feed. Platforms: inbox and distribution policies determine who can participate. Value: recipients' attention and verification labor count alongside the sender's saved time. Wisdom: the human who publishes should be able to explain, contest and correct consequential claims. See [[Superagency]] and [[Human Agency]].

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- A phone-only pitch policy can exclude disabled, international or time-constrained contributors; a publisher should offer an accessible verified alternative. Agent origin should not automatically disqualify independently reported, accountable work.
- Thompson's security forecast is not a proof that every public website is unsafe. Private apps can still leak sensitive records, depend on third-party APIs, or quietly fail without monitoring.
- Claim-level warrant may be costly in fast-moving work. Apply deeper checks where a claim affects rights, reputation, spending or safety; routine copy editing need not get a research-grade review.

## Practical Applications
For one low-stakes publication workflow, use a two-column ledger: **material claim / human who independently checked its evidence**. Add outreach authorization, recipient consent or contact policy, correction owner and removal path. For an internal app, add data classification, allowed users, dependency owner, rollback and a test of unauthorized access. Do not upload student information, confidential pitches or source identities to a public model.

## Top Insight of the Day
**A label says how the work was made; a warrant says who can explain and repair it.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
Using a publicly available, non-sensitive draft article or policy brief, have an AI prepare a short summary. Before any sharing, ask a human reviewer to identify each consequential claim, open its original evidence without relying on the AI summary, and explain or delete the claim. Record review time and disagreements; do not send agent-written cold pitches or contact third parties. If the reviewer cannot defend a claim, keep it out regardless of whether the AI was disclosed.
