# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-27

**Theme:** More leverage is not automatically more agency. Intent control, credible measurement, creative breadth and access to skill all determine who benefits.

## Executive summary
A first-person account of unusually productive AI-assisted building illustrates what one expert can accomplish, while three research preprints test different parts of that promise. An intent-driven feed can rank content more effectively without proving users feel more in control; a benchmark audit questions how confidently skill gains and losses can be inferred; a creative-work study finds individual quality gains alongside less variety across a group. These sources are useful as design and evaluation prompts, **not** as district-level effect estimates or proof of causation beyond their study settings.

## Top 5 findings

### Best sources found
1. **Individual leverage is tangible but selective.** [Ben Thompson, *The Individual Exception* (Nov. 24)](https://stratechery.com/2026/the-individual-exception/) describes personally building apps and a distributed agent harness in days. This is one highly skilled operator's account, not a labor-market sample. [[The Turing Trap]] asks who can access the same leverage and who maintains it.
2. **User intent can be optimized without demonstrating user control.** [Luo et al., *Agentic Waterfall* (Nov. 25)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34407) report up to 16.1% relative improvement against a baseline on their intent-oriented dataset, built with simulated interactions. Inspectable goals and reversible preference inferences still need live testing. [[AI as Copilot]] and [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]] frame the distinction.
3. **Skill benchmark scores depend on what the test actually loads and grades.** [Zhang and Long, *What Does Skill Lift Actually Measure?* (Nov. 25)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33943) audit 7,554 public trials and identify grader dependence, placeholder skill directories and a harness that adds built-in skills. This is a preprint audit of one benchmark. [[Adoption Readiness Checklist]] should track measurement configuration before drawing adoption conclusions.
4. **Apparent negative skill effects and clever gating need replication.** The [same reanalysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33943) finds no task-level negative effect statistically significant after multiple-testing correction; a gating rule that looked better on training logs did not show a statistically significant improvement on held-out runs. This does **not** establish that all skills help, only that a small negative cell is weak evidence of harm. [[Human Review Checkpoints]] remain necessary.
5. **Creative quality and collective diversity can diverge.** [Nguyen et al., *Dynamics of Collective Diversity* (Nov. 15; surfaced in current RSS)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33192) report improved quality and reduced effort but lower diversity across participants in two creative domains, with interaction design and varied agent policies mitigating the drop in their study. [[Creativity]] needs a group-level variety measure, not just a quality rubric.

## Superagency connections
The common thread is a **two-sided agency test**: measure what the individual can now produce *and* whether other people retain access, choice, distinctive voice and a credible say in the system's design. [[Superagency]] needs more than throughput; [[Human Agency]] needs the ability to redirect and contest the tool.

## Tensions / counterarguments
- Thompson's reported pace may depend on existing skill, compute, money and testing discipline; it should not be projected onto novice workers or district staff.
- A feed that tracks browsing behavior may infer sensitive interests even if its interface advertises user control. Explicit deletion and privacy review matter.
- The three papers are preprints. One benchmark reanalysis cannot settle whether a specific skill or tool configuration is safe; two creative tasks cannot establish a general effect across all art or classrooms.
- More diverse outputs alone are not sufficient if creators lose ownership or compensation; see [[AI and Creator Rights]].

## Practical applications
For an approved, low-risk AI-assisted content workflow, define the human's intent and evaluation rubric **before** invoking an agent. Track completion time and reviewer effort; additionally record whether the human can change the goal, whether evidence is reproducible and whether independent drafts remain distinct. Do not connect real student records, employee information or unapproved browsing histories to a test agent.

## Practical experiment to try
**The agency-and-variety pilot:** Recruit volunteers to draft an internal, non-student-facing explainer on a common public topic. In one round, people draft without AI; in another, they use an approved AI tool with a fixed, documented prompt and can revise or reject its suggestions. Use a blinded reviewer to score accuracy and usability, and an independent reviewer to group overlapping ideas/themes across drafts. Record total time, human edits and whether participants could correct the AI's inferred goal. **Decision rule:** do not scale if accuracy declines or if distinct viewpoints collapse, even if drafts get faster. This tests the local workflow only, not the papers' population claims. Use public material; do not submit FERPA-protected or confidential district data.
