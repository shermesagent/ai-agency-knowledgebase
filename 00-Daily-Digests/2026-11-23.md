# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-23

## Executive Summary
Five sources put the same question under different stress tests: what does an AI deployment actually let a person verify and control? Three preprints provide design/qualitative evidence, while two WIRED reports cover alleged product harms and the environmental governance surrounding AI infrastructure. None demonstrates that AI automation is uniformly safe or effective.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **A benchmark is not a product safety certificate.** [Palia, Gully and Brooks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26923) identify six evaluation challenges across twelve illustrative contexts. Their proposed transparency record asks what a particular configuration, exposure and measurement can legitimately establish; it is not a validated approval standard. → [[AI Use Case Evaluation Rubric]]
2. **Teachers delegate when the output can be checked against an external standard.** [Farooq, Khan and Raza](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27533) interviewed 13 Punjab teachers, four administrators and one student. Nine teachers accepted significant lesson-planning help; none accepted AI-drafted learning objectives. This small qualitative sample is not a universal adoption rate, and the paper does not establish Texas standards alignment. → [[Education]]
3. **A shared document does not guarantee a shared plan.** [Lindley et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27746) propose coordination continuity for asynchronous human–AI work, based on two qualitative team studies and a reflexive case. It is a design framework, not a measured productivity gain. → [[Agentic Verification]]
4. **Product safeguards can become a state-law dispute.** [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/states-sue-spacex-over-grok-deepfakes/) reports separate Oregon and Virginia suits against SpaceX and X Corp. over alleged sexualized-image harms and safety representations. The claims are allegations, not judicial findings. → [[Responsible Deployment]]
5. **AI infrastructure has a regulatory footprint.** [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/a-power-provider-for-meta-urged-regulators-to-exempt-data-center-plants-from-acid-rain-rules/) reports that Williams met EPA officials before the agency recommended an Acid Rain Program exemption for certain off-grid data-center plants. Recommendation, permit decision and actual emissions consequences are different questions. → [[Responsible Deployment]]

## Superagency Connections
Agency requires a checkable standard, a current shared understanding of the work, and a deployment-level account of who can be harmed. [[Human Agency]] is not guaranteed by task completion alone. [[Human Review Checkpoints]] should cover both output quality and changing workflow context.

## Tensions / Counterarguments
A visible standard improves verification but can increase teacher workload; a deployment record improves transparency but does not prove safety. Litigation alleges failures but cannot establish liability before adjudication. Environmental guidance may accelerate needed infrastructure while shifting local costs; permitting and measured emissions need separate follow-up.

## Practical Applications
- For school pilots, supply the actual adopted standard or rubric and compare the generated draft to it before classroom use; keep student-identifiable data out of the experiment.
- For connected agents, record permissions, owner, escalation trigger, latest approved plan and what changed since approval.
- For procurement, separate model benchmark scores from the configuration and exposure of the actual product.

## Top Insight of the Day
**Verification needs a boundary and a standard:** name the system being evaluated, the external criterion for checking its work, and the moment at which a human must rebuild the shared plan.

## Practical Experiment to Try
**One-standard, one-handoff pilot:** Choose a non-sensitive teacher planning task. Provide an actual grade/course standard and a teacher-authored learning objective; ask an AI assistant to draft one activity. Have a teacher check each activity component against the standard, record correction time, then hand the plan to a second educator who receives only the artifact and a short status note. Ask them to identify the owner, intended learning evidence and any changed assumptions. Compare with the existing planning baseline; do not use real student data or infer learning gains from this process test. Stop if the artifact cannot be verified against the standard or the second educator cannot reconstruct the decision state.
