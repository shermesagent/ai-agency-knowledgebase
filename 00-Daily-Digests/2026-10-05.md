# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-10-05

## The Usable-Choice Test

## 1. Executive Summary
Two newly published essays and two preprints appearing in Monday's arXiv feeds point to a shared gap: people can **use** AI without endorsing its rollout, produce excellent work without owning its reasoning, and formally review a system without the capacity to intervene. Five findings below come from **four sources**, not five independent studies. Web search returned empty on two different probes; publisher feeds, full publisher pages and arXiv abstracts supplied the evidence. The preprints were submitted October 1–2 and appeared in today's feeds; neither is a K–12 intervention trial.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- **Choice without compulsion:** [[Case for AI Optimism]] must earn its claim through opt-in use, alternatives and demonstrable capability gains—not the inference that high usage equals trust.
- **Ownership beyond the product:** [[Cognitive Surrender]] gains a process lens: ask who set the goal, integrated evidence and evaluated the result. A good grade alone cannot answer.
- **Human control as a capability:** [[Human Review Checkpoints]] should account for the reviewer's attention, time, connectivity and actual stop rights, not just the existence of an approval button.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- Mandating manual approval for every low-risk step makes useful assistance unusable; use risk-proportional thresholds. But high-stakes actions should pause when the reviewer is unavailable.
- Thompson's account illustrates agent-aided detection **and** a preventable exposure of a remote-access surface. The right lesson is layered security, prompt visibility and isolated access—not blanket full-disk permission or public screen sharing.
- Collecting all student–AI transcripts to assess ownership could create a privacy and surveillance problem. A short, teacher-designed process reflection and unaided demonstration are safer starting points. Do not use interaction-trace analysis for disciplinary inference without a validated method and due process.

## 5. Practical Applications
1. For a proposed AI feature, document opt-in/default status, alternate workflow, data exit route and the user's independently demonstrable gain.
2. For an AI-permitted assignment, compare one finished artifact with a short explanation of direction/integration/evaluation and an unaided transfer task; apply the same rubric to both modes.
3. For an agent approval step, document the stop mechanism, reviewer's available time, workload, fallback and escalation; do not treat silence as consent.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**Agency is not established by usage, output quality or an approval prompt.** It requires a person who can choose the tool, explain what they contributed and realistically stop the next action.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
On a **fictional, non-student** district workflow such as drafting a public meeting agenda from public documents, compare two designs: AI auto-draft with a generic approve button versus opt-in draft with a visible alternative, an explicit change log and a two-minute review window. Ask two colleagues to name (a) what the AI did, (b) what they changed, (c) how to stop publication and (d) what happens if nobody reviews. Record which steps each design actually makes possible, not whether participants say they "trust AI." Do not upload student, employee or protected records to a public tool.
