**The Evidence-to-Action Test** · Tuesday curator pass · Four distinct sources: two arXiv preprints, one research newsletter, one reported interview. The five findings are not five independent studies.

## Executive Summary
An AI decision can look well-evidenced yet still fail the human it is supposed to help. Assessment policies can ask for a declaration without observing learning; a model evaluation can generate a score without changing deployment; an agent can choose the right action after the window closes; a city can communicate widely without giving residents a way to alter a decision. **Agency is the chain from usable evidence to a timely, authorized consequence.**

## Top 5 Findings
1. **Redesign assessment, but retain independent assurance.** Ansari finds support in the coded policy sample for defined AI-use categories and testing unaided ability as one lane; 31 of 55 documents define named use categories. Neither self-disclosure nor a polished artifact alone establishes learning. → [[Education]], [[AI Tutors]].
2. **An evaluation needs an action owner.** In Stelling et al.'s 37 interviews, 16 raised the absence of a guarantee that companies or governments would act on eval results. A measurement without a threshold and decision right can become permission to proceed. → [[Balanced Governance]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]].
3. **The clock changes the verdict.** Clark reports Realtime-Bench's top wall-clock success at 36% while some high-reasoning models approach 70–80% with time paused. These benchmark conditions are not an operational procurement test; they show why latency belongs in one. → [[AI Use Case Evaluation Rubric]], [[Task-Level AI Adoption]].
4. **Communicating a policy is not the same as sharing power over it.** In WIRED, Mamdani describes reaching Spanish-speaking constituents by WhatsApp while criticizing opaque data-center energy economics. Neither the interview nor a channel establishes that a resident can inspect assumptions or alter an approval. → [[Government and Civic Life]], [[Compute and Agency]].
5. **A school AI ban proposal is not learning evidence.** The same WIRED interview states the mayor's proposed 2K–8th-grade student-facing generative-AI moratorium. It raises a real developmental question—where must students practice unaided?—but cannot settle which age-specific policy improves learning. This is a second finding from source four, not an additional study. → [[Education]], [[AI for School Districts]].

## Superagency Connections
**Capacity:** AI can support learning and automate time-sensitive work. **Freedom:** students need an unaided skill lane, residents a way to question cost assumptions, and operators a manual takeover. **Platforms:** policy categories, benchmark clocks and infrastructure disclosures shape what counts as success. **Value:** track independent learning, timed task completion and changed civic decisions—not merely declarations, scores or audience reach. **Wisdom:** precommit who acts when evidence crosses a threshold. Read alongside [[Superagency]], [[Human Agency]] and [[Responsible Deployment]].

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- Restricting generative AI for younger students may protect practice but can also deny supervised literacy and accessibility supports. The interview documents a proposal, not an enacted rule or a comparison with scaffolded access.
- Evaluation-based governance is not worthless: the interviewees identified concrete pathways for risk reduction. The missing question is how evidence compels a response.
- Realtime-Bench's specific success rates may shift by model and task; not every useful agent acts under a closing window. Do not generalize its ranking to school or municipal systems.
- Transparent project data may not produce meaningful public choice if the utility or permitting authority has already committed; a response record must include what can actually change.

## Practical Applications
In a public-data-only sandbox, use a **three-column evidence-to-action card** for one candidate district AI use case: *what is observed* (unaided task result, timed completion, data source), *who can decide* (teacher, operator or board) and *what changes* (continue, revise, pause or stop). Record the exact threshold before testing. If the proposed use handles student work, staff information or live procurement, stop at the design exercise until district privacy and approval review is complete.

## Top Insight of the Day
**A measurement expands agency only when a person can use it in time to change a consequential decision.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
Choose one teacher-authored, publicly shareable learning task and define an AI-assisted version plus a short independent check. Before any pilot, specify the rubric, allowed AI use, the unaided skill the check measures, review time and a stop/revise rule. Have two adult reviewers test the instructions on public example work, not students or student records. Log whether they agree about when the rule fires; unresolved disagreement is a reason to revise the instrument, not to deploy it.
