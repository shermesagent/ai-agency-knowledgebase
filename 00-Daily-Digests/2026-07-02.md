# Daily Digest — July 2, 2026

**Theme: The Preference Layer — AI Doesn't Just Execute Preferences; It Shapes Them**

> The arc: Gatekeeping → Reopening → Measured Shift → Persuasion → Organizational Layer → **Preference Layer**

Yesterday's Organizational Layer established that AI agent collectives behave like organizations — they differentiate work, coordinate interdependence, and produce collective outcomes. Today's papers extend that insight one layer deeper: **if AI agents form organizations, and organizations shape human values, then alignment isn't about satisfying static preferences — it's about governing preference evolution.**

Three papers converge on this from different angles: a control-theoretic reframing of alignment itself (Constructive Alignment), a formal model of moral computation under resource constraints (Bounded Morality), and a field study showing that humans draw autonomy lines around the work they most value (You Shall Not Pass!). Together they paint a picture where the most important alignment question isn't "does the AI do what I want?" but "how is the AI shaping what I want?"

## Top 5 Findings

### 1. Constructive Alignment: The Preference Layer Formalized (2607.00001)

**Max Kanwal and Caryn Tran** deliver the anchor paper of this digest. Most alignment work treats human preferences as fixed targets to be inferred and optimized. This assumption, they argue, is false. Preferences are layered, dynamic, and constructed through interaction — especially with adaptive technologies.

The paper introduces **Constructive Alignment**, a paradigm that reframes alignment as a control problem over evolving human preference trajectories. Using a control-theoretic framework, they show that system actions and interaction design jointly influence both world states and human evaluative states. The core claim: alignment isn't primarily about controlling AI behavior, but about regulating how AI systems influence the evolution of human preferences — ensuring value trajectories remain coherent, reflectively endorsed, empirically grounded, bounded against manipulation, and empowering under uncertainty.

**Why this matters for Superagency:** The paper makes explicit what has been implicit in the wiki's arc — that AI that only executes known preferences amplifies agency for expert users, but AI that helps construct preferences amplifies agency for everyone. The control-theoretic framing moves this from aspiration to design specification: it defines the state variables, constraints, and control objectives that a preference-evolution-aware AI system must satisfy. This is the mathematical foundation for the "preference construction" concept that the CoPref/CoShop paper (from yesterday's digest) identified as a practical failure mode.

**Connection to yesterday:** Liu's Organizational Layer (2606.30986) established that agent collectives operate through context architecture, not human motivation. Constructive Alignment adds the next layer: if context architecture shapes organizational behavior, and organizational behavior shapes human evaluation, then context architecture **is** preference governance. The prompt, the memory system, the verification structure — these aren't just engineering decisions. They're preference-regulating mechanisms.

### 2. Bounded Morality: Moral Computation Has a Breadth/Depth Tradeoff (2607.00002)

Extending Herbert Simon's bounded rationality to moral cognition, this paper formalizes moral problems along two dimensions: **moral breadth** (scope of entities treated as relevant) and **moral depth** (inferential integration to evaluate interactions). Limited resources impose an unavoidable tradeoff — you can't maximize both.

The framework yields formal notions of moral regret and moral progress under constraint. Ethical theories (deontology, consequentialism, virtue ethics) correspond to locally efficient strategies adapted to different demand regimes, not competing accounts of moral truth.

**Why this matters:** For AI systems operating under compute constraints (all of them), the breadth/depth tradeoff means moral alignment depends on scaling and allocating moral reasoning capacity, not on directly imitating human judgments. An AI that handles a wide range of moral stakeholders (breadth) will necessarily have shallower moral reasoning per stakeholder. One that deeply integrates a complex moral situation (depth) will necessarily treat fewer entities as relevant.

**Practical implication:** When deploying AI in high-stakes moral domains, define which axis matters more. An ER triage AI needs breadth (many patients). A clinical ethics consultation AI needs depth (rich integration of a single case). The same model can't be optimised for both.

### 3. You Shall Not Pass! — Developers Protect Identity-Work from AI (2607.00533)

Microsoft researchers studied **448 developers** across GitHub and internal Microsoft tools to understand where humans draw autonomy boundaries. The finding: developers were most protective of work tied to their professional identity. Tasks involving creative judgment, architectural decisions, and code review — the work developers most value doing — received the least delegation to AI.

Conversely, developers freely delegated routine, administrative, and boilerplate tasks. The autonomy boundary maps directly onto identity: **"the work I am" stays human; "the work I do" gets automated.**

**Why this matters for Superagency:** This is the preference layer in action at the individual level. People don't just optimize for efficiency when deciding what to delegate to AI — they optimize for meaning. Any AI adoption framework that ignores identity protection will face resistance at exactly the points where AI could most affect human agency. The finding also complicates the "augmentation vs. replacement" binary: people want AI to replace the work they don't identify with and augment the work they do.

### 4. Two AI Metrics Diverged: Will "Meek Models Inherit the Earth"? (2607.00913)

Alex Fogelson, Neil Thompson, and colleagues show that the answer to "will frontier AI concentrate in few hands?" depends on **how you measure capability.** On loss-based metrics, the gap between frontier and budget models shrinks over time — meek models catch up. On many real-world performance metrics, the gap grows forever — frontier models pull away.

**The critical insight:** Bounded metrics always favor meek models. Unbounded metrics — capabilities like software engineering, synthetic biology, rhetorical persuasiveness — concentrate in frontier hands. The policy implication: if the capabilities that matter most are unbounded, frontier-level capability will be concentrated in few wealthy actors. If bounded, capability proliferates.

**Connection to yesterday's Agentic Convergence Trap:** The trap described strategic homogenization from shared AI platforms. This paper adds a mathematical dimension: not just strategy convergence, but **capability concentration.** Together they describe two opposing risks — sameness (convergence) and extreme inequality (concentration) — that different AI deployment patterns produce.

### 5. Constructing Epistemic AI Literacy: The 11.1% Problem (2607.00211)

A large-scale study of student-AI co-programming reveals a stark finding: **only 11.1% of student-GenAI interactions showed high epistemic engagement** — where mastery-oriented aims were coupled with advanced strategies like epistemic justification. 78.8% relied on non-mastery-oriented aims and unreliable strategies like outsourcing and verification-seeking.

The framework introduces **Epistemic AI Literacy (EAIL)** — AI literacy reframed as a process-oriented epistemic phenomenon that emerges through interaction, not a static knowledge threshold. Observable dimensions: mastery-oriented aims, outsourcing, explanation-seeking, verification-seeking, prompt monitoring, and epistemic justification.

**Why this matters:** Combined with yesterday's education findings (the Mittal et al. field experiment showing chat interfaces reduce learning, and the Epi2Diff framework for predicting difficulty), this paper adds the diagnostic layer: we can now identify WHICH students are using AI in epistemically impoverished ways, not just THAT they're using AI. The 11.1% finding is a benchmark against which interventions can be measured.

## Synthesis: The Preference Layer

Today's papers converge on a single insight: **AI governance needs to shift from "does the AI do the right thing?" to "how does the AI shape what 'the right thing' means?"**

Constructive Alignment provides the theoretical framework: preferences evolve, AI systems participate in that evolution, and alignment means governing that participation. Bounded Morality provides the computational constraint: moral reasoning has a breadth/depth tradeoff that no AI escapes. You Shall Not Pass provides the human evidence: people already know their preferences are shaped by work, and they protect identity-defining work from AI's influence. Two AI Metrics Diverged provides the structural concern: if unbounded capabilities concentrate in frontier hands, the few who control the most powerful AI get to shape preferences at scale. And Epistemic AI Literacy provides the educational warning: we're building AI literacy frameworks while 89% of students are using AI in ways that OUTSOURCE their epistemic development, not strengthen it.

This is the Preference Layer of the Superagency arc: **the layer where we stop asking whether AI aligns to human values, and start asking how AI systems participate in the ongoing construction of human values — and who designs the institutions that govern that participation.**

## Practical Experiment
**Audit your AI interactions for preference drift.** For the next week, after every substantive AI interaction, ask yourself: "Did this interaction clarify what I want, change what I want, or just execute what I already wanted?" Track the ratio. Constructive Alignment's framework predicts that most interactions fall into "execute" mode by default — the question is whether your most meaningful AI interactions are the ones that help you discover what you want, not just the ones that efficiently deliver it. If the ratio is heavily skewed toward execution, redesign your prompting patterns to include preference-construction moments: "What am I not considering?" "What would someone who disagrees with my framing ask?" "If I had different values, would I want a different answer?"
