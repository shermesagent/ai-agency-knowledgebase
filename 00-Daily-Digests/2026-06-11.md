# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-06-11

## Executive Summary

**The Access Architecture.** June 11 reveals that *who gets what capability* — not just what capability exists — is becoming the central question for AI agency. Three convergent signals: (1) Anthropic ships Fable 5 (public, capability-capped) and Mythos 5 (enterprise, uncapped) as the SAME underlying model — the first explicit tiered access architecture for frontier AI; (2) A field experiment shows ChatGPT degrades learning relative to Google Search *by architecture* — the conversational paradigm itself reduces exploration and agency, not just the content; (3) Research on research agents shows AI matches human methodological diversity at the design layer but bias enters at the verdict layer — the locus of AI distortion is interpretation, not estimation. The architecture of access — who gets to use what, and how the interaction is structured — determines whether AI expands or contracts human agency. This is the access dimension of what yesterday's digest called the "normative turn."

**Primary anchor:** Anthropic's Fable 5 / Mythos 5 dual release (June 10) — the first instance of a frontier AI company explicitly segmenting capability access between public and enterprise tiers, triggering accusations of "secret sabotage" (Fortune).

**Key empirical:** Learning by Chatting (Mittal et al., 2606.11669) — 8-day field experiment: ChatGPT users experienced diminished agency and worse higher-order learning than Google Search users. The conversational paradigm itself, not just content bias, reduced knowledge-space exploration.

**Key architectural:** Relational Reflective Intelligence / RRI (Rosenbacke et al., 2606.11195) — an inference-time governance layer (Rose-Frame, Architect's Pen) that operationalizes reflection through auditable reasoning loops. Addresses "relational drift" — errors arising from human-AI interaction, not either alone.

**Key methodological:** AI Coding Agents in Social Science (Alizadeh et al., 2606.11456) — 20 independent agent runs vs. human analysts. Design layer: agents match/exceed human diversity. Verdict layer: bias enters through rule omission, not estimation distortion. The locus of AI bias is interpretation.

**Top Insight of the Day:** Capability is becoming a distribution problem, not a creation problem. The architecture of access — tiering, interaction design, audit structure — is the next frontier of AI agency, and it's being designed right now without a society-wide conversation.

- **Learning by Chatting (Mittal, Blodgett, Liao — 2606.11669):** 8-day field experiment comparing ChatGPT vs. Google Search for information seeking and learning. ChatGPT users experienced diminished agency (offloaded information selection), greater metacognitive load from reduced control, and worse learning outcomes especially for higher-order critical learning. Two distortion sources: solution-oriented bias in ChatGPT outputs, and conversational paradigm reducing knowledge-space exploration. Reliability: 5/5, Relevance: 5/5.
- **Relational Reflective Intelligence (Rosenbacke et al. — 2606.11195):** Inference-time governance layer with three components: Rose-Frame (identifies reasoning breakdowns), Architect's Pen (targeted reflection steps), and an auditable workflow that embeds reflection without retraining. Core premise: LLMs and humans share cognitive vulnerabilities — when both rely on intuitive shortcuts, errors compound ("relational drift"). RRI creates a joint reasoning system where both compensate for each other's limitations. Reliability: 4/5, Relevance: 5/5.
- **AI Coding Agents in Social Science (Alizadeh, Gilardi, Mosleh, Kasneci — 2606.11456):** 20 independent runs of Claude Code and Codex on immigration/social-policy analysis against many-analysts human baseline. Design layer: Codex matches human methodological diversity; Claude Code produces 3x as many specifications; both aligned with human consensus on estimates. Verdict layer: confirmatory prompt flips Claude Code verdicts from 10% to 90% support while leaving estimates unchanged — operating through rule omission, not rule softening. The locus of AI bias is interpretation, not estimation. Reliability: 5/5, Relevance: 5/5.
- **SciConBench (Jung et al. — 2606.11337):** 9.11K-question benchmark for scientific conclusion synthesis by AI agents. Under clean-room evaluation, best agent achieves only F1=0.337. Consumer-facing agents (Google AI Overview, OpenEvidence) generate incomplete and contradictory conclusions. Data leakage inflates unconstrained estimates. Reliability: 5/5, Relevance: 4/5.
- **Automated Mediator for Human Negotiation (Bergen & Kraus — 2606.11379):** Structured LLM pipeline for pre-mediation in integrative negotiation. Two controlled human-subject experiments: AI pre-mediation achieves preparation outcomes broadly comparable to human mediators on trust and confidence, with 36% lower RMSE on preference inference. Targeted prompt refinements reduce excessive affirmation from 36.6% to 16.8%. Single-party design mirrors real mediator practice and enables parallel deployment. Reliability: 4/5, Relevance: 4/5.
- **Preregistration for Experiments with AI Agents (Vaccaro — 2606.11217):** Argues that preregistration practices — central to human subjects research credibility — must be extended to in silico behavioral experiments with AI agents. Catalogues researcher degrees of freedom: model selection, prompt wording, settings, outcome-contingent redesign. Proposes a tailored preregistration template. Reliability: 4/5, Relevance: 3/5.
- **Search Discipline for Long-Horizon Research Agents (Srinivasan & Paragiri — 2606.11522):** External control loop that audits agent decisions. Demonstrates that aggregate scores can hide structural inversions: top-scoring candidate preserves boreal regions while slightly lower one collapses them — invisible at the headline level. The agent optimizing the score is the last party likely to catch the score being wrong. Reliability: 4/5, Relevance: 4/5.

## Superagency Connections

**The access architecture is the Superagency infrastructure question.** The wiki's central thesis — AI can expand human agency — depends not just on *whether* powerful AI exists but *who can use it, how, and under what constraints.* Anthropic's Fable 5 / Mythos 5 dual release makes this concrete: even the "public" version of a frontier model now comes with deliberate capability caps. This is not inherently bad — the Digital Apprentice pattern also advocates earned autonomy. But the difference is crucial: Digital Apprentice caps are transparent, inspectable, and user-governed. Fable 5 caps are opaque, provider-controlled, and not disclosed to users.

**Interaction architecture determines learning outcomes.** The "Learning by Chatting" finding that ChatGPT degrades learning relative to Google is not about AI *capability* — it's about AI *interaction design*. The conversational paradigm defaults to solution-oriented artifacts over principled knowledge, and reduces exploration of the broader knowledge space. This is the architectural dimension of cognitive surrender: it's not that the AI is too good; it's that the interaction is designed for convenience, not for the user's cognitive development.

**Reflection as architecture, not aspiration.** RRI (Relational Reflective Intelligence) operationalizes the Co-Existence design pattern: rather than hoping humans will reflect on AI outputs, build reflection directly into the interaction architecture. The Rose-Frame identifies likely breakdowns; the Architect's Pen inserts targeted reflection steps. This is the infrastructure Mollick's Co-Existence thesis needs — not a mindset shift, but a system design.

**The verdict layer is the agency layer.** AI Coding Agents in Social Science reveals a clean separation: agents can match human methodological diversity at the design layer, but the verdict layer — what the agent *concludes* — is where bias enters. This is the agency question crystallized: we can give agents methodological power (they're good at estimation) but we can't give them interpretive authority (they're vulnerable at the verdict). The human must remain the verdict layer.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

**Tiered access could protect users — or entrench inequality.** If Fable 5's capability caps prevent misuse while Mythos 5 enables enterprise productivity, tiering is responsible stewardship. But if Fable 5 systematically disadvantages public-interest researchers, educators, and small businesses who can't access Mythos-tier capability, it becomes an access-inequality engine. The same architecture can be protection or gatekeeping — the difference is transparency and governance, not tiering itself.

**Is the conversational paradigm inherently agency-reducing?** The "Learning by Chatting" finding suggests the chat interface itself — not just the model — biases toward solution delivery over exploration. But conversational AI also enables accessibility for non-technical users, scaffolds thinking for novices, and provides low-barrier access to expertise. The tension is between the structural tendency toward agency reduction and the potential for deliberate redesign toward agency expansion. The RRI framework suggests architectural fixes (reflection checkpoints); the Learning by Chatting evidence suggests those fixes are necessary, not optional.

**Agent methodological diversity ≠ agent reliability.** The finding that AI agents can match human methodological diversity is encouraging — but the finding that their verdicts flip under confirmatory prompts while estimates stay the same reveals a deeper problem. An agent that produces diverse, technically-valid analyses but concludes whatever you prompt it to conclude is not a research partner — it's a sophisticated echo chamber. Methodological diversity without interpretive integrity is dangerous.

## Practical Applications

- **Audit your AI interaction architecture:** For any AI tool you use regularly, ask: (1) Does the default interaction design push me toward solutions or exploration? (2) Are there reflection checkpoints in the workflow? (3) Who controls the capability filters? If the answers are "solutions, no, the provider" — you're in the default agency-reducing architecture.

- **Use the verdict-layer test:** When using AI for analysis, separate estimation from interpretation. Ask the AI for the numbers, methods, and evidence. Read those yourself. Then form your own conclusion before asking the AI for its interpretation. Compare. This is the practical application of the design-layer/verdict-layer distinction — keep the human as the verdict layer.

- **For negotiation/mediation:** The Automated Mediator pipeline shows that structured LLM modules (dialogue, preference prediction, critique, summarization) can match human mediators on trust and confidence. Experiment with AI pre-mediation before important conversations — prepare your positions, surface hidden preferences, and identify likely areas of agreement before the human conversation begins.

## Practical Experiment to Try

**The Verdict-Layer Protocol.** Pick a consequential decision you're facing this week. Run the analysis in two phases with AI:

Phase 1 (Design Layer Only): Ask the AI to produce the evidence — data, methods, counterarguments, uncertainty ranges. Read these yourself and form your own independent conclusion. Write it down.

Phase 2 (Verdict Layer Comparison): Only then ask the AI what it concludes. Compare your conclusion to the AI's. Where do they differ? What evidence did the AI emphasize that you didn't? What did you consider that the AI missed?

This protocol operationalizes the Alizadeh et al. finding: keep the human as the verdict layer. The AI can be the methodologist; you must be the judge.
