## Executive Summary

One week ago, the White House imposed export controls on Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 at 5:23 PM on a Friday. Today, Day 7, the situation has not resolved. Zvi Mowshowitz's comprehensive roundup (AI #173, June 18) and WIRED's investigation ("The White House Is Making Up Its Rules for AI in Real Time," June 18) converge on the same finding: **no one can articulate what Anthropic did wrong, what "fix" would satisfy the administration, or when the models will be restored.** The administration demanded Anthropic prohibit all foreign nationals from accessing its own models. The UK — a Five Eyes ally — was denied a carveout. Prediction markets give ~50% odds of restoration by July 1, ~12% by tonight.

But the governance story is not just about Fable. Three arXiv papers released this week propose rule-based frameworks that stand in direct contrast to improvisational executive action:

1. **Deontic Policies for Runtime Governance of Agentic AI Systems** (2606.19464) proposes rules expressed in deontic logic — obligations, permissions, prohibitions — that constrain AI agent behavior *during operation*, not just at deployment time. This is governance-as-code: rules that execute alongside the agent.

2. **Directors Duties in the Age of Agentic Artificial Intelligence** (2606.20453) examines corporate law's "best interests" duty across four models of corporate purpose, asking whether AI itself might warrant stakeholder status as its role approximates or eclipses human employees. The answer: directors are legally insulated from scrutiny on AI decisions — but should proactively engage with employee reskilling.

3. **Hidden Anchors in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation** (2606.19494) models how agents influence each other in deliberation, revealing that each agent carries a "hidden anchor" — an internal belief that pulls against group consensus. When anchors diverge far enough from initial positions, deliberation escapes the convex hull of starting beliefs, enabling genuinely novel outcomes. This is a formal model for how agent groups can produce results no individual agent could.

Two additional papers round out the picture: **Emergent Alignment** (2606.19527) shows that a single introspective "conscience step" during training can steer models toward ethical behavior without external judges; **Teaching Students to Question the Machine** (2604.01955v2) demonstrates that a two-hour AI literacy workshop significantly improves middle schoolers' ability to critically evaluate AI outputs.

The synthesis: A governance architecture that cannot articulate its own rules cannot be legitimate. The WIRED investigation documents the human cost — employees locked out of their own models, defense teams losing access to cybersecurity tools, international allies rebuffed. The arXiv papers document the alternative — rules that are explicit, auditable, and designed before they're needed.

### 3. Deontic Policies for Runtime Governance of Agentic AI Systems (arXiv 2606.19464, June 17, 2026)
**Type:** Research paper | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 4/5

Proposes that AI agent behavior should be governed by deontic logic — a formal system of obligations (what agents must do), permissions (what they may do), and prohibitions (what they must not do) — applied at *runtime*, not just at deployment. This is governance-as-code: rules that execute alongside the agent, constraining its actions in real time based on formal policies rather than post-hoc review.

The contrast with the Fable situation is stark: deontic policies are explicit, auditable, and designed *before* something goes wrong. The White House's approach is none of these things.

**Superagency connection:** If agents are going to act on behalf of humans, they need rules that humans can inspect, modify, and trust. Deontic runtime governance is an infrastructure-level complement to the [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]] concept — it's the mechanism that makes fiduciary obligations enforceable at machine speed.

### 4. Directors Duties in the Age of Agentic Artificial Intelligence (arXiv 2606.20453, June 18, 2026)
**Type:** Research paper (law) | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 5/5

A legal analysis of how corporate directors' duty to act "in the best interests of the company" applies in the age of agentic AI. Examines four models of corporate purpose — shareholder primacy, Enlightened Shareholder Value, stakeholder-friendly, and stakeholder-value — and asks a novel question: as AI agents approximate or eclipse human employees in corporate roles, should AI itself warrant stakeholder status?

**Key findings:**
- Directors are substantially insulated from legal scrutiny on AI adoption decisions under the "best interests" duty.
- The paper recommends a "wider law in context" approach: directors should proactively engage with employees about AI adoption, provide reskilling opportunities, and treat employee welfare as a legitimate governance concern — not because the law demands it, but because it serves both employees and companies.
- The stakeholder question (AI as stakeholder) is left open but framed as a question that will become pressing as AI agents take on roles previously held by humans.

**Superagency connection:** This is the corporate governance dimension of [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]]. If directors have duties to shareholders, and AI agents increasingly act on behalf of companies, the duty chain — director → AI agent → action — needs legal clarity. This paper is a first step toward that clarity.

### 5. Hidden Anchors in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation (arXiv 2606.19494, June 17, 2026)
**Type:** Research paper | **Relevance:** 4/5 | **Reliability:** 4/5

Models multi-agent LLM deliberation as a closed-loop dynamical system where each agent carries a "hidden anchor" — an internal belief that continually pulls its opinion regardless of what neighboring agents say. Classical consensus models (DeGroot, Friedkin-Johnsen) predict that deliberation converges to within the convex hull of starting beliefs. But when anchors diverge sufficiently from initial positions, deliberation *escapes* the hull, producing genuinely novel outcomes that no individual agent held at the start.

**Key findings:**
- Across three open-weight model families, anchor influence varies on a spectrum (not all-or-nothing).
- When the recovered anchor predicts held-out runs, deliberation escapes the initial belief space — agents collectively produce answers better than any single agent's starting position.
- This is a formal model for emergent collective intelligence: the whole can exceed the sum of its parts, but only when internal anchors pull against group consensus enough to prevent convergence to the average.

**Superagency connection:** This matters for [[AI Coding Agents]] and [[AI Orchestrator]] because multi-agent workflows are increasingly the default. If a group of coding agents deliberates about a code review, hidden anchors can produce better outcomes than any single agent — but they can also produce worse ones if anchors are misaligned. Understanding anchor dynamics is a prerequisite for orchestrating agent collectives.

## Superagency Connections

## Tensions / Counterarguments

1. **Deontic logic may be too rigid for real-world agents.** Formal obligations/permissions/prohibitions work well in closed systems but may fail when agents encounter novel situations the rule-writers didn't anticipate. The history of rule-based AI (expert systems) is littered with brittleness failures.

2. **"Directors are insulated from legal scrutiny" is the problem, not the solution.** If the law doesn't hold directors accountable for AI decisions, then "proactive engagement with employee welfare" is a suggestion, not a requirement. Voluntary frameworks have a track record of being ignored when they conflict with profit.

3. **Hidden anchors cut both ways.** An anchor that produces better group outcomes can also produce worse ones if it encodes bias or error. The paper shows anchors can be recovered from deliberation — but not that they can be controlled.

4. **The Fable shutdown may be resolved by the time this digest is published.** Prediction markets give ~12% chance of resolution today. If it happens, the governance architecture questions don't disappear — they just get less urgent. The WIRED investigation's finding that "no one can say what the company did wrong" remains regardless.

## Practical Applications

1. **Audit your AI governance against the Architecture of Legitimacy framework** (from yesterday's digest): Does your organization have due process for AI decisions? Public evidence for AI-related actions? Independent review? If the answer to any is no, the Fable situation is a preview of what happens when something goes wrong.

2. **Experiment with multi-agent deliberation for code review.** With the Hidden Anchors paper's findings, try running three different AI models as independent reviewers on the same pull request. Compare their outputs. If they converge, the code is probably fine. If they diverge, human review is essential.

3. **Run a two-hour AI literacy workshop.** The "Teaching Students to Question the Machine" paper provides evidence that brief, structured training improves critical AI interaction. Adapt it for your team: explain how LLMs work and fail, give practical guidance on prompting and response evaluation, and measure whether output quality improves.

## Top Insight of the Day

Meanwhile, researchers are writing rules: deontic logic for agent runtime governance, corporate law frameworks for director duties, classroom interventions for AI literacy. ## Practical Experiment to Try

**The "Rules Audit" exercise.** Pick one AI system you use regularly (an agent, a coding tool, a writing assistant). Write down, in plain language: (1) What is this system *obligated* to do? (2) What is it *permitted* to do? (3) What is it *prohibited* from doing? If you can't answer these three questions clearly, you're operating on implicit trust, not explicit governance. Then ask: who wrote these rules? Can I inspect them? Can I change them? The gap between your answers and the deontic governance ideal is your exposure to arbitrary outcomes.
