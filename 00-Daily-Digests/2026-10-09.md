## 1. Executive Summary
Four directly inspected sources yield five findings about the difference between a model's apparent safeguard and a human institution's actual control: a tutor metric can improve while educators prefer the original teaching; refusal both blocks harms and gives its rulemaker power; a remote pilot can supervise autonomous flights but needs an effective intervention path; and a risk ceiling cannot simply be inferred from a development deadline. Two reported articles and two research preprints. Web search returned empty on two different queries; publisher full text and arXiv RSS abstracts supplied the evidence. No student or personnel data used.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- [[Education]]: agency cannot be reduced to an optimization score; the learner's trajectory and an educator's judgment remain the evaluative unit.
- [[Generative Refusal]]: developmental withholding, missing-evidence abstention and a mandatory safety block are different decisions, with different owners and appeal paths.
- [[Pacing the Frontier]]: retain the option to advance while making the safety criterion independently checkable and the date conditional.
- [[Future of Work]]: supervision can be meaningful employment only when training, workload and override powers are real.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- Refusing dangerous instructions is necessary; critique of overbroad or fragile refusal is not an argument for removing all safeguards.
- The tutoring study reports a strong reversal in one designed setting, not that all metrics or fine-tuning are harmful. Whole-session tests and unaided learning measures are better complements than abandoning measurement.
- Delaying release may carry benefits forgone. The pacing model clarifies uncertainty; it does not choose a socially acceptable risk threshold.
- A remote supervisor may add value without offsetting displaced jobs, and anecdotal interventions cannot establish fleet reliability.

## 5. Practical Applications
1. In an **approved, non-identifiable** tutor sandbox, compare original and revised systems on whole-session educator review: adaptation to a changed learner response, repetition, misconception detection, and delayed unaided performance. Do not optimize only the same metric used to judge training success.
2. For a refused request, record the category (learning scaffold, insufficient evidence, or safety rule), policy owner, safe next step, review path, and whether override is appropriate. Never use an educator override to bypass a non-optional safety boundary.
3. For any autonomous workflow, verify that the named human can see the right signal, intervene before harm and explain the restart condition; track simultaneous load and interventions rather than counting human names on an org chart.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**A safeguard is not a control until the right person can diagnose the failure and change the next decision.** A high tutoring score, a refusal message, a safety deadline and a human supervisor each need an accountable decision owner.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
Using a newly written, non-student multi-turn tutoring scenario, insert a learner misconception halfway through. Compare two tutor configurations blind: does either repeat the same prompt after the learner changes their reasoning? Have an educator mark the next useful move and the reason; log disagreement before changing a prompt or buying a product. This is a design check, not evidence of improved student learning. No identifiable student work or unapproved data transfer.
