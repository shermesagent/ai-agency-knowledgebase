# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-25

**Theme:** The learner-choice and audit-authority test. Five newly reviewed arXiv preprints (including one cross-list) point to a consistent question: who retains control of timing, attention, evidence and data when AI helps?

## Executive summary
Promising AI support can still diminish agency when it interrupts thinking, conceals work, produces unequal interface experiences or stores learner histories without a clear purpose. These are *preprints and design evidence*, not proof of K–12 effectiveness. Today’s practical recommendation is a privacy-safe, staff-only evaluation of an AI tutor’s prompts and disclosure behavior before any student pilot.

## Top 5 findings

### Best sources found
All five linked studies below are original arXiv source records; their claims are bounded by the described methods rather than product claims or news summaries.

1. **Agency-preserving help can paradoxically constrain agency.** [Fan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31573) report an 18-student co-design workshop around AI support for programming and writing. Participants negotiated help vs. reflection, timing vs. self-regulation, visibility vs. attention and adaptation vs. control. A learner should be able to ask for help, decline a nudge and explain work without the AI. Workshop evidence does not establish improved learning. → [[Human Agency]], [[AI Tutors]].
2. **Average interface ratings can hide different user experiences.** [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31899) randomized university students between structured visual and text-only AI for a graph-algorithm task; 282 post-task questionnaires were analyzed. Visual structure improved unadjusted usability and perceived-effectiveness ratings, but not efficiency, satisfaction or post-use emotion; device/prior-experience subgroup findings are exploratory. These are perceptions, not a measured K–12 learning gain. → [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]].
3. **Quiet automation needs a visible return path.** [Calm Agent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32009) is a position/design paper with a desktop prototype: Delegate → Adjust → Check, so background work does not demand constant attention yet remains inspectable. It proposes a design pattern; it does not demonstrate long-term productivity or agency effects. → [[Human Agency]], [[Responsible Deployment]].
4. **Audit access is task-dependent, not a compliance checkbox.** [Audits Need Access, Data, and Compute](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31968) studies three audit tasks and argues that data and compute matter especially for some fairness tests, while model access matters more for manipulation and lie-detection tests. Do not infer one universal minimum; match auditor authority to the failure mode and govern access to sensitive data. → [[Balanced Governance]].
5. **Cross-session answer controls trade leakage risk for retention risk.** [Leaks by Installments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31429) tests an AI-tutor ledger tracking earlier disclosed answers. It reduced renumbered-variant leakage in simulations and small live tests, but the live split-request attack did not improve over a per-session gate. Persistent student histories require explicit minimization, access control and deletion before deployment. → [[AI Tutors]], [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]].

## Superagency connections
The agency test runs through learner choice, inspectable agent work, and audit access matched to the actual risk. [[Human Agency]] and [[Balanced Governance]] reinforce each other: control without observability is fragile; oversight without control is cosmetic.

## Tensions / counterarguments — what the sources do and do not support
The shared pattern is **bounded support, not frictionless delegation**: let people control interruptions and inspect outcomes, test actual learning separately from perceived helpfulness, and demand an audit that can probe the claimed risk. The educational findings are an 18-person co-design workshop, a university interface experiment and tutor-defense tests; none validates a district product or student-data policy. Preprint findings need replication and local review.

## Practical applications
Evaluate helpfulness together with interruption control and independent explanation. Require an audit plan tied to the specific failure mode before vendor procurement, and resolve retention/deletion rules before using persistent learner histories.

## Practical experiment to try — a staff-only tutor agency test
Using teacher-created, non-student prompts in an approved environment, compare a tutor with optional reflection nudges to the same tutor with nudges always on. In a short scripted walk-through, record whether each tester can (a) defer a nudge, (b) ask for a hint without receiving the final answer, (c) return after interruption and explain the work independently, and (d) locate and delete any stored session history. Compare completion and independent explanation, *not* satisfaction alone; log device/accessibility differences. **No student names, work, accounts or recordings** until privacy, accessibility and instructional review approve the pilot. Do not interpret this staff test as evidence of student learning.
