## The Explain-and-Diagnose Test

## 1. Executive Summary
Four newly checked sources yield five findings: automated research needs a human who can explain the work; AI grading can look granular without diagnosing misconceptions; and impressive robot demonstrations are not evidence of general-purpose deployment. Zvi Mowshowitz makes the strongest opposing case for slowing the frontier, but his conference impressions and timelines are not independent measurements. Two reported/analytical non-paper sources and two arXiv preprints; no student or personnel records were used. Publisher search returned empty, so sources were checked against publisher pages and today's arXiv RSS/abstracts.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]]: genuine educational agency depends on catching *why* a learner is wrong, not multiplying rubric labels.
- [[Automated AI R&D]] and [[Strongest AI Risk Arguments]]: a human sign-off has meaning only if someone can independently explain and challenge the contribution; this is a proposed assurance mechanism, not a proven one.
- [[Work]]: simulation, controlled benches and deployed physical tasks are different capability thresholds. Preserve the opportunity to augment workers without selling a demo as a finished replacement.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- Comprehension audits could create expensive ceremonial explanations; independent sampling, demonstrated reconstruction, and a real stop condition would matter more than an oral summary.
- Human grading also misses misconceptions in the study; teacher review is a comparator, not perfect ground truth. Pilot-specific validation should compare actionable diagnosis, not simply teacher-versus-model labels.
- More robot training data and better controls may change the frontier; today's limitations do not establish a permanent ceiling. Conversely, optimistic timelines are not procurement evidence.
- Zvi's warning about the frontier deserves engagement, while his conference-derived urgency and quantitative predictions require independent validation before policy use.

## 5. Practical Applications
1. For **approved, de-identified** exemplar responses, have a teacher label the misconception and useful next question; compare model feedback blind on misconception detection and instructional usefulness, not tone or score agreement. Use no live student work in an unapproved external tool.
2. Before an AI-generated workflow goes live, ask its named owner to explain its data dependencies, failure mode, rollback and a surprising output without consulting the agent. Pause when they cannot; this adapts the *proposed* audit idea without implying that it has been validated.
3. For physical-AI procurement, ask for an on-site trial on the actual task distribution, exception and recovery counts, maintenance time, worker override and total cost—not a video of a laboratory task.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**The appearance of inspection is not inspection.** An elaborate rubric, a human approval checkbox or a polished robot demo is evidence of presentation until someone can diagnose, explain or recover from a real error.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
Use one public-domain or newly written *non-student* sample answer containing a known conceptual error. Ask a model for multi-dimensional scores and feedback. Give the same answer to an educator without sharing the model response; compare whether each identifies the error and proposes a useful next question. Record disagreement and the educator's final decision, but do not infer vendor effectiveness or student learning from a single trial.
