# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-11

## Executive Summary
Five new arXiv preprints sharpen one practical rule: **separate the claimed result, the evidence actually checked, and the human decision made from it.** Today spans research verification, reasoning-trace transparency, civic infrastructure, exam grading, and workplace adoption. These are proposals, small studies, or single-organization findings—not proof of broadly deployable outcomes. Source discovery also checked the preferred commentary outlets; the five accepted, distinct findings below came from papers with accessible primary abstracts.

## Best Sources Found — Top 5 Findings
1. **A proof checker can certify the wrong version of a claim.** [Eigner, Süli and Hollós, *Trust, but verify, audit and attribute*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13042) (submitted Nov 9; reliability 3/5, relevance 5/5) propose independent tool checks, a claim-fidelity audit, human decision points and contribution attribution. In their two-claim mathematics example, two readings of an ambiguous claim yielded opposite results. A design proposal and small worked example, not field-validated reliability. See [[Agentic Verification]] and [[Human Review Checkpoints]].
2. **Reasoning-trace oversight needs measurable disclosures.** [Davies and Bearman, *Transparency Disclosures for Chain-of-Thought Monitorability*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13873) (submitted Nov 10; reliability 3/5, relevance 5/5) propose five indicators across access/architecture, trace naturalness, faithfulness, detection and uplift. Their public-disclosure audit says none of three major developers covers all five; missing disclosure does not establish that a monitor fails. See [[Agentic Verification]] and [[Reasoning Trace Theft]].
3. **A civic assistant can aid inspection yet bias the range of views.** [Pačes-Wiles et al., *EJAI*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13525) (submitted Nov 10; reliability 3/5, relevance 5/5) report evidence support for 98.4% of factual claims in a 21-query benchmark and missing-evidence acknowledgment in 32/33 cases. In a simulated town-hall task with 15 residents, a corporate persona sometimes omitted inconvenient evidence. These small studies cannot establish broad civic impact or representativeness. See [[Government and Civic Life]] and [[The Artificial State]].
4. **High grading agreement can mask consequential response errors.** [AlGhamdi, *Reliability of Claude Code as an Agentic AI Grading System for Handwritten University Exams*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13217) (submitted Nov 9; reliability 3/5, relevance 5/5) reports total-score r = 0.97 and mean absolute error = 0.85/20 marks on a 20-paper comparison against one course instructor, alongside a 0.68-mark AI-positive bias; drawings and corrected answers caused trouble. The 60-student university dataset is not Texas K–12 validation, and one expert's scores are not an inter-rater gold standard. See [[Education]] and [[Agentic Verification]].
5. **Use and policy literacy are different adoption signals.** [Verì, *Uneven Self-Reported AI Use*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14154) (submitted Nov 10; reliability 3/5, relevance 4/5) finds 36/65 respondents on comparable instruments reported frequent use, while 31/36 of that group reported no awareness or uncertainty about policy details. A further 33 respondents used different response anchors; do not pool them for like-for-like comparisons. One organization's self-report cannot prove impact, compliance, or population prevalence. See [[Work]] and [[Adoption Readiness Checklist]].

## Superagency Connections
The five cases draw the same boundary at different scales: a validated *artifact* is not necessarily the intended research claim; a visible *trace* is not necessarily faithful; a grounded *civic response* may still exclude an affected view; a correlated *grade total* may misjudge an individual item; and frequent *tool use* does not imply policy comprehension. [[Human Agency]] requires explicit recourse: someone can inspect the evidence, correct the interpretation, and decline an unsafe decision.

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- More checkpoints can slow beneficial work; calibrate review to consequence and make the original claim/evidence/decision trail easy to compare.
- Small, context-specific studies can inspire local tests but should not be promoted into universal procurement or school policy.
- Public civic records may underrepresent communities; persona prompts can compound, rather than repair, that imbalance.
- Handwritten student work and employee interviews are sensitive. No identifiable records should be entered into an unapproved model for a pilot; teacher evaluation and local district rules remain authoritative.

## Practical Applications
- Require a **claim-versus-check** line in AI-assisted research and grading review forms: what was asserted, what was actually checked, and who signs off.
- For civic summaries, preserve document citations, source omissions and an accessible route to challenge conclusions.
- In workplace adoption reviews, ask separate questions about use, policy understanding and demonstrated benefit; do not use self-reports as a disciplinary surveillance measure.

## Top Insight of the Day
**Never substitute evidence that a tool checked *something* for evidence that a person can defend the *right claim*.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
Use an approved, **teacher-authored fictional response** and its answer key—no real student work or personal information. Have two staff reviewers independently mark (a) the intended claim/rubric criterion, (b) the exact portion an AI-produced check addresses, and (c) any ambiguity or missing evidence. Compare disagreements against a prewritten rubric; require a human resolution and record how often a plausible check addressed the wrong criterion. This tests a local review workflow, not model grading validity. Do not deploy to student grading without district approval, privacy review and item-level validation.
