# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-09-01

## The Handover Question — what state must cross the boundary when work passes between human and AI?

**Theme:** Yesterday's Asking Question asked *when* an AI should involve a human. Today's sources ask the adjacent, more mechanical question: when control actually passes — human to AI, AI to human, or AI to AI — **what state must travel with the work?** Three papers give the mechanics, one gives a governance diagnostic, and the Hugging Face coverage supplies the failure case at full scale.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The strongest signal today is that "handover" is becoming an engineering discipline, not just a coordination nicety. A new provenance-aware pipeline (2608.28907) treats task handover as a **state-reconciliation problem**: system records are precise but partial; human reports capture intent but are lossy; the work is aligning the two into a shared, conflict-checked task state. A student interview study (2608.28837) shows what happens when handover happens *before learning*: students delegate their highest-stakes professional writing to AI, the learning loop that builds the skill is removed, and confidence migrates from the writer's own ability to the system's. An organizational-rules paper (2608.29055) identifies why procedures handed to AI fail: the AI receives the procedure, but the organization actually operates on the procedure **plus** negative boundaries, runtime judgments, responsibility assignments, and learning history — none of which are captured. The O-I-B-A-R scaffold is a proposal for externalizing exactly those missing decision boundaries, including explicit "suspension" states that define human-AI handoffs.

On the governance side, a university stress-testing method (2608.28925) finds that when institutions document AI governance, the pathways collapse as capability escalates: 16 of 25 augmentation cases had governed pathways, but only 4 delegation cases and **zero** autonomous-substitution cases did. The failure isn't missing roles — it's missing decision criteria and process. And MIT Technology Review's culture piece on the Hugging Face hack (Grace Huckins, 08-31) supplies the human-factors layer the technical reports left out: OpenAI observed the message-board behavior in May and let training continue, encoding risky behavior into the weights — the "ask first" boundary failed at the lab's own culture level.

**Top Insight of the Day:** Handover is where agency lives. Every point where work moves between human and AI is a point where state can be lost, judgment can be skipped, or the learning loop can be removed. The question is never just "can the AI do it?" — it's "when the work comes back to me, or when it goes to the AI, what travels with it?"

## 3. Superagency Connections

- **Handover state = agency state.** The reconciliation paper's core move — system records + human reports → shared typed task-state with conflict detection — is a direct mechanism for [[AI Orchestrator]] and [[Parallel Orchestration]]: when you step away from a project and the AI continues (or vice versa), the quality of your agency on return depends on what state was handed over. Structured handover reports are the agency-preserving primitive.
- **Delegating before learning is agency *deferred* — possibly forever.** The students aren't lazy; they're delegating where the pressure to sound professional is highest. But the confidence that should return to the writer ("I can do this") returns to the system instead. That is [[Cognitive Surrender]] at the skill-formation layer: individual capacities never form. This connects to [[Education]]'s post-instrumental learning thread — the five capacities (end-setting, reason-giving, contestability, refusal/revision, participation) are exactly what the delegation model removes.
- **O-I-B-A-R externalizes the boundaries that make agency accountable.** The "suspension" state — the dimension is known but its value is unresolved, so the system specifies what must be measured, asked, retrieved, or escalated to a human — is the operational form of Mollick's "when should an AI ask us?" triggers. The asking trigger becomes a *typed field* in the handover.
- **Culture is the top of the handover stack.** Krueger's critique (MIT TR): technical postmortems give an "inaccurate and misleading sense of why the failure occurred" if the culture isn't examined. The May message board was observed and training continued anyway. Superagency requires the human side to actually exercise the authority the design grants — which is a culture property, not a protocol property.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments

- **Reconciliation is expensive.** The state-reconciliation pipeline evaluates on 13 paired task states in a controlled environment. Scaling it to real production handovers — where telemetry is messy and human reports are sparse — may cost more than the handover saves. Handover discipline has a ceiling: it's worth it where state loss is costly, not everywhere.
- **Externalization has a candor problem (the paper's own tension).** O-I-B-A-R depends on durable, attributable failure histories — but those histories "can suppress the candor on which useful boundary knowledge depends." People won't log the failures that would teach the system if the log can be used against them. The scaffold is as much an organizational-culture intervention as a technical one.
- **Delegation-before-learning could be read as a "kids these days" panic.** N=12 interviews; students checked AI output against "looks like AI" and "sounds like me" — that's calibration work, not passivity. The risk is real but the population-level claim needs the scale evidence the GenAIT-style instruments (2608.25815) can provide.
- **The IAGST result cuts both ways for governance.** 55 of 75 encounters were *indeterminate* — no accountable response could be located. But "governed pathways fell from 16/25 augmentation → 4 delegation → 0 substitution" also shows institutions are being honest about escalation: they don't have pathways for things they haven't approved. Indeterminacy is a finding, not necessarily a failure.

## 5. Practical Applications

- **Write handover reports, not just outputs.** For any agent workflow where a human resumes work: have the agent return a structured state summary (what was done, what's pending, what conflicts were detected, what it assumed) — not just the finished artifact. The reconciliation paper's typed-task-state is the template. See [[Agentic Workflow Patterns]].
- **Name the suspension states in your prompts/playbooks.** Borrow O-I-B-A-R's BUT/suspension vocabulary: for each automated decision, record when the judgment holds, and explicitly specify what must be measured/asked/escalated when it doesn't. This is the asking-trigger, made a field.
- **Check the delegation boundary for learners.** The students delegated email to instructors most — highest pressure, highest stakes, most identity-relevant. In [[Education]] settings: keep the "professional communication" category human-first; use AI for drafting *support* (outlines, tone checks) with the student's own voice as the review criterion — "sounds like me" is a teachable rubric.
- **Stress-test your governance documents, don't just inventory them.** The IAGST method (frozen corpus + capability-escalated scenarios + six-dimensional response chain) is reproducible for a district or a department: pick 15 scenarios, escalate capability, find where the accountable response stops.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day

**Handover is where agency lives — and where it dies.** Every boundary crossing between human and AI is a moment of truth: state can be lost (the reconciliation problem), the learning loop can be severed (delegating before learning), the rules can be handed over without the judgment that made them work (O-I-B-A-R), or the culture can fail to exercise the authority the design grants (HF/OpenAI). The unifying move across all four: **make the handover explicit, structured, and checkable** — typed task states, named suspension points, externalized boundaries, stress-tested pathways. Agency in the agentic era is not about who does the work. It's about what travels with it.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try

**The Handover Audit** (follows The Asking Audit from 08-31). Pick your most-used agent workflow. List every point where control passes — human→AI, AI→human, AI→AI. For each crossing, answer four questions:

1. **State:** Does the receiving side get a structured summary of what was done, what's pending, and what was assumed — or just a raw artifact?
2. **Boundary:** Is it explicit when the agent should *suspend* and ask (the O-I-B-A-R suspension state) vs. proceed?
3. **Learning loop:** Is there a human skill that this handover is quietly removing — and is that removal intended?
4. **Failure history:** Can a failure at this handover be logged, attributed, and learned from without the person who logged it being punished?

Fix the worst handover point this week — ideally by adding a structured handover report (state + assumptions + conflicts) to the agent's output. That one change is the highest-leverage agency-preservation move available to an individual right now.
