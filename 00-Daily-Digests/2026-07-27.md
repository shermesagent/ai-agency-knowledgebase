## Top 5 Papers

### 1. FlowEvo — Self-Evolving Agents (arXiv 2607.21596)

**The paper:** Ren et al. present a training-free framework that compiles successful agent traces into reusable skill records with admission checks (interface, replay, safety). Three coupled mechanisms: workflow-to-skill compilation, skill-to-workflow feedback, and skill curation that suppresses negative transfer.

**The result:** 82.8% success rate on ALFWorld — **23.6 points above the strongest baseline**, with less than half the token usage of the most efficient competitor.

**The Superagency connection:** This is the accumulation layer. Phase 3 agents set goals. Phase 3.5 agents remember how they achieved them and build on those memories. For organizations deploying AI, the difference between "agent executes tasks" and "agent builds institutional memory" is the difference between temporary productivity and compounding intelligence.

### 2. Opaque Epistemic Mediation (arXiv 2607.22513)

**The paper:** Scarso et al. tested how four LLM families evaluate ethnonationalist pseudo-science across four temporal snapshots. Grok's Fast versions (default X experience) consistently assigned credibility scores of 70-75 — two to five times higher than all other models (15-40). This pattern was absent from control prompts on basic scientific consensus.

**The bombshell findings:** (1) A silent patch reversed Grok's behavior from chaotic to stably high validation overnight with no public documentation. (2) The same model identifier produced radically divergent outputs via API (75) vs web (5.5) three months later. (3) Refusal to rate — the most defensible response — appeared in two model families through different interfaces and *eroded in the successor version of each*.

**The Superagency connection:** This is the Calibration layer's nightmare scenario. The epistemic stance of a commercial LLM is not a stable property of the model — it's a contingent effect of deployment configuration: system prompts, safety layers, interface routing, silent updates. Users have no way to know whether the AI they're consulting today is the same AI they consulted yesterday. This is not a bug; it's a feature of the architecture. The paper argues this constitutes a matter of public concern requiring new forms of epistemic accountability — a demand the five-layer architecture's Calibration and Scaffolding layers are designed to meet.

→ Relevant to [[03-Arguments/AI Enclosure]], [[06-Frameworks/Risk-Benefit Matrix]]

### 3. LLMs Create an Uneven Informational Layer Over Cities (arXiv 2607.06260)

**The paper:** Chen et al. audited restaurant recommendations from three major LLMs across 304 neighborhoods in five U.S. cities using 320 synthetic user profiles.

**Key findings:** LLMs both fabricate venues and systematically overlook real ones. Fabrication concentrates in neighborhoods with weaker digital and physical footprints. But **invisibility persists even when models are given verified venue lists**: 47.5% of establishments are never recommended, and 31.9% of these blind spots are shared across all three model families. Higher-income users receive more expensive and less popular venues. Tourists are directed toward costlier but more socially diverse establishments than locals.

**The Superagency connection:** This is a *new dimension of enclosure*. Subscription-based, political, and analytical enclosure all gate *access* to AI. Informational enclosure gates *what AI chooses to show you* — and it persists even when access is perfect. The 31.9% shared-blind-spot rate is the key number: these aren't model-specific errors but stable patterns of selective attention, which means they scale with AI adoption rather than being solved by model competition.

→ Relevant to [[03-Arguments/AI Enclosure]], [[AI and Inequality]]

### 4. Co-Design Drives Overtrust (arXiv 2607.21757)

**The paper:** People who co-designed an LLM-based preference agent rated its results as more accurate — even when they weren't. Participation produced overtrust when not paired with calibration feedback.

**The Superagency connection:** Co-design is normally a good thing — it aligns AI with human values and needs. But this paper shows that participation without calibration is value-destructive: psychological ownership suppresses skepticism. The fix isn't less participation — it's participation + transparency about error rates. Every participatory AI design process must include a calibration step: here's when this tool is wrong, here's how often, here's why.

This is the Calibration layer's most counterintuitive mandate: the more people are involved in building AI, the more they need to be shown its failures. Trust earned through participation must be balanced by trust calibrated through evidence.

→ Integrated into [[02-Domains/Healthcare]]

### 5. Human-AI Substitution Principle (arXiv 2607.20781)

**The paper:** Banerjee & Singh present a formal analytical model (HAT — Human-AI Task Allocation) for hierarchical organizations. The key formalization: economic asymmetry between human skill acquisition (slow, costly, bounded) and AI capability scaling (fast, cheap, potentially unbounded).

**Key results:** AI adoption can produce abrupt workforce transitions. Middle-management roles exhibit elevated vulnerability. The vulnerability of highly skilled workers depends on a skill threshold shaped by organizational depth, baseline costs, and risk differentials. Risk heterogeneity can sustain human and AI roles without requiring a minimum-human-fraction constraint.

**The Superagency connection:** The HAT model is the Development layer's economics. The question isn't just "should we automate this?" — it's "what structural conditions make automation likely regardless of whether we should?" The five-gate PHP-AIO protocol (integrated into [[04-Use-Cases/AI for Small Businesses]]) provides a defense against automation driven by cost asymmetry alone. But the HAT model suggests that organizational depth itself — how many layers of management separate the decision from the work — determines vulnerability. Flatter organizations are more resilient to AI substitution not because they're smarter about AI but because their structure leaves fewer roles that AI can absorb.

→ Relevant to [[04-Use-Cases/AI for Small Businesses]], [[Future of Work]]

## Practical Experiment: Build a Personal Skill Bank

FlowEvo's core insight — compile successful traces into reusable skill records — is implementable today without any framework:

1. **For one week, log every successful AI-assisted task.** Not the prompt, but what was accomplished. Format: "Task: [what]. Approach: [how]. Key insight: [what made it work]."

2. **At the end of the week, compile the log into a skill bank.** Group by task type. Identify patterns. The skill bank is a living document — add to it weekly.

3. **Before starting a new task, check the skill bank.** Did you solve something similar before? Retrieve the approach and adapt it.

4. **After one month, audit the skill bank.** Which skills got reused? Which caused negative transfer (the approach that worked once but failed on retry)? Suppress the negative-transfer skills — FlowEvo's curation mechanism, applied manually.

The result is a personal institutional memory — the difference between someone who uses AI and someone whose use of AI compounds over time.
