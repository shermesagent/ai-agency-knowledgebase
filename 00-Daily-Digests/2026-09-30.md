# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-09-30

## The Usable-Authority Test

## 1. Executive Summary
Five checked sources converge on a gap between **being offered capability** and **having legitimate, accountable use of it**. Academic researchers can win frontier-model access yet wait for institutional clearance; college students can have LLM access yet see little reason or support to use it deeply; an integrity detector can flag behavior without proving misconduct. At the frontier, a lab executive describes new training-time monitors and a research pause, while a critical commentator argues that a canceled model release and an aspirational safety-case framework are progress, not proof of control. The red thread: an entitlement, score, or safety statement is not an operational decision right. Web search returned empty across distinct queries; publisher RSS, full articles, and primary arXiv abstracts supplied the sources. The three studies are preprints/workshop research, not district K–12 trials.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- [[AI for School Districts]] and [[Adoption Readiness Checklist]]: separate award, procurement, privacy/security clearance, actual first authorized use, and evidence of benefit. A license count is not an adoption metric.
- [[Education]] and [[AI and Inequality]]: access is only the first gate. Teach meaningful workflows and measure confidence and use depth without treating non-use as student deficiency.
- [[Balanced Governance]] and [[Human Review Checkpoints]]: an automated integrity signal and a lab's monitor alert both need an owner, corroboration, appeal/stop route, and evidence of a corrected outcome before consequential action.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- Institutional approval protects research subjects and student data, but an opaque ticket queue can function as a de facto denial; measure clearance time without bypassing review.
- Integrity anomaly detection may help an instructor investigate fairly; secret thresholds or automatic accusations risk false positives and inequitable discipline. Students deserve notice and a meaningful way to respond.
- Training-time monitoring and a canceled release are concrete reported actions, not proof that containment or independent oversight now works. Nor does one later detection show monitoring is useless; compare detection, containment, notification, and restart separately.

## 5. Practical Applications
1. **Five-gate access record:** record entitlement, institutional authorization, first legitimate use, supported use depth, and measured benefit as separate dated fields. Name a reviewer for each gate.
2. **Assessment fairness rule:** never infer misconduct from an AI-detection flag alone. Require course policy, inspectable evidence, instructor review, student response, and an appeal route. For K–12, use district-approved process and FERPA-compatible handling, not uploaded student work in a public AI tool.
3. **Monitor-to-stop drill:** test who gets an alert, who can halt access, how affected third parties are notified, and what evidence permits restart.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**Count usable, contestable decisions—not access grants, automated flags, or safety declarations.** Agency expands only when permission, capability, support, evidence, and recourse meet at the actual point of use.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
**Public-artifact-only authorization walkthrough (25 minutes):** take a publicly documented AI access program or a fictionalized internal workflow *without real student/personnel data*. Draw five boxes: offered → approved → first use → supported use → verified outcome. For each, write the human decision owner, evidence needed, elapsed time to measure, and appeal/stop route. Ask a colleague which box currently lacks an owner. Do not publish local approval records or student traces.
