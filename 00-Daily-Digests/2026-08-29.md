# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-08-29

**Theme: The Threshold Question.** Reach asked how far an agent should extend (08-28); the postmortem asked what happens when the loop fails (08-27). Today the question is where the line sits — the substitution/complement threshold. Zvi surfaced Steve Hsu's model: *below some threshold of human ability, AI is a substitute; above it, AI becomes a complement* — and Zvi's inversion: as AI improves, more people's ability falls below the line. Every major source today is a contest over that line: 100+ AI companies warn the cyber-defender line has been crossed ("months" to prepare); a JAMA paper argues AI alone crosses the doctor line on five core tasks; Meta's data-center robots cross the physical-labor line; job-retraining programs fail to move anyone across it; and artists discover the line for data protection doesn't exist at all. The Threshold Question: **where does the line sit, and can human agency move it — or only redraw it?**

## Executive Summary

1. **The industry's own alarm: AI-enabled cyberattack is "months" away, per 100+ cosigned companies.** OpenAI, Anthropic, and more than 100 companies cosigned a letter (reported by WIRED's security roundup, 08-29) warning that everyone else has mere months to prepare for AI-enabled cyberattacks — the industry self-warning that follows the Hugging Face incident's demonstration that fully automated offense exists (see 08-27 digest). The week also brought the FBI/DOJ takedown of QScan and QTRouter — the hacking platforms of QTFY, a Chinese state-sponsored group that breached the Federal Reserve, NASA, the Energy/DOJ/HHS departments, and the US Senate. Offense is industrialized on both sides; the letter is the defenders' collective admission that they are below the threshold.
2. **A JAMA paper claims AI alone will exceed physicians — and "humans in the loop degrade AI performance."** Emanuel, Khosla et al., "Will Autonomous AI Exceed AI-Physicians as the Best Medical Care?" (WIRED's Steven Levy, 08-28): reviewing all published AI-in-medicine research since Jan 1, 2024, they argue medicine "is rapidly approaching the transition point at which AI alone will exceed physicians and physician AI-hybrids" on five fundamental tasks — history-taking, diagnosis, test selection, treatment, chronic-disease management. Their recommendation: clinicians should refrain from meddling. AMA CEO John Whyte dissents (simulations, not blind trials; a Feb 2026 Nature study found most patients couldn't effectively converse with LLMs); Wachter concedes the argument's importance while invoking the "doorman fallacy" — the human role shrinks but doesn't vanish.
3. **The threshold model of labor: retraining doesn't work, and the "problem handled" frame is the danger.** Zvi's AI #183 (08-27) catalogs: job retraining programs "sound great, are very popular and don't work," raising employment ratios by only a few percent — a small mistake unless people treat them as *the answer* to displacement, which they're not. Steve Hsu's threshold model (below the line, AI substitutes; above, it complements) predicts winner-take-most in law and elsewhere: top talent is worth more, everyone else gets automated away. Gates has moved to "economic catastrophe" framing — and Zvi's meta-observation is the agency-relevant one: Gates is a barometer of a *preference cascade*, where AI concern stops looking odd. When the rich and connected start treating the threshold as real, the politics of the line change.
4. **Meta's data-center robots cross the physical threshold — 80% of some workloads.** WIRED (08-28): Meta is testing robots (Kinova Gen3 arms, Watney, ABB) for cable swapping, server power-cycling, and restarting devices; one worker estimates a bot could replace up to 80% of some people's workloads — "We thought those of us performing the physical tasks were safe for a while, but not anymore. It's coming for us all, unfortunately." Meta's response: "we need more workers, not fewer" amid the biggest infrastructure boom since WWII. The physical-labor line is moving in real time, in the least glamorous jobs first.
5. **The scrape-and-enclose cycle: 12 million artworks for under $10.** WIRED (08-28): Cara — the artist portfolio platform built for people who *don't* want their work used for AI training — was hit by three scrapes in August: a 12-terabyte archive of 12M works (the scraper bragged it cost less than $10), 8.5M links + metadata uploaded to Hugging Face (which refused further takedowns: "no copies of the artworks are hosted here"), and 123,000 images with personal info on Academic Torrents. The twist: the original scraper ("Heft") was so struck by the harm that he's now collaborating with founder Jingna Zhang on an open-source artist-protection tool. "If it makes them feel better, deleting your work and leaving Cara, I support that. But... if they go somewhere else, they are safer? They're not. Bigger platforms get scraped more." Enclosure by default; protection is a legal battle fought with GoFundMe money.

## Superagency Connections

- **The threshold model is the missing frame for [[The Turing Trap]].** Brynjolfsson's warning — human-like imitation steers AI toward substitution — now has a location: the line where AI ability crosses human ability, per task. Hsu/Zvi give the trap a gradient: complementarity above the line, substitution below it, and the line moves as AI improves. The agency response isn't to fight the line — it's to design for the side you're on: above the line, AI amplifies ([[Intelligence Amplification]]); below it, humans get automated ([[Task-Level AI Adoption]]'s preserve-human category is the explicit refusal to cross).
- **The doctors fight is [[Human Review Checkpoints]] under maximum stress.** The JAMA paper's "humans in the loop degrade AI performance" is the strongest empirical claim yet against the review-gate design this wiki tracks. The counter — 2607.28677's asymmetric-loss argument (a must-not-miss diagnosis is a low-probability, high-value outcome that most-probable-token AI is structurally blind to) — is the answer: the human in the loop is not a performance drag; the human is where the loss function is priced. The threshold claim is task-specific, and the tasks where humans are "degrading" (documentation, history-taking, refills) are precisely the tasks where the loss function is symmetric. ([[Healthcare]], [[Risk-Benefit Matrix]])
- **Retraining's failure is the governance gap no one wants to price.** If retraining "doesn't work" and the threshold keeps rising, then the honest policy instruments are direct income support and demand-side job creation, not supply-side reskilling. That's an uncomfortable conclusion for every institution (schools included) whose answer to displacement is "we'll train people." This wiki's [[Education]] page tracks the same pattern from the other side: the entry-level pathway is what's breaking, and reskilling people into AI-accelerable skills resubmits them to the same automation pressure.
- **The Cara scrape is [[AI Enclosure]]'s data front made literal.** The platform built to *opt out* of training was scraped three times in a month, at near-zero cost, with platform-hosted takedown limits ("no copies hosted here"). Enclosure isn't just subscription gates and export controls — it's the inability to withhold your own data from the training commons. The scraper-turned-collaborator is the human-scale version of the [[AI and Creator Rights]] fight: even the attacker, confronted with the harm, switched sides. Agency at the data layer currently requires a GoFundMe.
- **Local LLMs are the counter-enclosure move — and WIRED just mainstreamed them.** "How to Run a Chatbot on Your Own Computer" (08-29) is the practical-ai counterpart to the enclosure page: offline access, no subscription, data never leaves your machine, free Meta/Google models. This is the agency answer to the cyber-apocalypse letter too — when the cloud is the attack surface, local inference is a defensive posture. ([[Home Server AI Agents]], [[Compute and Agency]])
- **The Breaker's Advantage is the offense/defense asymmetry with a business model.** Stratechery extends the HF-incident lesson (attackers break things; defenders must not) to startups vs. incumbents and to the AI economy's adoption timeline. The optimistic read: AI's economic takeover is slower than hype suggests because incumbents are structurally defensive. The agency read: the same asymmetry that favors the attacker favors the entrepreneur — offense is where leverage lives.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

- **"Humans degrade AI performance" vs. "the human is where the loss function lives."** The JAMA paper and this wiki's Clinician's Veto/abstention architecture cannot both be right about the same tasks — and the resolution is task structure. For documentation, history intake, and refill triage, symmetric-loss tasks, the paper's claim is plausible and the "degrade" finding is a design bug (checkpoints placed at the wrong layer). For diagnosis under uncertainty, asymmetric-loss tasks, the veto is not a drag — it is the decision. The danger is letting the paper's sweeping version ("AI alone") license the removal of humans from the asymmetric-loss tasks, where the evidence is weakest.
- **The industry letter is a warning and a lobby.** 100+ companies saying "months to prepare" could be responsible disclosure — or it could be the industry setting the table for defensive-security procurement and favorable liability rules. The HF postmortem's own lesson applies here: verify the instrument, don't just register the alarm. The letter is a signal that the frontier labs *believe* offense is outrunning defense; it is not proof.
- **Meta's "we need more workers" is technically true and strategically soothing.** Robots replacing 80% of some workloads in the fastest-scaling industry in America is a labor-market event even if total headcount rises: it changes *which* jobs exist (fewer physical-tech roles, more robot-maintenance and training roles), and the displaced worker's skills don't transfer automatically. "More workers, not fewer" describes aggregate demand, not individual security — the same gap the retraining critique names.
- **The threshold model can read as fatalism.** If the line is "as AI improves, more people fall below it," the only rational individual strategy is to sprint up the complementarity side forever. The counterweight from this week: the line is not a single global boundary — it is per-task, per-context, and per-loss-function. A doctor below the line on documentation is above it on prognosis. The agency question is not "which side of the line am I on?" but "which tasks keep me on the complement side, and what institutions protect that?"

## Practical Applications

1. **Map your own threshold.** For your five most-used professional tasks, ask: is AI currently a complement or a substitute here — and is the line moving? Write down the task, the current side, and one action that keeps you on the complement side (deepening judgment, client relationships, context, taste). This is the Hsu model as a personal audit ([[Task-Level AI Adoption]]).
2. **Check your checkpoint placement.** If you run any AI in a high-stakes flow, ask whether your human review sits at the symmetric-loss or asymmetric-loss stage. The JAMA paper's "humans degrade" finding is real — for the wrong tasks. Move your checkpoints to the asymmetric-loss decisions and stop reviewing the documentation output ([[Human Review Checkpoints]]).
3. **Run the local-LLM weekend project.** WIRED's guide is a Saturday-afternoon task: pick a free model (Meta or Google), install on your machine (16 GB RAM is enough for the useful tier), and move one recurring private task — summaries of sensitive documents, drafting, classification — onto local inference. You'll own the privacy, skip the subscription, and have a defensive posture if cloud access becomes the attack surface ([[Home Server AI Agents]]).
4. **Test the retraining assumption before funding it.** Before approving any reskilling program, ask the Zvi question: does it end in *direct job placement* with a specific employer (the only variant that works), or does it end in a certificate? If the latter, treat it as a small positive and a large false comfort — not as "the problem handled" ([[Education]], [[Future of Work]]).
5. **Audit your data-holding posture.** The Cara scrape shows the default is exposure: any platform holding your work is a scrape away from the training commons. For anything you care about — student work, family photos, your own writing — decide deliberately what's public, what's gated, and what should never be on a platform at all. The <$10 cost of 12M works is the price of the default ([[AI and Creator Rights]], [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]]).

## Top Insight

**The threshold is the variable; agency is a strategy for living on the right side of it — per task, not per person.** The day's sources converge on a single mechanism: AI ability is crossing human ability at different rates in different domains, and everything else — the cyber-apocalypse letter, the doctors fight, the data-center robots, retraining's failure, the artist scrape — is downstream of that crossing. The unhelpful version of this is fatalism: "the line rises, so humans lose." The useful version is per-task: medicine's documentation line has crossed (and humans genuinely degrade that output), while its prognosis line hasn't (the doorman fallacy); the data-center cable-swap line has crossed, while the judgment-of-what-to-build line hasn't; the retraining line never moves because it trains people for the wrong side of already-crossed lines. The practical Superagency program is therefore not "stop the threshold" — it's the constant, institutionally supported work of identifying which tasks keep humans on the complement side, moving checkpoints to the asymmetric-loss decisions, and refusing to let "we'll retrain them" substitute for the harder distributional choices. That is what the Threshold Question is for: not to predict the line, but to force the question of what we're doing about it.

## Practical Experiment to Try: The Threshold Audit

Yesterday's Reach Audit mapped how far your agents extend. Today's audit maps where your line sits:

1. **List your recurring tasks** — the 10-15 things you actually do in a week, at work and at home.
2. **Score each one on two axes:** (a) *Side of the line*: does AI currently complement (you direct, it executes) or substitute (it could do the whole task without you)? (b) *Loss asymmetry*: if the AI gets it wrong, is the cost symmetric (redo it) or asymmetric (a must-not-miss that cascades)?
3. **Apply the two rules:** Move your human review effort to the asymmetric-loss tasks and stop reviewing the symmetric ones (the JAMA "degrade" finding, applied correctly). For tasks on the substitution side, name the institution or practice that could move you back to complement — or accept the crossing deliberately.
4. **Run one local-LLM task.** Pick one recurring private task and move it onto local inference this weekend. You'll own the data, and you'll have personally tested the counter-enclosure move the Cara scrape makes urgent.
