# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-05

**The Reliance-and-Resilience Test** · Thursday curator pass · Five distinct sources: two non-paper pieces and three preprints. The findings are source-specific, not five independent demonstrations of successful AI deployment.

## Executive Summary
AI can widen useful capacity without preserving the foundations that let people trust it: political permission for compute, reliable evidence for public decisions, professional skill for oversight, meaningful participation in school policy, and real-world validation in science. **The question is not only whether AI can do the next task; it is whether the community, institution and worker can still steer what happens after it does.**

## Top 5 Findings
1. **Political permission changes the economics of access.** Thompson's argument: restricting new compute sites may give communities leverage while favoring firms with existing permitted capacity and keeping AI usage costly. The consequence is plausible, not proven. → [[Compute and Agency]], [[AI Enclosure]].
2. **Simulated discovery does not remove physical validation.** WIRED reports a startup planning physics-based synthetic chemical data; outside experts emphasize limits of prediction and the time/cost of animal and human tests. The useful outcome would be better triage, not a skipped safety process. → [[Healthcare]], [[AI-Augmented Scientific Collaboration]].
3. **The worker's future competence is an adoption outcome.** Diener et al.'s dynamic model finds that immediate reliance can leave novices with low skill and experts losing skill even while assisted accuracy looks good. It motivates—but does not validate—protected independent practice. → [[Work]], [[Cognitive Surrender]].
4. **Similar accuracy can mask different public decisions.** Yorke reports 68.2–70.3% accuracy across eight configurations, with 24.3% of questions changing label across unchanged repeats and human-review routing ranging 0.9–41.0% under the study's procedure. → [[Adoption Readiness Checklist]], [[The Judge Problem]].
5. **Policy vocabulary is not local operating capacity.** Memon et al. find privacy and academic integrity in 97% of sampled state guidance and equity of access in 86%, while specific capacity-building/accountability lags; only five documents include students as contributors and none include parents. → [[Education]], [[AI for School Districts]].

## Superagency Connections
**Capacity:** simulation and AI assistance can expand what scientists and workers attempt. **Freedom:** residents and educators need meaningful influence before infrastructure or school policies become fixed. **Platforms:** site permissions and review-routing rules determine who controls the benefit. **Value:** measure human skill retention, stable decisions and real-world validation rather than task throughput. **Wisdom:** an accountable institution can explain what it did *not* establish. Read with [[Human Agency]] and [[Balanced Governance]].

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- A local veto may protect a community yet concentrate compute among incumbents; approval without independently tested local costs is not the remedy.
- Simulation could improve candidate selection without replacing lab work; neither the startup's ambitions nor the expert cautions quantify future clinical impact.
- Independent practice costs time and can be frustrating. Choose skills worth retaining instead of treating every task as a training exercise.
- Repeated AI classifications may vary; human labels also need quality checks. Repeatability alone is not validity.
- Documented contributor lists may omit informal consultations; ask states/districts directly before asserting that no family input occurred anywhere.

## Practical Applications
For a district AI-use decision, add three evidence columns to a small *approved or public-data-only* pilot brief: **(1) who can contest the decision; (2) what independent check shows the result holds across runs; (3) what unaided human capability must remain.** For infrastructure questions, add host-community cost and incumbent-access effects. For science or health claims, distinguish candidate triage from tested outcomes. No identifiable student, employee or patient data should be entered into an unapproved tool.

## Top Insight of the Day
**An AI rollout is not resilient if the public cannot contest its footprint, the institution cannot defend its evidence, or the worker cannot perform the oversight skill without the tool.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
With a public, non-personal set of ten already-labeled policy topics or school-document snippets, ask two adults to agree on a human reference label and reasons first. Run one *approved* AI configuration twice, record disagreements and which examples would reach review, then have the reviewers decide whether any category-level conclusion would change. Keep this a design rehearsal, not an inference about actual students or employees; no production decision follows from ten examples.
