**[OpenAI] "How Agents Are Transforming Work" / "The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex"** (June 25, 2026, 4 days ago)

- **What:** OpenAI's landmark research paper analyzing Codex usage across the company and its ecosystem. The paper provides the first large-scale empirical evidence of the transition from chatbot interfaces to agentic workflows, using internal telemetry from 98% of OpenAI employees and external non-developer adoption data.
- **Key findings:**
  - **Codex dominates output.** As of June 11, 2026, Codex accounts for 99.8% of output tokens at OpenAI — the chatbot is vestigial; the agent is primary.
  - **98% employee adoption.** Nearly all OpenAI employees now use Codex agents for work tasks, moving from simple chatbot questions to delegating multi-step, complex jobs.
  - **Tasks are getting longer.** 25% of Codex tasks exceed eight hours — sustained autonomous workflows, not quick exchanges. This is the quantitative confirmation of the agent paradigm: agents don't just answer, they execute.
  - **Productivity multipliers are extreme.** Legal workers' output boosted 13-fold; researchers over 50-fold. These are not marginal efficiency gains — they're order-of-magnitude shifts.
  - **Non-developer growth is explosive.** Individual non-developer users multiplied 137x since August 2025; organizational non-developer users increased 189x. The agent revolution is no longer a developer-only phenomenon — it's crossing into knowledge work broadly.
  - **The shift is rapid but uneven.** Weekly active Codex usage increased sharply over the first six months of 2026, but adoption patterns vary by role, task type, and organization.

But the unevenness of the shift (rapid inside OpenAI, varied externally) also surfaces the agency distribution question: who gets these multipliers, and who's still waiting? ### The Dual-Use Persuasion Asymmetry

**[arXiv] "AI Persuasive Framing in Collective Dilemmas"** (2606.27951, new, June 29, 2026)

- **What:** 1,283 participants in iterated Collective Risk Games tested whether AI assistants could nudge cooperation through personalized persuasive framing based on Social Value Orientation profiles.
- **Key finding — the asymmetry is the story:** AI persuasive framing significantly increased contributions and group success rates — but these cooperative effects were short-lived, fading after the first few rounds. When the same AI treatments were reconfigured to promote selfish behavior through exculpatory framing, the negative effects were **larger and substantially more persistent**, particularly for personalized interventions.
- **Why it matters:** This is the persuasion layer's dual-use problem in microcosm. The same AI that can nudge people toward cooperation can nudge them more effectively and more persistently toward defection. The asymmetry — pro-social effects are transient, anti-social effects are sticky — has profound implications for AI deployed in collective action settings (climate, public health, civic participation). The June 23 digest documented that AI can out-persuade expert humans; this paper shows that the persuasive advantage may be inherently asymmetric in the wrong direction.
- **Superagency relevance:** AI persuasion as an agency amplifier cuts both ways. It can amplify collective agency (cooperation on shared problems) or individual defection (free-riding that undermines collective action). The asymmetry toward persistent anti-social effects challenges the Superagency thesis: if AI persuasion is a net-negative for collective action, agency amplification at the individual level may come at the cost of agency destruction at the collective level. Connects to [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[Public Trust and AI]], and [[Human Agency]].
- **Score:** Reliability 4, Relevance 4
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27951

### Keystroke Dynamics as Cognitive Effort Signal

**[arXiv] "Typing Behavior in Human-LLM Interaction: Keystroke Dynamics Reveal Cognitive Effort During Prompting"** (2606.28090, new, June 29, 2026)

- **What:** A user study (N=36) examining whether keystroke dynamics can serve as real-time behavioral measures of mental effort during LLM interaction. Participants completed easy and hard tasks on both desktop and mobile devices.
- **Key findings:**
  - Hard tasks led to significantly more keystrokes, slower typing, increased pauses, and higher self-reported workload (NASA-TLX).
  - Device type had weaker effects — the cognitive signal is in the keystrokes, not the platform.
  - **Keystrokes captured cognitive effort but did NOT predict perceived LLM output usefulness.** Users could be working hard without finding the AI's help useful, or find it useful without working hard. Cognitive effort and perceived collaboration success are distinct dimensions.
- **Why it matters:** This is the most granular HCI data yet on what it FEELS like to work with LLMs. The finding that keystrokes capture effort but not satisfaction suggests that AI interface design needs to measure both what users DO (effort) and what they EXPERIENCE (usefulness). It also provides an empirical basis for the "cognitive surrender" concept: if hard tasks require significantly more keystrokes, the temptation to accept AI output without verification is a rational response to cognitive load, not just laziness. The practical implication: if you're typing less with AI over time, you may not be getting better at prompting — you may be surrendering more cognition.
- **Superagency relevance:** Keystroke dynamics provide a potential real-time signal of when human agency is being exercised vs. surrendered during AI interaction. Hard tasks requiring more keystrokes suggests cognitive engagement; easy tasks with fewer keystrokes may indicate passive acceptance. A measurable "agency signal" in typing behavior could become a UX primitive — alerting users when their interaction pattern suggests cognitive surrender. Connects to [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[Family and Personal Life]], and [[Practical AI]].
- **Score:** Reliability 4, Relevance 4
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28090

### LLM Reasoning Traces Predict Human Learning Difficulty

**[arXiv] "Cognitive Episodes in LLM Reasoning Traces Enable Interpretable Human Item Difficulty Prediction"** (2606.28186, new/cross, June 29, 2026)

- **What:** The Epi2Diff (Episode to Difficulty) framework maps LLM reasoning traces into cognitively grounded episode sequences — grouping trace segments into functional problem-solving states — to predict human item difficulty.
- **Key findings:**
  - Epi2Diff consistently outperforms strong baselines including fine-tuned small language models, LLM in-context learning, and supervised LLM adaptation.
  - On SAT-derived classification benchmarks, Epi2Diff achieves an 8.1% average relative gain over supervised LLM fine-tuning baselines.
  - **Harder items induce more effortful, iterative, and implementation-centered episode dynamics — not merely longer responses.** The structure of reasoning, not its length, predicts difficulty.
  - The framework extracts compact episode-dynamic features (reasoning scale, effort allocation, state transitions) and combines them with semantic item representations.
- **Why it matters for education:** This is a new lens for educational measurement. Instead of asking humans to rate item difficulty (expensive, slow, subjective), LLM reasoning traces can predict it — and the prediction is interpretable (you can see WHY an item is hard by examining the episode dynamics). For AI tutoring, this means difficulty calibration can be automated and explained. For assessment design, it means item difficulty can be predicted before items reach human test-takers.
- **Superagency relevance:** The educational agency dimension: if AI can predict what students will find difficult, tutoring systems can adapt more precisely, teachers can anticipate misconceptions before they surface, and assessment can be calibrated without extensive human piloting. The interpretability of the predictions — knowing WHY something is hard, not just THAT it's hard — is what makes this an agency amplifier rather than a black-box replacement. Connects to [[Education]], [[AI Tutors]], and [[Democratization of Expertise]].
- **Score:** Reliability 4, Relevance 4
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28186

## Tensions / Counterarguments

- **The OpenAI adoption data is from OpenAI.** The 98% employee adoption, 99.8% output token share, and 13-50x productivity multipliers come from the company that builds Codex — not an independent third party. Internal usage patterns at a frontier AI lab may not generalize to other organizations. Treat as strong signal, not universal proof.

- **Non-developer adoption growth (137x) is from a very small base.** August 2025 non-developer Codex usage was likely near-zero. 137x growth from a tiny base is still a small absolute number. The growth rate is impressive; the absolute adoption level may still be modest.

- **The persuasion asymmetry may be task-specific.** Collective Risk Games capture one type of collective action problem. Whether the pro-social/anti-social asymmetry generalizes to other cooperative contexts (climate, public health, civic participation) is an open empirical question.

- **GPT-5.6's defensive-over-offensive cyber asymmetry could shift with fine-tuning or deployment context.** System card evaluations measure controlled settings. Real-world deployment — with varied users, prompts, and objectives — may surface capabilities the evaluations missed. The asymmetry is a positive signal, not a permanent guarantee.

- **Keystroke dynamics as cognitive effort signal is N=36.** The study is small, lab-based, and uses contrived tasks. Whether the signal holds in naturalistic, extended, real-world LLM interaction is unknown.

## Practical Applications

1. **Audit your own agentic shift.** The OpenAI paper provides a template: what percentage of your AI interactions are chatbot-style (single question/answer) vs. agent-style (delegating multi-step tasks)? If you're still mostly in chatbot mode, the 137x non-developer growth curve suggests you're behind the adoption curve. Try delegating one multi-hour task to an agent this week.

2. **Track your keystroke dynamics pattern.** The keystroke study suggests that harder tasks produce more keystrokes. If you notice you're typing less as you use AI more, it may be a signal of cognitive surrender — you're accepting AI output without the wrestling that produces genuine understanding. The practical countermeasure: deliberately type out your own draft or analysis before engaging AI, even when it's less efficient.

3. **Use the persuasion asymmetry as a design principle.** When deploying AI for collective action (team coordination, community decisions, civic participation), build in safeguards against the anti-social persistence bias: limit personalization, require re-confirmation of cooperative choices after time delays, and monitor for the pattern where a single anti-social nudge propagates across rounds while pro-social nudges fade.

4. **Pre-test educational materials with LLM reasoning traces.** The Epi2Diff framework suggests that running assessment items through LLMs and examining the reasoning dynamics can predict which items students will find difficult — before those items reach students. Teachers and curriculum designers can use this to pre-calibrate difficulty and identify likely misconceptions.

## Practical Experiment to Try

**The Agentic Shift Self-Audit**

1. **For one day, log every AI interaction as either Chat or Agent.** Chat = single question/answer exchange. Agent = delegating a multi-step task that runs autonomously. At the end of the day, calculate your ratio. If it's above 80% Chat, you're operating in the pre-shift paradigm.

2. **Try one eight-hour agent task.** The OpenAI paper shows 25% of Codex tasks exceed eight hours. Pick a task you'd normally spread across a week (competitive analysis, research synthesis, data cleaning pipeline) and delegate it to an AI agent in one session. Document what the agent got right, what it missed, and where you needed to intervene.

3. **Measure your keystroke-to-acceptance ratio.** For your next three AI interactions, count (roughly) how many keystrokes you type in your prompt vs. how many words the AI produces in its response. A ratio dropping below 1:50 may indicate cognitive surrender — you're accepting more than you're contributing. The countermeasure: for the next interaction, type a draft of your own answer first, then ask the AI to critique it.
