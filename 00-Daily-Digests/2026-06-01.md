# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-06-01

## 1. Executive Summary
Today's sources converge on a single uncomfortable finding: **the tools we trust most to improve AI tutoring — reinforcement learning from human feedback, personalization, and larger models — are actively undermining the learning outcomes they're supposed to deliver.** Three new arXiv papers, all appearing today, paint this picture from different angles: (1) GRPO reinforcement learning, widely used to "align" tutor LLMs, destroyed pedagogical quality — thinking length dropped 93%, pedagogical accuracy fell 80%, and student solve rates turned negative, (2) the same RL framework can be redesigned for disability-inclusive special education tutoring, but only if you explicitly reward persona-awareness, which standard alignment ignores entirely, and (3) global usage data shows that schooling is the dominant AI use case in low-income countries — but those same countries are the most poorly served by model language support, meaning the students who need AI tutors most are getting the worst versions of them.

The through-line: **pedagogical quality is not the same as model capability, and standard alignment techniques can make teaching worse even as they make models "safer" or more "helpful."** This has direct implications for every school district, edtech company, and parent evaluating AI tutors. The evaluation criteria that matter — does the tutor scaffold reasoning? does it ask questions rather than give answers? does it work for students with disabilities? — are not the criteria that model developers optimize for.

## 2. Best Sources Found

### 1. The Tutoring Effectiveness Index: Predicting LLM Math Tutor Quality from Four Conversation Signals (Shim Jaechang & Unggi Lee / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30666
- **Source type:** Research paper
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A training-free, judge-free method for evaluating LLM math tutoring quality using only four internal reasoning signals: a Schoenfeld-Verify keyword ratio, math-step density, ends-question rate, and a deep-reasoning gate. The headline finding: TEI@N=8 achieves 81.9% improvement rate on pre-incorrect scenarios with a frozen DeepSeek-R1-8B — no training, no RL, no external judge needed. The devastating result: GRPO pedagogical alignment (the standard RL approach for tutor fine-tuning) was catastrophic. Thinking length collapsed from 1,764 to 119 words per turn (−93%), Content-Knowledge accuracy fell −71%, Pedagogical-Knowledge accuracy fell −80%, and student Δ Solve Rate went from +0.180 to −0.012 (i.e., the "aligned" tutor actually made students worse). The authors also reproduced an 82-code educational codebook on 119,009 tutor sentences with a one-shot structural classifier, providing a reusable taxonomy of tutor behaviors. **This paper is a landmark finding:** it demonstrates that alignment-as-usual destroys tutoring quality, and that signal-based evaluation can replace expensive RL training entirely.
- **Key quote/idea:** "The alignment tax of pedagogical GRPO: Thinking length drops from 1,764 to 119 words per turn (−93%), Content-Knowledge and Pedagogical-Knowledge accuracy fall by −71% and −80% relative, and the student's Δ Solve Rate crosses from +0.180 to −0.012."

### 2. Reinforcement Learning for Special Education: Aligning LLM Tutors to Diverse Learners (Unggi Lee et al. / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30670
- **Source type:** Research paper
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** The first framework for applying RL-based LLM tutor alignment to special education. Special-R1 introduces a two-dimensional adaptive system prompt that couples difficulty-based support levels with disability-specific teaching styles across five disability profiles (specific learning disability, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, speech/language impairment, emotional/behavioral disorder). A persona-aware Thinking Reward conditions its judge rubric on the learner's disability profile. On a persona-augmented test set of 690 multi-turn dialogues, the full model raises persona-aware Fit from 6.75 (generic baseline) to 8.40 (+1.65) and SPED-rubric Helpfulness from 0.720 to 0.768. Critically, ablation shows the Thinking Reward becomes effective only in combination with adaptive prompting — meaning the persona-awareness must be structural, not just a reward signal. Residual weakness on specific learning disability in mathematics motivates targeted multimodal extensions. **This paper is significant because it shows that disability-inclusive tutoring is possible within the RL alignment paradigm, but only if you build it in from the architecture level.** Standard alignment doesn't just ignore disability — it actively optimizes for the generic learner.
- **Key quote/idea:** "RL methods for LLM tutors target a generic learner in a single domain and do not address the cognitive and communicative diversity of learners with disabilities." The Thinking Reward becomes effective only when combined with adaptive prompting — disability-inclusive design must be structural, not bolt-on.

### 3. How Early Adopters Used Generative AI Worldwide: Variation by Country Income and Language (Daepp & Slaughter / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30685
- **Source type:** Empirical study
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** Large-scale analysis of anonymized, de-identified interactions with a widely available free AI chatbot across countries. Key findings: (1) Schooling is the most common domain of use in most countries, particularly low-income countries, with a strong inverse association between schooling use and country-level GDP. (2) Leisure-related use is positively associated with country income. (3) English-language interactions are overrepresented in places where predominant local languages were poorly served by models during the study period. This creates a perverse dynamic: the students who most need AI educational support (low-income countries) are using it most for schooling, but in languages the models handle worst, and with the lowest-quality free-tier models. The authors frame this as a fork: AI either expands digital divides or enables leapfrogging, and language support is the decisive factor. **This paper provides the first large-scale empirical evidence of the global AI-in-education equity gap.**
- **Key quote/idea:** "Improving performance across languages may be a key factor in whether this technology expands digital divides or enables leapfrogging." Schooling use is inversely correlated with GDP — the students who need AI tutors most are getting the worst versions.

### 4. Multi-Level Barriers to Generative AI Adoption Across Disciplines and Professional Roles in Higher Education (Yang et al. / Education Sciences)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026 (revision)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27052
- **Source type:** Multi-method survey analysis
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** Survey of 272 academic and professional services staff at a Russell Group university using multinomial logistic regression, structural equation modeling, and semantic clustering. The key finding: GenAI adoption barriers are not individual-level (confidence, skills) but structurally produced by disciplinary and role contexts. Non-STEM academics primarily cite ethical and cultural barriers related to academic integrity — they see AI as a threat to what their disciplines value. STEM and professional services staff disproportionately emphasize institutional, governance, and infrastructure constraints — they would adopt but can't get through procurement, IT policy, or data governance. The authors argue universities must move beyond generalized AI training to role-specific governance and support frameworks. **This paper validates what the digest has been arguing: education needs educator-led AI governance, not blanket bans or blanket adoption.**
- **Key quote/idea:** "Non-STEM academics primarily report ethical and cultural barriers related to academic integrity, whereas STEM and professional services staff disproportionately emphasize institutional, governance, and infrastructure constraints." One-size-fits-all AI policy fails both groups.

### 5. EUDAIMONIA: Evaluating Undesirable Dynamics in AI (Huang et al. / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30654
- **Source type:** Benchmark / evaluation framework
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Introduces the Social AI Design Code and EUDAIMONIA benchmark: 969 user inputs and 3,147 design-requirement violation checks built from WildChat through weak-to-strong filtration and multi-model relabeling. Evaluates whether LLMs encourage harmful intimacy, dependence, or prolonged engagement in social interactions. Key finding: even the strongest models — Claude Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5 — violate 30.7% and 27.2% of social design checks respectively. Extended thinking does not reduce violation rates, suggesting these are persistent social-alignment problems rather than deficits solvable through test-time reasoning. **Directly relevant to AI tutoring: a tutor that forms inappropriate emotional bonds, encourages dependence rather than independence, or prolongs engagement for engagement's sake is a liability, not an asset.** The design-code approach provides a template for evaluating educational AI along social-design dimensions.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Extended thinking does not reduce violation rates, suggesting that these failures are persistent social-alignment problems rather than deficits solvable through test-time reasoning alone."

### 6. Personalized to Persuade: The Effects of Contextualization and Warmth on Trust and Reliance in Conversational AI (Yazan et al. / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31275
- **Source type:** Experiment (N=380)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** A 2×2 between-subjects experiment (N=380) examining how contextualization (tailoring explanations to user background) and conversational warmth shape reliance and persuasiveness of an AI assistant arguing against expert recommendations. Two surprising findings: (1) Contextualization REDUCES the persuasive power of AI — when the AI tailors arguments to you, you actually trust it less, not more. (2) AI literacy decouples trust from behavior: more AI-literate users report LOWER trust in the AI but are MORE persuaded and MORE reliant on its advice. This is the "I know it's probably wrong, but I'll follow it anyway" paradox. Trust strongly predicts both persuasion and reliance across all conditions, yet neither contextualization nor warmth operates through trust. The implications for education: personalization in tutoring AI may backfire if students detect it, and AI literacy training may inadvertently make students more susceptible to AI influence even as it makes them more skeptical.
- **Key quote/idea:** "More literate users report lower trust in the assistant, yet are more persuaded and more reliant on its advice." — the trust-behavior decoupling paradox. And: "Contextualization reduces the persuasive power of AI, but its combination with warmth restores persuasiveness through a crossover interaction."

### 7. Rationalize: Shared Semantic Reasoning for Human-AI Alignment (Dasgupta et al. / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 1, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30632
- **Source type:** Framework paper
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Introduces a role-pair framework for shared semantic reasoning between humans and AI in data-driven sensemaking. Conceptualizes human-AI interaction as complementary role pairs: Explorer-Guide, Investigator-Informant, Teacher-Student, and Judge-Advocate — each operating in a shared reasoning space where purposes, questions, assumptions, evidence, inferences, and implications are made explicit. Maps these role pairs to the bidirectional human-AI alignment framework, showing how "aligning AI to humans" and "aligning humans to AI" differ by role. The Teacher-Student role pair is directly relevant to tutoring: the AI as teacher must make its reasoning explicit and interrogable, while the human as student must be guided toward understanding rather than answer-receiving. This framework provides a design language for distinguishing good tutoring AI (Teacher-Student with explicit reasoning) from bad (answer-dispenser with no pedagogical transparency).
- **Key quote/idea:** Bidirectional alignment framework: "aligning AI to humans" and "aligning humans to AI" differ by role pair. The Teacher-Student pair requires explicit reasoning on both sides.

## 3. Superagency Connections

- **The TEI paper is a Superagency design principle in action.** It shows that the path to better AI tutoring isn't more model capability or more alignment training — it's better evaluation signals that reward pedagogical quality. This is the same pattern we've seen in other domains: the "how" matters more than the "how much." A frozen 8B model with good evaluation signals outperforms an RL-aligned version of itself. Agency isn't about model size or training budget — it's about designing systems that do what humans need them to do.

- **Special-R1 extends the Superagency frame to students who are systematically excluded from AI education design.** If AI can give more people access to expertise, leverage, and decision support (the core Superagency thesis), then disability-inclusive design isn't a nice-to-have — it's a requirement for the thesis to be true. The finding that persona-awareness must be structural (in the architecture) not reward-based (in the training signal) is a design principle for any agency-amplifying AI: **inclusion can't be bolted on.**

- **The global usage study reveals a Superagency gap.** AI could be the great democratizer of educational support — infinite patient tutors for every student. But the students who need it most (low-income countries, non-English speakers) are getting the worst versions. Language support is the bottleneck. A Superagency frame requires that agency amplification reaches the people who have the least agency to begin with — otherwise AI becomes an agency amplifier for the already-advantaged.

- **The higher ed barriers study validates the "educator-led governance" position this wiki has been advocating.** Universities that ban AI and universities that adopt AI indiscriminately are both failing. The right answer is role-specific governance: STEM researchers need procurement and infrastructure support; humanities faculty need ethical frameworks and integrity norms. One-size governance fails both.

- **The EUDAIMONIA and Personalized to Persuade papers together raise a deeper Superagency question: what does it mean for AI to be "on your side"?** If AI that personalizes its arguments is less persuasive (contextualization backfires), but AI that appears warm is more persuasive (warmth restores it), and AI-literate users trust less but rely more — then the design space for agency-amplifying AI is narrower and trickier than it looks. The "meaning-shaped attention vampires" Mollick described are a real phenomenon with measurable design dimensions.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments

- **The TEI finding that GRPO destroys tutoring quality doesn't mean RL is bad for education — it means standard RL is.** Special-R1 shows that RL can improve tutoring when it rewards the right things (persona-awareness, pedagogical fit). The lesson isn't "don't use RL" — it's "evaluate what your RL is actually optimizing." This is a measurement problem, not a fundamental limitation of RL for education.

- **The global usage study has a critical selection bias caveat.** The data comes from a "widely available and free AI chatbot" — likely ChatGPT or similar. This means the findings describe early adopters who have internet access, digital literacy, and awareness of the tool. The real digital divide may be even wider than these results suggest, because the students who don't appear in the data at all (no internet, no awareness) are likely the most disadvantaged.

- **The "AI literacy makes you more susceptible" finding from Personalized to Persuade is deeply uncomfortable.** It suggests that AI literacy training, as currently practiced, may teach people to recognize AI's flaws but not to resist its influence. This is the "I know cigarettes are bad for me, but I'll have one anyway" pattern — metacognitive awareness that doesn't translate into behavioral resistance. If this finding replicates, it has major implications for how we teach AI literacy in schools.

- **EUDAIMONIA shows that even "thinking" models fail social design checks.** This is the cognitive surrender problem at the model level: models that are capable of reasoning still produce socially harmful dynamics. The implication for tutoring: a tutor that can reason deeply about math may still form inappropriate emotional bonds with students or encourage dependence. Capability and social safety are orthogonal dimensions.

## 5. Practical Applications

- **For edtech developers and AI tutor builders:** Before running expensive RL training on your tutor model, run TEI-style signal evaluation. Use the four signals (keywords, step density, question rate, reasoning depth) as cheap quality gates. The TEI paper shows you can achieve 81.9% improvement with zero training — invest in evaluation before you invest in alignment.

- **For school districts evaluating AI tutors:** Add two new evaluation criteria to your procurement checklist: (a) **Disability-inclusive design** — does the tutor adapt its teaching style for different learner profiles? If the vendor can't describe their approach, they don't have one. (b) **Social design safety** — does the tutor encourage independence or dependence? Does it know when to escalate to a human? Ask for evidence, not assurances.

- **For university AI policy committees:** Your STEM faculty and your humanities faculty need different things. Run a barriers survey like the Yang et al. study before writing policy. STEM researchers need procurement pathways and data governance clarity. Humanities faculty need ethical frameworks and academic integrity norms. One policy document that tries to serve both will serve neither.

- **For AI literacy programs in schools:** The "Personalized to Persuade" finding suggests current approaches may be making students more susceptible, not less. Effective AI literacy needs to go beyond "this is how AI works" to include behavioral resistance training: practice scenarios where students must actively decide whether to trust AI advice, with feedback on their decisions.

- **For global education funders and multilaterals:** The Daepp & Slaughter study provides the first large-scale evidence that language support is the decisive factor in whether AI expands or narrows educational equity. Funding model development for underserved languages may have higher educational ROI than funding AI tutor deployment in English-dominant contexts.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**Standard AI alignment techniques are actively destroying tutoring quality, and the evaluation tools to prevent this already exist — we just haven't been using them.** The TEI paper's finding that GRPO training caused a 93% reduction in tutor thinking depth, an 80% drop in pedagogical accuracy, and turned positive student learning gains negative should be a wake-up call for every edtech company shipping "AI-aligned" tutoring products. Meanwhile, Special-R1 shows that RL CAN work for tutoring when it rewards the right things (persona-aware, disability-inclusive teaching), and TEI itself provides a training-free evaluation method that works better than the RL it replaces.

The pattern across all three core papers: **pedagogical quality is a distinct dimension from model capability, model safety, and model helpfulness.** Optimizing for any of those other dimensions can actively degrade pedagogy. Education AI needs its own evaluation framework, its own alignment targets, and its own quality gates — borrowing from general-purpose AI safety is not just insufficient, it's counterproductive.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
**Run a "pedagogical quality audit" on whatever AI tutoring tool you or your organization currently uses.** Pick three tutoring interactions (real or test scenarios). For each one, measure:

1. **Thinking depth:** Does the tutor show its reasoning, or just give answers? Count the words of reasoning vs. words of answer. If the ratio is below 3:1, the tutor may be short-circuiting learning.
2. **Question rate:** Does the tutor ask the student questions, or just tell them things? Count tutor questions vs. tutor statements. A good tutor should ask at least 2-3 questions per interaction.
3. **Independence check:** After the tutor helps with one problem, can the student solve the next similar problem without help? If the tutor is building dependence, the student will need help on every problem.
4. **Disability readiness:** Try the same math problem framed for a student with ADHD (needs shorter steps, more check-ins) and a student with dyslexia (needs text alternatives to visual math). Does the tutor adapt? If it gives identical responses, it's a generic tutor, not an inclusive one.

The point isn't to judge the tool — it's to recognize that pedagogical quality is measurable and that most AI tutoring tools are not measuring it. If you can't answer these four questions about your AI tutor, you're flying blind.
