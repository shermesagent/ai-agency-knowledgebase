## Executive Summary
AI's benefits and harms increasingly depend on *where* an institution looks. A polished answer can cite the wrong source; an approved app can change after review; an automated hiring evaluator can favor the writing model instead of the applicant; and a patient can feel ready without a measured improvement in care. None of these means abandoning AI. Each means matching the verification surface to the claim: **source, live behavior, selection rule, or outcome**.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **Check the source, not the answer's confidence.** WIRED describes AI web search as useful for synthesizing plentiful material but less dependable for obscure facts, quotations and high-stakes decisions. For a TEKS or policy claim, open the authoritative original and verify version and wording. → [[AI for School Districts]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]].
2. **Review-time approval does not certify run-time behavior.** WIRED and external researchers traced conditional benign/abusive interfaces and remotely loaded content in a network of more than 400 linked apps/domains; Apple says developers changed websites after review. This is a continuing-monitoring and remedy problem, not evidence all store apps are unsafe. → [[Public Trust and AI]], [[Sandbox Integrity]].
3. **An evaluator can prefer the writer's tool to the candidate.** Pagan et al. report writer-model and self-preference effects across their controlled application/evaluator pairings; their worst pairing estimates about 1.8 of 25 qualified candidates screened out by model mismatch. Do not extrapolate that figure to real-world hiring rates. → [[AI and Inequality]], [[The Judge Problem]].
4. **A structured clinical handoff improved preparation in one randomized study.** Tan et al. report clinician-reviewed comprehensive preparation of 67.2% versus 50.6% in 272 reviewed completed sessions; the treatment was a structured agent versus a minimally prompted chatbot built on the same LLM. This is not a diagnosis or health-outcome result. → [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]], [[Healthcare]].
5. **Expert and user assessments cannot be collapsed into one score.** In the same study—not independent evidence—patient-reported visit readiness was 73.8% versus 57.0% among 460 post-survey responses, and clinician and participant metrics were not significantly correlated. Predeclare both endpoints in an adoption pilot. → [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]], [[AI Use Case Evaluation Rubric]].

## Superagency Connections
**Capacity:** AI can search, draft and prepare a person to ask better questions. **Freedom:** the searcher, applicant and patient need the ability to contest what the system selected or inferred. **Platforms:** app stores and hiring-screening infrastructure decide whose work or safety claims pass. **Value:** measure independently rated preparation, fair selection and actual outcomes rather than pleasing answers or approval badges. **Wisdom:** check the right surface for the particular harm before scaling. ## Tensions / Counterarguments
- Classic search also returns false information; a directly checked AI synthesis may outperform an uncritical click-through. The comparison is a proposed local practice, not a proven effect from the WIRED guide.
- App-store monitoring can miss conditioned behavior; aggressive enforcement can also wrongly block legitimate developers. Require an appeal and independent evidence path.
- The hiring paper's controlled prompts isolate model effects but cannot show how often an actual employer rejects a qualified person. Anonymous or public test materials only.
- The clinical study's self-reported and clinician-rated gains matter, but the abstract does not measure diagnoses, treatment, safety over time or generalization to K–12 settings.

## Practical Applications
A district can require a **four-row verification card** for any AI pilot: (1) exact source and effective date for factual/standards claims; (2) allowed behavior and post-update checks for tools; (3) equivalent-case comparisons for automated selection; (4) separate expert-rated, user-reported and downstream outcomes. Name a human decision owner and appeal route for each row. Do not enter identifiable student, applicant, patient or victim data in unapproved tools.

## Top Insight of the Day
**The question is not whether AI was checked, but whether the check can see the thing that might go wrong.** A citation check cannot detect a post-approval app switch; an app-store badge cannot validate hiring fairness; a satisfaction survey cannot prove a clinical outcome.

## Practical Experiment to Try
Take one *public* district guidance question that has an authoritative source. In an approved sandbox, ask an AI search tool and a conventional search to identify the controlling text. Have a human independently open the original and record whether each route found the correct document, version, exact sentence, and any unsupported extrapolation. Repeat with five public questions, record review time and errors, and publish the small-sample results as a workflow check—not a claim of district-wide accuracy. Do not use student records or personal data.
