## Executive Summary

The weekend's most important AI finding came from a Stanford study that wasn't designed to find it. Researchers wanted to test whether human guides could improve student engagement with AI tutoring platforms. They couldn't answer that question because they hit a more fundamental one: **students wouldn't use the AI tutors at all.** Weekly usage averaged 2-5 minutes. Human support increased it to 5-9 minutes — still negligible. The capability existed (the platform worked, the AI could tutor), but the willingness to engage didn't.

This finding is the anchor for a broader pattern visible across this weekend's signals:

1. **Stanford Tutoring Engagement Study (June 17)** — 2-5 min/week AI tutor usage; "a key finding that we weren't even meaning to test." The AI tutoring promise collides with the engagement reality: access ≠ adoption.

2. **ChatGPT Loses Majority Market Share (June 16-18 data)** — Down to 46.4%, first time below 50%. Gemini at 23.2%. AI Insiders: "The Monopoly Cracks." Users are redistributing engagement across platforms — competition as engagement diversity.

3. **GPT-5.6 Imminent (June 2026)** — OpenAI's next model, described by Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki as a "meaningful improvement" over GPT-5.5. The Information reports a June launch. Polymarket: 89% for June 30. The capability frontier races ahead while adoption lags.

4. **Two Governance Signals:** (a) CDT's Kevin Bankston publishes "Don't Let Perfect be the Enemy of Portable" (June 18) — arguing that AI governance rules must be portable and practical, not perfect and unusable. The governance equivalent of the engagement gap: perfect rules nobody follows are worse than good-enough rules people use. (b) The Fable 5 ban reaches day 10 — capability exists, governance has severed the engagement pipeline. An engagement gap at institutional scale.

The synthesis: **The Diffusion Layer** (yesterday's theme) describes the infrastructure between capability and benefit. **The Engagement Gap** describes the human dimension of that same problem — the willingness, motivation, and institutional permission to actually use what's available. You can build the scanner, deploy the agent stack, open the API — and still nobody shows up.

## Best Sources Found

### 1. AI Tutoring Engagement Gap: Students Won't Use AI Tutors (Stanford SCALE / Chalkbeat / The 74 Million / K-12 Dive, June 17, 2026)
**Type:** Research study + Journalism | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 5/5

The most important AI-in-education finding of 2026 may be the one the researchers weren't looking for. Stanford SCALE researchers partnered with two school districts to test whether human guides could improve student engagement with AI tutoring platforms. The study hit a wall before it could answer that question: **students left alone with the AI tutor spent just 2-5 minutes per week interacting with it.** Even with human tutor support, usage increased by only 1-4 minutes per week — to roughly 5-9 minutes total.

Key findings from the study and coverage:

- **Baseline engagement was shockingly low:** "Most students wouldn't use it" — Chalkbeat headline. "A key finding that we weren't even meaning to test" — the researchers weren't studying baseline engagement; they had to because it was so low it blocked their actual research question.
- **Human support barely moved the needle:** Working with human tutors increased average weekly platform usage by only 1-4 minutes. The 71-80% engagement improvement statistic sounds impressive but is from a nearly-zero baseline.
- **The capability was there:** The AI platform functioned. It could tutor. The problem wasn't technical — it was motivational, behavioral, and structural.
- **Multi-outlet coverage:** The finding was covered by Chalkbeat, The 74 Million ("Study: Giving Kids Access to AI Tutors Doesn't Mean They'll Use Them"), and K-12 Dive ("AI tutor access alone doesn't equate to student gains").

**Superagency connection:** This is the most direct challenge yet to the AI tutoring thesis — not that AI can't tutor (it can), but that giving people AI tools doesn't mean they'll use them. Access ≠ agency. The ParaTutor finding from Wednesday (June 17 digest: "generic LLM assistance reduces parent's role") was about role erosion *when AI is used.* This finding is more fundamental: most students won't use AI tutors *at all.* The engagement gap is the first barrier to AI-amplified human agency — before any question of whether the AI is good enough, before any question of whether it preserves the human role. Does anyone actually want to use it?

Key elements:

- **Timing:** The drop below 50% was reported June 16-18, based on May usage data
- **Competitive dynamics:** Claude and Gemini are growing while ChatGPT's share erodes — not because ChatGPT usage is declining, but because competitors are growing faster

**Superagency connection:** Market competition in AI assistants is a democratization signal. When one company controls the default AI interface, agency is concentrated. When users actively choose between competing platforms — and increasingly choose non-ChatGPT options — the engagement is distributed. The engagement gap narrows when there's something to engage *with* that matches your needs and preferences. Market diversity = engagement diversity.

### 4. CDT: "Don't Let Perfect be the Enemy of Portable" (Kevin Bankston, Center for Democracy & Technology, June 18, 2026)
**Type:** Policy essay / Analysis | **Relevance:** 4/5 | **Reliability:** 5/5

Kevin Bankston of CDT's AI Governance Lab argues that AI governance must prioritize portability and practicality over theoretical perfection. The essay explicitly references GDPR Article 20 (data portability rights) as a model: a regulation that works because it's implementable and portable, not because it's comprehensive.

Key elements:
- **The thesis:** Perfect AI regulation that can't be implemented across jurisdictions is worse than good-enough regulation that's portable and practical
- **GDPR Article 20 as precedent:** Data portability works across EU member states because it's specific, implementable, and doesn't require a unified global framework to function
- **AI governance application:** AI governance needs portable rules — model evaluation standards, transparency requirements, agent behavior specifications — that work across jurisdictions rather than perfect rules that only work in one
- **Portability as engagement:** Governance that travels is governance that gets used. Rules that require every jurisdiction to build its own perfect framework create an engagement gap between governed and ungoverned AI

**Superagency connection:** This is the governance dimension of the engagement gap. The Fable 5 ban demonstrates what happens when governance is centralized and non-portable — one jurisdiction's decision severs the engagement pipeline globally. Bankston's argument is that portable, practical governance is agency-preserving governance: it lets people engage with AI under known, consistent rules regardless of where they are.

## Superagency Connections

The Engagement Gap bridges multiple knowledgebase themes:

1. **AI Tutors → AI Adoption:** The Stanford finding is devastating for the proposition that "if we build it, they will come." The AI tutoring platform was built. They didn't come. Access ≠ engagement ≠ benefit. The Superagency thesis needs not just AI tools that work, but ecosystems that make people want to use them — motivation, incentives, social norms, institutional permission.

2. **Democratization of Expertise:** ChatGPT losing its majority market share is democratization in practice — not because any single alternative is better, but because choice itself distributes agency. When users actively select between platforms rather than defaulting to one, they're exercising the kind of informed engagement the Superagency thesis depends on.

3. **Governance as Engagement Infrastructure:** The CDT portability argument and the Fable 5 ban are two sides of the same coin. Portable governance preserves engagement across jurisdictions. Non-portable governance (export controls with no criteria, no timeline) severs it. The engagement gap has a governance dimension: rules that block AI access without due process create an engagement gap by design.

4. **Cognitive Surrender meets Engagement:** The cognitive surrender literature has documented what happens when people DO engage with AI (attentional atrophy, preference inversion, emotional dependence). The engagement gap finding adds the inverse question: what happens when people WON'T engage with AI at all? Both are forms of agency loss — surrender through too much engagement, and surrender through none.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

1. **The tutoring engagement finding may be age-specific.** The Stanford study involved elementary school students. Adults using AI tutors for professional upskilling may show very different engagement patterns. Don't overgeneralize "students won't use AI tutors" to "nobody will use AI tutors."

2. **ChatGPT "losing majority" is relative, not absolute.** ChatGPT usage is almost certainly still growing — just slower than competitors. This is market expansion, not market contraction. The engagement gap narrative shouldn't be confused with "AI adoption is slowing" — it's not.

3. **GPT-5.6 being "imminent" and "meaningful" are both vague.** "Meaningful improvement" is the minimum a chief scientist can say about a new model. The actual capability jump from GPT-5.5 to 5.6 may be modest. The engagement gap argument doesn't depend on 5.6 being revolutionary — it depends on the structural fact that capability advances faster than adoption.

4. **The CDT portability argument is narrowly about data rights.** The essay's primary reference is GDPR Article 20 — a data portability provision, not a comprehensive AI governance framework. Extending it to AI agent governance is a useful analogy but may not map perfectly. Portable evaluation standards are harder than portable data.

## Practical Applications

1. **The "Engagement Audit" for AI deployment.** Before deploying any AI tool in your organization, ask: who will actually use this, and why? The Stanford finding suggests that even when the AI works, engagement is not automatic. Map the engagement barriers: motivation (why would they use it?), friction (how many steps to start?), social permission (is it sanctioned or stigmatized?), and institutional alignment (does using it align with how performance is evaluated?).

2. **Track the GPT-5.6 launch.** If GPT-5.6 releases in June 2026 (~9 days from now), compare its capabilities to Fable 5/Mythos 5. The Fable ban creates a natural experiment: what happens when the capability frontier is split — one company's best model is blocked by export controls while another's advances freely?

3. **Apply the portability test to your AI governance.** Bankston's argument: for any AI governance rule you're considering, ask "does this work outside our organization?" If the answer is no, the rule creates an engagement gap — it governs your AI use but doesn't travel to partners, vendors, or other jurisdictions. Portable governance scales; non-portable governance isolates.

## Top Insight of the Day

**The Engagement Gap is the Diffusion Layer's human face.** Yesterday's theme — the Diffusion Layer — described the infrastructure between AI capability and human benefit in technical terms: deployment pipelines, regulatory pathways, agent stacks. Today's finding — that Stanford researchers couldn't get students to use AI tutors for more than 5 minutes a week — reveals the human dimension of the same problem. You can build the scanner, deploy the agent stack, open the API, clear the regulatory pathway — and still, the humans don't show up. The Superagency thesis has always been about what humans do with AI, not what AI can do. The engagement gap is the empirical finding that the "what humans do" part is not automatic — it's the hardest part, and it's the part we're least equipped to measure, design for, or fix. Capability without engagement is a locked door. The key is not better AI. The key is understanding why people don't turn the handle.

## Practical Experiment to Try

**The "Five-Minute Test."** Pick an AI tool you theoretically have access to but rarely use. (Examples: AI code review, AI meeting summarizer, AI writing assistant, AI research agent.) Set a timer for five minutes — the Stanford baseline. Open the tool and use it for something real. After five minutes, stop and write down: (1) Did you accomplish anything useful? (2) If you didn't, what stopped you — was the tool bad, or was the barrier something else (habit, friction, forgetting it exists, not knowing what to ask it)? (3) If you did accomplish something useful, would you do this again tomorrow? The Stanford finding suggests that most people's answer to (3) will be "probably not" — even if (1) was "yes." The engagement gap is personal before it's structural. Understand your own five-minute threshold before you try to close anyone else's.
