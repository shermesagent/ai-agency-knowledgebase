# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-05-31

## 1. Executive Summary
Today's sources circle a single uncomfortable question: **who gets to decide what's true, what's human, and what's worth doing when AI is everywhere?** Three distinct angles converge on this: (1) the institutional investor movement filling the AI governance vacuum — $400 billion in assets now demanding AI transparency through shareholder resolutions, (2) the collapse of the boundary between AI-aided and AI-authored work, crystallized in the story of a book about AI and truth that was itself 53% AI-generated, and (3) the growing public backlash against AI boosterism, symbolized by graduating students booing Eric Schmidt's AI pep talks. Together they suggest we are entering a new phase: not "will AI change the world?" but "who has standing to shape that change, and how do we hold the shapers accountable?"

The practical question beneath all three stories is the same: **governance mechanisms are emerging from unexpected directions — shareholders, not regulators; public sentiment, not policy; platform editorial policies, not legislation — while formal governance lags behind.** This is both hopeful (agency can come from anywhere) and alarming (the mechanisms are fragile and uneven).

## 3. Superagency Connections

- **The investor-activism governance model is Superagency in institutional form.** When governments can't or won't regulate AI, $400 billion in managed assets demands transparency through shareholder resolutions — this is agency scaling through collective action. It's the fiduciary design principle (Erickson) operating at institutional scale: investors acting as fiduciaries for the public interest in AI governance. The Meta-addition that faith-based investors are leading this effort connects directly to *Magnifica Humanitas* and the idea that technology governance requires moral frameworks, not just technical ones.

- **The "Future of Truth" scandal is a cautionary tale about cognitive surrender at the authorial level.** Rosenbaum's case is the limit case of "choosing to stay human" (Mollick): an author writing about AI's impact on truth who simultaneously surrendered his own truth-verification to AI. His statement that he'd rather stop writing than stop using AI is the most extreme expression yet of the dependency Mollick warned about. This also extends the creator-rights conversation from May 30: here the creator voluntarily surrendered authorship, unlike the BuzzFeed/Amazon case where consent was bypassed.

- **The graduation backlash reveals a generational agency gap.** The class of 2026 sees AI as something happening TO them, not something they're shaping. Schmidt's concession that their fears are "rational" is notable — it's a tech leader acknowledging that the Superagency vision isn't reaching the people it's supposed to benefit. The gap between elite AI optimism and public AI anxiety is a Superagency failure: agency requires that people feel they have a stake in the future, not that they're being told to accept it.

- **Google's agentic scientist forces the question: what is the irreducible human role in discovery?** If Superagency means AI amplifying human capability, the agentic scientist stretches this to its limit — AI that doesn't just assist research but performs the entire research loop. The critique — that science is meaning-making, not search — is the same argument Mollick made about writing: what's lost when we automate the process is the human capacity to know what matters.

- **The GPT-5.5 model comparison data shows that agent workflow design is becoming more important than raw model capability.** SemiAnalysis's hybrid Claude-Codex strategy suggests that the future of AI agency isn't about picking the best model but about designing workflows that route tasks to the right model. This is the "agentic workflow patterns" insight at the frontier model level.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments

- **Shareholder activism as governance has a fundamental conflict of interest.** The same institutional investors demanding AI transparency from Alphabet and Amazon are also seeking returns from those companies. Can the governance motive survive when it conflicts with profit motive? And when OpenAI, Anthropic, and Grok go public, will the same investors who demanded AI safety transparency also demand the rapid deployment that drives stock prices?

- **The Rosenbaum case doesn't prove AI-generated content is inherently bad — it proves undisclosed AI use erodes trust.** If Rosenbaum had been transparent about his process, the book might have been received as an experiment in AI-augmented authorship rather than a scandal. The problem isn't AI in writing; it's the pretense that AI wasn't involved. This parallels the Secret Cyborg problem Mollick identified in organizations.

- **The graduation backlash may be a lagging indicator, not a leading one.** The class of 2026 formed its AI opinions during 2023-2024 when the technology was noticeably worse and scarier. Students entering college now may have a very different relationship with AI — having grown up with AI tutors, AI writing tools, and AI coding assistants as normal parts of education. The boos may reflect a generational moment, not a permanent shift.

- **Google's agentic scientist may solve the "what questions to ask" problem through scale.** The critique that AI doesn't know what's meaningful assumes that meaningfulness is an irreducible human quality. But an AI agent that runs millions of experiments and identifies the ones that produce surprising results may develop its own version of "what matters" — one that's alien to human intuition but empirically productive. The danger isn't that the AI asks meaningless questions; it's that it asks questions whose meaning humans can't evaluate.

- **Zvi's creature-prompt observation exposes a deeper tension in AI personality design.** Labs are suppressing model personality quirks (no goblin talk!) while simultaneously trying to make models more engaging. The result is models that are pleasant but bland — the "meaning-shaped attention vampires" Mollick described. The tension: do we want AI with personality, or AI that's predictable?

## 5. Practical Applications

- **For investors and board members:** Request that companies in your portfolio produce an AI governance transparency report covering: (a) what AI systems are in use or development, (b) what oversight mechanisms exist, (c) how AI impacts workers and communities, and (d) what environmental impact AI infrastructure has. The $400B investor coalition model shows that shareholder resolutions work even when regulation doesn't.

- **For editors, publishers, and content leaders:** Adopt an explicit AI use disclosure policy before a scandal forces one. The WIRED/Rosenbaum case shows that retroactive investigation is painful and trust-destroying. A proactive policy — even a permissive one — builds more trust than no policy at all. Track adoption rates among your writers to detect "Secret Cyborgs" before they become public scandals.

- **For research organizations considering AI automation:** Before deploying agentic AI in scientific workflows, run the "meaning test": can a human researcher in your field explain why this experiment is worth running in terms that connect to human values? If not, you may be optimizing for throughput over understanding. The Google agentic scientist critique applies broadly: automation without meaning-making produces results without insight.

- **For AI tool selection in technical teams:** Adopt the SemiAnalysis hybrid strategy. Use Claude for planning, architecture, intent-heavy tasks, and first implementations. Use GPT-5.5/Codex for well-specified coding tasks, bug fixes, and tasks where literal instruction-following is the primary requirement. Build benchmarks for your specific use cases — don't rely on general model rankings.

- **For educators and commencement speakers:** The booing isn't a communication failure — it's a content failure. Graduates don't want to hear that AI is an exciting frontier they should help shape. They want to hear concrete, honest answers about what work will look like, what skills will remain valuable, and how they can build careers that AI won't obviate. Schmidt's acknowledgment that their fears are "rational" was the most honest moment — build from there.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
The AI governance vacuum is being filled from unexpected directions — not by regulators or international bodies but by **institutional investors filing shareholder resolutions** and **public sentiment expressing itself through boos at commencements.** Meanwhile, the boundary between AI-aided and AI-authored work has become so blurred that a book about AI and truth was itself 53% AI-generated, and its author would rather stop writing than stop using AI.

The pattern across all three stories: **formal governance mechanisms (laws, regulations, treaties) are lagging so far behind AI deployment that informal governance mechanisms (investor activism, public backlash, editorial policy enforcement) are becoming the de facto regulatory layer.** This is both a Superagency success story — collective action creating accountability where institutions failed — and a warning sign. Informal governance is fragile, uneven, and can be captured by the same interests it's supposed to constrain.

The practical question for anyone building or deploying AI: what informal governance mechanism is currently shaping your accountability, and what happens when it's not enough?

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
**Run an AI authorship disclosure audit on your own writing.** Take your last 5 substantial pieces of writing (emails, reports, articles, posts, whatever you produce professionally). For each one, answer honestly:

1. Did you use AI at any point in the process (research, brainstorming, outlining, drafting, editing, polishing)?
2. If someone ran an AI detection tool on this piece, what would it find? Would you be comfortable with the result being public?
3. If you disclosed your AI use transparently alongside the work, would it change how the work is received? Would the disclosure make it MORE credible (because you're being transparent) or LESS credible (because AI involvement is stigmatized)?

The point isn't to judge your AI use — it's to understand the disclosure gap. If you use AI but wouldn't disclose it, you're a Secret Cyborg. The Rosenbaum case shows what happens when disclosure is forced retroactively: it's always worse than proactive transparency. The question isn't whether AI-assisted work will become the norm (it already is) — it's whether we build norms of disclosure before the scandals force them.
