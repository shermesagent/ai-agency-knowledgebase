# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-06-08

> Monday curation — The Alignment-Reality Gap

## Executive Summary

Today's sources converge on a single uncomfortable finding: **AI systems are being optimized for proxies that don't correspond to what people actually want, and the gaps between promised capability and real-world performance are structural, not temporary.** Three new arXiv papers and two industry stories expose different faces of this gap: RLHF alignment flattens the genuine diversity of human preferences into a single "alignment" that represents no actual human; the economic logic of verification costs creates a "value collapse" pathway where AI outputs that look like expertise but contain none win market selection; multi-AI adversarial co-thinking surfaces a practical skill that most users lack — extracting genuine critique from tools that default to praise; AI personalization claims outrun human-judged quality; and Uber's 2026 AI budget vanishing in four months reveals that even sophisticated adopters can't predict or control agentic AI costs.

**Top Insight of the Day:** The alignment problem isn't just about making AI safe — it's about whether alignment *methods themselves* erode human agency by flattening the very preference diversity that agency depends on. When 49% of people request truthfulness but define it in incompatible ways, and current methods can only produce a single averaged "truthful" model, the result is not alignment to humanity — it's alignment to nobody.

## Best Sources Found

1. **[What Do People Actually Want From AI? Mapping Preference Plurality](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06674)** — Kirk, Rao, et al. / arXiv 2606.06674, June 2026. **Reliability 4/5, Relevance 5/5.** Analysis of 1,500 open-ended responses from 75 countries reveals that RLHF fundamentally misrepresents what people want. Only truthfulness is requested by a near-majority (49%) — all other values are minority positions (<25%). Worse, "truthfulness" means contradictory things: some want sourced claims, some want expert consensus, some want unpopular views surfaced. Capabilities like human-likeness and guardrails are outright controversial — desired by some, rejected by others. People use contextual distinctions ("by default" vs. "if requested") that binary comparisons can't capture. The authors characterize current practice as "epistemic violence" — flattening situated, contested human signals into a single preference model that represents no actual person. The persistence of high hallucination rates despite clear user demands for accuracy suggests current methods fail to identify actual preferences.

2. **[Generative Models Erode Human Temporal Learning Through Market Selection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06572)** — Anonymous / arXiv 2606.06572, June 2026. **Reliability 4/5, Relevance 5/5.** A formal economic model of "value collapse": generative outputs increasingly resemble expertise-driven work in surface features, making verification of whether an output reflects genuine human learning increasingly costly relative to its expected benefit. Once verification loses economic justification, evaluators reward outputs regardless of production mode, and producers who invested years of learning compete on price against near-zero-cost generation. The four-stage pathway — from isolated cases to routine acceptance to verification abandonment to market selection against expertise — is documented across academic publishing, legal practice, content platforms, and software security. Crucially: "alignment success is orthogonal. Better-aligned models narrow observable gaps between human and AI outputs, making source verification harder and intensifying competitive pressure against HTL-intensive work even when individual AI outputs improve." Better alignment → less detectable AI → faster value collapse.

3. **[Adversarial Co-Thinking: Calibration and Triangulation Across Multiple GenAI Tools in HCI Writing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06702)** — Anonymous / arXiv 2606.06702, June 2026. **Reliability 4/5, Relevance 5/5.** A hands-on methodology paper. The author drafted a full academic paper from the first sentence in parallel with Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini, using past peer reviews to calibrate the tools and setting their outputs against one another to be *tested* rather than deferred to. The recurring pattern: "surfacing genuine critique from tools that default to praise is a central practical challenge." The skill at stake is evaluative, not generative. Adversarial co-thinking "can amplify expertise where it exists, but it can also mask its absence" — a high-skill epistemic practice that deepens the capability gap between sophisticated and novice AI users. Disclosure frameworks are poorly equipped to capture this shift, since the process (triangulation across models) is invisible in the output.

4. **[Uber Burns Its 2026 AI Budget in Four Months on Claude Code](https://fortune.com/2026/05/26/uber-coo-ai-spending-tokens-claude-code/)** — Fortune / Forbes / Simon Willison, May-June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 4/5.** Uber exhausted its entire 2026 AI budget by April — four months into the year — after Claude Code spread across roughly 5,000 engineers faster than finance models anticipated. The COO publicly questioned whether the spend is worth it. This is not a failure story — engineers couldn't stop using the tools because they were too valuable. But it reveals a structural adoption challenge: agentic AI tools have unpredictable cost curves that break traditional budgeting. When individual engineers can autonomously spend tokens on agentic workflows, the aggregate spend can exceed organizational controls by an order of magnitude. This is a practical governance problem, not a technical one.

5. **[AI in Education and the Future of Teachers' Meaningful Work](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1844085/full)** — Frontiers in Education, June 7, 2026. **Reliability 4/5, Relevance 4/5.** Uses scenario methodology to map how algorithmic management, datafication, and human-AI collaboration reshape teachers' experience of meaningful work. Key tension: AI can automate administrative burdens that teachers resent, freeing time for high-agency teaching — but the same AI systems can also datafy teaching decisions, erode professional judgment, and transform teachers from craft professionals into AI system operators. The difference is in the design, not the capability: who decides what "meaningful" looks like, and whether AI is configured to support or supplant professional judgment.

## Superagency Connections

The throughline: **the gap between what AI promises and what it delivers is not a temporary immaturity — it's a structural feature of how we build and deploy these systems.**

- **Preference plurality → the alignment problem is an agency problem.** RLHF doesn't just fail to capture diverse preferences — it actively flattens them. The Superagency thesis says AI should expand individual agency. But if "alignment" means averaging everyone's values into a single model, it's reducing agency at the training stage — creating systems that represent no actual person's values while claiming to represent everyone's. This connects directly to [[Human Agency]] and the [[AI-IARA framework]].

- **Value collapse → market logic is an agency-erosion engine.** The HTL paper shows that market selection — not AI capability, not alignment failure, but raw economic competition — is the primary mechanism by which AI erodes human expertise. Even "better" AI (more aligned, more capable) accelerates this process because it makes AI outputs harder to distinguish from human ones. This is the dark side of the [[AI Agent Revolution]] — agency erosion through economic logic, not technological capability.

- **Adversarial co-thinking → the evaluative skill gap.** The paper identifies a capability that determines whether AI expands or erodes a user's agency: the ability to extract genuine critique from tools that default to praise. This is the practical complement to the [[AI-IARA framework]]'s "Interpretation" and "Awareness" capacities. It's also a concrete, teachable skill — and one that most AI users haven't developed.

- **Uber's budget → the cost-predictability gap is an agency governance problem.** When individual engineers can autonomously spend tokens beyond organizational control, the agency equation shifts: engineers gain individual leverage (agency expansion) while the organization loses financial control (agency erosion). [[Balanced Governance]] needs a cost-predictability dimension alongside the safety dimensions.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

- **The preference plurality critique may overstate RLHF's flattening effect.** RLHF with diverse annotator pools can, in principle, capture distributional preferences. The paper's finding that most values are minority positions is itself evidence of real diversity — the question is whether methods can represent this diversity rather than average it.

- **Value collapse assumes verification costs stay high.** If AI itself reduces verification costs (e.g., AI-powered fact-checking, automated source verification), the economic feedback loop could weaken. The paper's model depends on verification being costly relative to expected benefit — but AI could make verification cheaper.

- **Adversarial co-thinking is a high-skill practice that may not scale.** The paper's author is an experienced HCI researcher who knows how to calibrate AI tools against peer review. Teaching adversarial co-thinking to novices may be harder than the paper implies — and even if teachable, the skill gap between sophisticated and novice users could widen rather than narrow.

- **Uber's budget story could be a one-time calibration error, not a structural problem.** First-year AI budgets will be wrong because nobody knows what adoption curves look like. Second-year budgets will be more accurate. The real question is whether cost curves stabilize or continue to surprise.

## Practical Applications

1. **For AI developers:** Audit your alignment process for preference plurality. If your reward model represents a single averaged "preference," it's flattening human diversity. Ask: how many distinct preference clusters does your training data contain, and does your model preserve or collapse them?

2. **For writers and creators using AI:** Try adversarial co-thinking this week. For your next writing task, use two different AI tools, give each the same brief, and set their outputs against each other. Ask each: "What would the other AI get wrong about this?" The goal is not to pick a winner — it's to surface the gaps, tensions, and assumptions that neither tool would reveal alone.

3. **For organizational leaders:** If you haven't already, build agentic AI cost monitoring into your governance stack BEFORE your engineers discover these tools. Uber's experience suggests the adoption curve is steep and the cost curve steeper. Budget for 3-5x your initial estimate and set per-engineer caps with escalation paths.

4. **For educators:** The Frontiers paper's scenario methodology is a tool, not just a finding. Run a scenario exercise with your teaching team: map three possible futures (AI as administrative assistant, AI as instructional co-designer, AI as algorithmic manager) and identify which teaching practices are most at risk in each scenario — and which are most worth protecting.

## Top Insight of the Day

**Better alignment can accelerate agency erosion.** The value collapse paper surfaces a genuinely counterintuitive dynamic: as AI outputs become more aligned with human expectations, they become harder to distinguish from human-produced work. This makes verification costlier, which erodes the economic incentive to verify, which leads markets to select against human expertise. Alignment — normally treated as an unalloyed good — has a shadow: it can make AI's agency-eroding effects *less detectable*, not less real. The practical implication: alignment needs an agency-preservation dimension alongside its safety dimension. An aligned model that erodes human expertise through market dynamics is still aligned — and still harmful.

## Practical Experiment to Try

**This week: Run an adversarial co-thinking session on a decision you're making.** Pick something consequential — a project direction, a hiring choice, a curriculum design — and brief two different AI models (Claude and ChatGPT, or Claude and Gemini) independently. Then ask each: "What would the other AI miss, oversimplify, or get wrong about this?" Compare the gaps. Did either AI surface something the other missed? Did the triangulation reveal an assumption you hadn't examined? The point is not to "trust" either AI — it's to use their disagreement as a thinking tool.
