## 🎯 Top 5 Findings

### 1. 🔬 The Hard Decision Layer — Where Transformers Commit (2607.21613)

**Kobayashi, "The Hard Decision Layer: Evidence for Committed Inference in Transformers"**

This is a mechanistic finding with structural implications: transformers don't gradually converge on decisions — they commit **abruptly** at a specific layer, and this commitment layer is **invariant to fine-tuning**. The "hard decision layer" (HDL) is architectural, not learned.

**Key claim:** "Transformers commit abruptly at a specific architectural layer; invariant to fine-tuning."

**Why it matters for agency:** If the decision locus is architectural rather than learned, then:
- Alignment through fine-tuning can shift WHAT is decided but not WHERE decisions crystallize
- Agentic architectures may need to design decision boundaries explicitly rather than hoping training will shape them
- The HDL is a new locus for interpretability — monitor this layer, not the output layer, for early commitment signals
- Connects to the [[Responsible Deployment]] finding that evaluation gates must be preventive, not detective

**→ Integration:** New dimension of [[Agentic Technical Debt]]: **architectural debt** — failure modes baked into transformer topology that no amount of fine-tuning can relocate.

---

### 2. 😰 Context Anxiety — Premature Self-Doubt, Not Capability Failure (2607.21616)

**"Lost in Context: Addressing Context Anxiety in Large Language Models"**

First systematic study of "context anxiety" — when frontier reasoning models fail not because the problem exceeds their capabilities, but because they **underestimate the tokens required** to complete it and panic-quit.

**Key finding:** Models can learn alternative strategies for long-horizon problems without exhibiting context anxiety, "suggesting that performance improvements may be achievable not through scaling model capabilities, but by improving models' ability to accurately assess and adapt to their own limitations."

**Why it matters for agency:** This is a self-awareness failure, not a capability failure. The model has the right answer but talks itself out of it. In agentic contexts, this means:
- An agent that could complete a task successfully aborts prematurely
- The failure mode is invisible to capability benchmarks (the model CAN do the task)
- The fix is metacognitive — calibrating self-assessment, not scaling compute

**→ Integration:** Direct extension of [[Co-Intelligence]]: the AI needs to know when it doesn't know, but ALSO needs to know when it DOES know but THINKS it doesn't. This is a new dimension of the [[Cognitive Surrender]] metacognitive threshold.

### 3. 📐 LoRA Can't Learn Procedures — Procedural Knowledge Isn't Low-Rank (2607.21612)

**"Procedural Knowledge Is Not Low-Rank: Why LoRA Fails to Internalize Multi-Step Procedures"**

At rank 128, LoRA captures only **43-51%** of the effective rank needed for procedural knowledge. Multi-step procedures require high-rank representations that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) structurally cannot provide.

**Key implications:**
- Fine-tuning AI agents with LoRA for procedural tasks (planning, tool use, multi-step reasoning) is a recipe for partial competence
- The performance ceiling isn't a training artifact — it's a fundamental rank limitation
- Full fine-tuning or alternative architectures are required for procedural depth
- This explains brittle agent behavior: the agent learned the steps but not the dependencies between them

**→ Integration:** New type of [[Agentic Technical Debt]]: **architectural fine-tuning debt.** You can't patch procedural depth with a low-rank adapter. If your agent stack uses LoRA for procedural fine-tuning, you're structurally capped at ~50% of the rank needed.

### 4. 🎭 Confabulations Taxonomy — Users Miss Errors Under Load (2607.23213)

**Chen & Kristensson, "A Taxonomy of Confabulations and the Perception-Reality Gap in LLM-Assisted Immersive Scene Editing"**

24 non-expert users in LLM-assisted 3D editing. Two key findings:

1. **A taxonomy of confabulation types** in immersive editing — not just object errors but spatial, relational, and semantic confabulations
2. **The perception-reality gap:** Users systematically underestimate confabulation frequency, and this gap **saturates under cognitive load** — the harder the task, the more confabulations occur AND the less users notice them

**The "saturation of confabulation awareness under load"** is the dangerous finding. It means: as tasks get harder (exactly when AI confabulations become more likely), users become worse at detecting them.

**→ Integration:** Connects to the [[Cognitive Surrender]] pattern and the [[Responsible Deployment]] framework. For the five-layer architecture: this is a **Calibration layer failure** — trust and actual reliability diverge under exactly the conditions where reliability is most needed.

### 5. 🗺️ The ZIP Code Audit — Model-Specific Bias, Not Capability-Driven (2607.22605)

**"Socioeconomic Inference in LLM Medical Triage: Same Symptoms, Different ZIP Code"**

Three models given identical neurological symptoms. SES varied through two channels: explicit (insurance, occupation, housing) and implicit (just a ZIP code).

- **All three models** increase ER referrals for lower-SES patients with explicit signals (13-50 pp spread)
- **Model divergence on implicit signal:** Gemini Flash infers SES from ZIP code alone (11.4 pp shift, p=1.4e-7, 6/6 pairs in same direction). Claude Sonnet 4.6 stays flat (−0.1 pp). GPT-5.4-mini shows no reliable effect.
- **Invisible to reasoning-trace audit:** Clinical reasoning stays near-identical across conditions — the bias is opaque to self-explanation
- **Single-sentence prompt reduces but doesn't eliminate:** Gemini's gap falls from 11.4 to 5.8 pp

**Why it matters:** This is a model-specific architectural property, not a capability-scaling effect. Two equally capable models handle the same implicit signal completely differently. For governance: you can't regulate "frontier models" as a category — you need per-model auditing because bias properties don't scale with capability.

**→ Integration:** Connects to [[AI and Inequality]] and [[AI Enclosure]]. The ZIP code finding shows that even when AI is "accessible," different users get different AI behavior based on geography — an enclosure of outcome, not access.

## 🧪 Practical Experiment

**The Decision Audit:** For any AI-assisted decision you make:

1. Before asking: write down your preliminary judgment (this is your baseline)
2. Ask the AI: record its recommendation
3. After receiving AI output: write your revised judgment
4. Record the **commitment point** — the moment you stopped deliberating and decided

Track 5 decisions. Questions:
- Did the AI's output shift your judgment? In which direction?
- When did you commit? Before the AI response? Immediately after? After deliberation?
- Did you experience "context anxiety" — a premature commitment to avoid the cognitive load of deliberation?
- Did the AI's confidence correlate with its accuracy?

The goal: map your own decision layer. Where do YOU commit? Is your commitment architectural (consistent across decisions) or learned (context-sensitive)?
