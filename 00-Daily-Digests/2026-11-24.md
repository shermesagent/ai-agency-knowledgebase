# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-24

## Executive Summary
Five research preprints sharpen a common rule: adoption is safer when organizations make invisible tool use visible **and** give people a usable alternative, a legitimate way to defer a decision, and a way to trace claims to evidence. Three papers were submitted November 20–22; two older September submissions are newly reviewed today, not new releases. The studies span a mixed-sector education survey, a synthetic hiring evaluation, a research-process framework, a reanalysis and a controlled team-forecasting task. None establishes a district-wide outcome or production safety guarantee.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **Shadow apps signal an unmet support need.** [Kelso et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28280) surveyed 375 educators and 21 administrators across K–12 and higher education. Educators reported 1,373 app uses across 494 unique apps; reported shadow-IT policy awareness was 30.3% for K–12 and 24.8% for higher ed. This is *not* an AI-only prevalence estimate or a representative Farmersville ISD measure. An approved alternative and clear privacy path matter as much as a ban. → [[AI for School Districts]]
2. **A model should be able to say the evidence is insufficient.** [Alsayed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28426) tested evidence sufficiency and stability on synthetic job-interview responses; 85.19% actionable coverage and 3.80% disagreement on 216 held-out cases were measured against AI-assisted *provisional* references. Neither figure validates real-world hiring decisions or fairness. Protect a human deferral route. → [[Work]]
3. **Unverified claims can become downstream obligations.** [Celik and Al-Hashimi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29434) propose a claim–source–test–decision map to manage “verification debt” in human–AI research. It is a conceptual framework, not a measured effectiveness study. A claim ledger can prevent an attractive but unverified summary from silently becoming policy. → [[Agentic Technical Debt]]
4. **Test whether people recover when help becomes unreliable.** [Liu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28006) proposes a resilience stress-test and reanalyzes five public studies with 1,074 analytic participants. The September submission is newly reviewed today. Accuracy while AI works and recovery after it fails are different outcomes; this is not evidence from a new school trial. → [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]]
5. **Team benefit depends on the question.** [Schumann et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28009) studied proactive scouting agents in a controlled, 34-game baseball forecasting task. Winner prediction improved for small teams; run-total prediction did not show a statistical change. This September submission does not prove the same effect in school meetings. → [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]]

## Practical Experiment — A no-student-data tool-review rehearsal
Run a 30-minute tabletop with a *fictional* classroom-app request and public documentation only. Have one group follow an existing policy route, and another follow a simple intake card: instructional purpose, data requested, contract/privacy review, accessibility, approved substitute, decision owner and time to answer. Compare whether both groups can identify the approver, a safe interim option and the information still missing. Record elapsed time and unresolved questions, not staff names or real app histories. If the new card shortens time without skipping privacy checks, consider testing it with an authorized real procurement request **outside any unapproved AI tool**. This tests workflow clarity, not the survey's prevalence claims or student outcomes. See [[Responsible Deployment]] and [[AI for School Districts]].
