# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-10-27

**The Authorized-Reader-and-Measure Test** · Tuesday curator pass · Four distinct sources (two non-paper, two preprints); five findings are not five independent studies.

## Executive Summary
AI-mediated access to news and learning can widen individual choice, but only if the *human purpose* stays attached to the handoff. Today's sources expose two easily missed decisions: who authorizes an agent to read and repackage a publisher's work, and who gets to set the learner's or evaluator's standard. A small audit of permissions, learner-owned decisions and versioned scoring is more useful than generic promises about personalization.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **The reader now sometimes delegates reading.** WIRED reports Axios Direct's first deal-content feed for subscriber agents and Every's prompts and queryable reporting databases. The later Axios training and consumer feeds are plans. The unresolved agency question is whether the reader can inspect a source and the publisher can license agent access. → [[AI and Creator Rights]], [[The Disclosure Effect]].
2. **Personalization can change the editorial act.** Every describes a subscriber agent making a tailored article, podcast or video from a reporting database. That is a reported product practice, not proof that the result stays accurate or pays contributors fairly. → [[AI and Creator Rights]], [[AI Enclosure]].
3. **Learning help needs an activity contract.** Haq's framework gives educators control over the cognitive objective, group artifact and learner-owned product, then derives permitted help and prohibitions. It offers a design to test, not evidence of improved student learning. → [[Education]], [[AI Tutors]].
4. **An upgraded judge can lose the ability to distinguish good from flawed work.** Zhang and Yang report one same-family transition assigning **93% of proposal-pool scores one value**. Mean-score recalibration cannot restore item-level resolution; test version replacements on fixed anchors before trusting a trend line. → [[The Judge Problem]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]].
5. **Automation has a real resource and verification bill.** Import AI recounts a Resolution researcher's reported week of agent-led Lean formalization, with an estimated **$135,000 token spend** and roughly **600,000 lines of Lean**. This shows the scale of one self-reported project, not that its mathematical result has been independently verified here or that typical schools can repeat it. → [[AI-Augmented Scientific Collaboration]], [[Compute and Agency]].

## Superagency Connections
Capacity: agents can reach reporting and formal methods that individuals could not previously navigate alone. Freedom: the reader and learner must be able to choose *what remains theirs to interpret and decide*. Platforms: subscriptions and feed licenses define access. Value: source credit, verification time and compute belong on the ledger. Wisdom: keep the same standard visible before and after automating an evaluation. See [[Superagency]] and [[Human Agency]].

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- An agent-ready feed could make licensed journalism more accessible, especially for time-poor readers or alternative formats; it could also concentrate distribution and obscure source context. The WIRED article supplies examples, not a sector-wide outcome study.
- Overly rigid classroom restrictions can prevent useful accessibility or language scaffolds. Educator approvals should be revisable, age-appropriate and tested with the actual task, not treated as universal settings.
- Stratified judge audits cost effort and can still miss rare errors. For consequential student decisions, they supplement rather than replace human assessment and an appeal path.
- The research formalization account includes substantial spending; the output volume alone says nothing about correctness, replicability or broad affordability.

## Practical Applications
For a low-stakes, publicly available reading assignment, give an agent a licensed/open article and ask for a brief with **source links and a visible distinction between direct claims and inference**. Have a human compare two consequential claims with the original. Before a class pilot, have the educator mark one learner-owned decision, one allowed hint and one prohibited full solution. If an automated rubric is involved, pin its version and score identical teacher-authored clean/flawed examples before and after any upgrade. No identifiable student work, unpublished manuscripts or subscriber-only articles in an unapproved tool.

## Top Insight of the Day
**Access through an agent is not agency unless a person can inspect the source, retain the decision and contest the measure.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
Use one open-access article and a teacher-authored public example task. Run the three-part **source–decision–measure audit**: (1) can a human trace the agent's summary to the article, (2) can the learner explain a decision the AI did not make, and (3) do clean and flawed examples remain distinguishable when the scoring model changes? Record failures and review time, not student identifiers. Stop before any live grading or publisher redistribution until licensing, privacy and human appeal are clear.
