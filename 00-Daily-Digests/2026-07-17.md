## Top 7 Findings

### 1. When Bots Join the Team: Institutional Strengthening, Not Homogenization

**[arXiv 2607.13679](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13679)** — The first large-scale empirical study of what happens when AI bots become regular participants in collaborative human groups. Studying **2,991 GitHub open-source projects**, the paper asks whether bot adoption weakens or strengthens the institutional fabric of collaborative communities.

**Core finding: bots strengthen institutions.** Bot adoption is associated with *more repeated engagement* among all contributors (not just bot operators), *fewer conflict-ridden pull requests*, *more distinctive project outputs*, and *fewer blocking "veto" comments.* The latter finding is especially striking — bots appear to reduce, rather than amplify, conflict cascades.

**The mechanism:** Bots handle routine coordination tasks (issue triage, dependency updates, formatting enforcement) that previously generated friction. By absorbing the coordination overhead, bots free human contributors for substantive work — and reduce the friction that escalates into conflict.

**Why it matters for the convergence trap:** This is the strongest empirical counterpoint yet to the [[Agentic Convergence Trap]] thesis. The convergence trap predicts that AI-driven homogenization erases strategic differentiation. The OSS data shows the opposite: bot-adopting projects produce *more* distinctive outputs than non-adopting projects. The key distinction may be *complementarity vs. substitution* — bots that complement human work (handling coordination) strengthen institutions; bots that substitute for human judgment (making strategic decisions) may produce convergence. The convergence trap isn't wrong — it's conditional.

### 2. Early Adoption of Agentic Coding Tools: 25,264 PRs Analyzed

**[arXiv 2607.14037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14037)** — The most granular picture yet of how agentic coding tools are actually being adopted. Analysis of **25,264 agentic pull requests** across **7,402 GitHub projects** during the first wave of Claude Code, Codex, and OpenClaw adoption.

**Key findings:**
- **Adoption is concentrated.** A small number of high-adoption projects account for most agentic activity — the agent revolution has early adopters, not universal uptake.
- **Single-human oversight dominates.** The most common deployment pattern: one human oversees multiple agentic tools, reviewing and merging agent-generated code. This is not "AI replaces developer" — it's "AI writes, human inspects."
- **Small projects adopt more aggressively.** Smaller, newer projects show higher rates of agentic PR adoption. Established projects with existing contributor bases adopt more slowly — institutional inertia is real.
- **Multiple agents per project is common.** Projects using agents typically use more than one — Claude Code for architecture, Codex for implementation, OpenClaw for maintenance tasks.

**Why it matters:** The single-human oversight model validates the Superagency thesis at the code level: AI amplifies what one person can ship. But it also raises the throughput bottleneck question: if one human oversees five agents, at what point does oversight quality degrade? The paper doesn't answer this — but it makes it measurable.

### 3. Networked Intelligence: Scaling Human-AI Connections, Not Just Models

**[arXiv 2607.13220](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13220)** — Introduces **Mycelium**, an active shared context graph for human-AI team science. The core insight: as AI agents join scientific teams, the bottleneck shifts from model capability to *shared context* — whether agents and humans can build and maintain a common understanding of what they're working on, what's been tried, and what the current hypotheses are.

**The Mycelium approach:** A living graph that tracks entities, relationships, hypotheses, experimental results, and provenance across human and AI contributions. Unlike static documentation, Mycelium updates as the team works — agents can query it for context, humans can inspect it for understanding, and both can contribute to it.

**The scaling thesis:** "Scaling connections, not models." The paper argues that the next frontier for AI-augmented science is not bigger models but better shared context — infrastructure that lets human-AI teams maintain coherent research programs across weeks and months rather than individual sessions.

**Why it matters:** Mycelium is the infrastructure counterpart to the Participation Layer. An agent that joins a scientific team needs to know what the team knows. Without shared context, the agent is a tool that answers questions. With shared context, it's a team member that contributes to an ongoing inquiry. This is the difference between AI *assisting* science and AI *participating* in science.

### 4. CAVA: Runtime Governance — Verifying What Agents Actually Did

**[arXiv 2607.13716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13716)** — Proposes **Canonical Action Verification and Attestation (CAVA)**, a governance layer for agentic AI systems. The problem: when an AI agent performs a sequence of actions across multiple systems (email → calendar → CRM → billing), how do you know what it actually did? Logs can be spoofed, actions can be disguised, and the chain of consequences can extend beyond any single system's visibility.

**CAVA's approach:**
- **Runtime evidence packages:** For each agent action, CAVA captures a cryptographically verifiable evidence package — what was attempted, what parameters were used, what system state existed before and after, and what downstream effects were observed.
- **Cross-runtime attestation:** Actions across heterogeneous systems (different APIs, different auth domains, different logging formats) are unified into a single attestation graph that can be independently verified.
- **Governance integration:** The attestation graph plugs into existing governance frameworks — compliance audits, incident investigations, access control reviews — with verifiable provenance rather than system-trust-based assertions.

**Why it matters:** CAVA is the institutional memory that agent participation requires. When a human team member makes a decision, you can ask them why. When an AI agent makes a decision, you need CAVA. The runtime evidence package is the answer to "what did the agent do and why?" — and the cross-runtime attestation makes it verifiable across the heterogeneous systems agents operate in. This is the [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]] at the technical level: an agent that can't produce a verifiable record of its actions can't be trusted to act in the user's best interest.

### 5. Memory-Driven Self-Disclosure: How Human-AI Relationships Develop Over Time

**[arXiv 2607.14593](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14593)** — A longitudinal multimodal study: **24 participants interacted with a conversational AI agent across 10 weekly sessions**, with the agent maintaining memory of previous interactions. The study tracked self-disclosure depth, relational turning points, and how the memory architecture shaped relationship development.

**Key findings:**
- **Self-disclosure increased over time** — participants shared more personal information in later sessions, consistent with trust development in human-human relationships.
- **Memory-driven relational turning points** — moments when the AI recalled something from a previous session created qualitative shifts in how participants perceived the relationship. "Oh, you remember that" was a recurring turning-point marker.
- **Memory failures as relationship damage** — when the AI forgot something it should have remembered, trust declined sharply. Memory isn't just a feature; it's the relational substrate.
- **Individual differences were substantial** — some participants developed deep disclosure patterns; others maintained instrumental, task-focused interactions across all 10 sessions. The relationship is co-constructed, not AI-determined.

**Why it matters:** This provides the empirical foundation for what [[Co-Intelligence]] calls the Co-Existence transition. Co-existence with AI that remembers you is qualitatively different from co-existence with AI that treats every interaction as session-zero. The memory architecture of AI agents — what they remember, what they forget, and how they use memory in interaction — is not a technical detail. It's the architecture of the human-AI relationship.

### 6. The Industrialization of Research: AI and the Craft-to-Pipeline Shift

**[arXiv 2607.15164](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15164)** — Argues that AI is transforming scientific research from a **craft model** (individual researchers making judgment calls about methods, interpretation, and direction) to a **pipeline model** (standardized workflows where AI handles experimental design, execution, and initial interpretation, with humans providing oversight at decision gates).

**Three consequences:**
1. **Volume over judgment:** Pipeline science produces more results but may reduce the role of researcher intuition in selecting which questions to pursue.
2. **Replicability improves, novelty may decline:** Standardized AI-driven methods improve replicability — but the standardization that enables replicability may suppress the methodological heterogeneity that produces breakthroughs.
3. **The research workforce restructures:** The pipeline model doesn't eliminate scientists — it changes what they do. The scientist becomes a pipeline designer and quality inspector rather than a hands-on experimenter.

**Why it matters:** The craft-to-pipeline shift is the Participation Layer applied to science itself. When AI joins the research team, it doesn't just assist — it industrializes the research process. This creates a tension: industrialization accelerates discovery (more experiments, faster iteration) but may also suppress the craft-level intuition and methodological diversity that produces paradigm shifts. The question is not whether AI should participate in research — it's whether the pipeline can be designed to preserve the craft functions that produce breakthroughs.

→ Connects to: [[Democratization of Expertise]], [[AI-Augmented Scientific Collaboration]], [[Future of Work]]

### 7. When AI Blurs the Boundaries of Contribution: Authorship Calibration

**[arXiv 2607.15006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15006)** — An empirical study of how people calibrate their sense of contribution when AI is a co-creator. The study investigates **authorship calibration** — whether individuals accurately perceive their own contribution to jointly produced work, and how AI involvement affects that perception.

**Key findings:**
- **Contribution inflation:** Participants systematically *overestimated* their own contribution to AI-assisted work. The more the AI contributed, the more participants inflated their own role.
- **Opacity drives miscalibration:** When the AI's contribution was less visible (integrated into the workflow rather than clearly demarcated), miscalibration was worse. Explicit contribution boundaries — "I wrote this section; AI wrote that section" — improved calibration.
- **Individual differences in calibration skill:** Some participants were consistently accurate in assessing their contribution; others were consistently inaccurate. Calibration appears to be a skill that varies across individuals.

**Why it matters:** The Participation Layer's central question — what happens when AI joins the team — has a psychological dimension. When people can't accurately assess their own contribution, they can't make good decisions about when to delegate to AI, when to override AI, and when to invest in their own skill development. Authorship calibration is the metacognitive skill that underlies effective co-existence: knowing what *you* brought to the work versus what the AI brought.

→ Connects to: [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[Human Agency]], [[Creativity]]

## Synthesis: The Participation Layer

The arc connecting this week's digests now spans four layers:

| Day | Layer | Question | Key Finding |

| July 17 | **Participation** | What happens to institutions when AI joins as a regular participant? | Bots strengthen institutional fabric; single-human oversight dominates agentic coding; memory architecture is the relational substrate |

**The participation thesis in three findings:**

1. **Institutions can strengthen, not weaken.** When Bots Join the Team (2,991 projects) provides the first large-scale evidence that AI participation can *improve* institutional health — more collaboration, less conflict, more distinctive output. The convergence trap is conditional, not inevitable.

2. **Oversight models are emerging, not missing.** Early agentic coding adoption (25,264 PRs) shows that single-human oversight is the dominant pattern — one human reviews and merges agent-generated code. The participation architecture is being built in practice, not just in theory.

3. **Memory is the relational architecture.** Longitudinal human-AI interaction (24 participants × 10 sessions) shows that what AI remembers — and what it forgets — fundamentally shapes the human-AI relationship. Memory architecture is not a technical detail; it's the participation substrate.

**The common thread across all four layers:** Each layer reveals a dimension of *agency design.* Abstention is designing for when agents shouldn't act. Infrastructure is designing the environment agents operate within. Sovereignty is designing who holds final authority. Participation is designing what happens to human institutions when agents are inside them. None of these are default properties of AI systems — they are deliberate design targets. The Superagency thesis is the claim that these design targets, pursued together, produce AI that amplifies human capability rather than replacing it.

## Practical Experiment: The "Bot on the Team" Audit

This week's experiment: audit a collaborative context where AI is already participating.

1. **Identify a team or project** where AI agents contribute regularly — a GitHub repo with automated PRs, a Slack channel with AI summaries, a shared document with AI drafting.
2. **Track three metrics for one week:**
   - **Engagement:** Are human contributors interacting more or less with each other since AI joined?
   - **Conflict:** Are disagreements, vetoes, and rework cycles increasing or decreasing?
   - **Distinctiveness:** Is the team's output becoming more similar to other teams' output, or more distinctive?
3. **Interview two team members** (informally): "Has AI changed how the team works together?" Don't ask about productivity — ask about collaboration.
4. **Reflect:** Is AI participating in your team as a complement (handling coordination so humans can focus on substance) or as a substitute (making decisions humans used to make)? The When Bots Join the Team finding suggests complementarity strengthens institutions. The convergence trap warns that substitution erodes them. Which pattern describes your team?

**What to learn:** The Participation Layer isn't theoretical — it's happening in every team using AI agents. The audit makes the participation architecture visible. The goal is not to remove AI from teams but to design its participation so that it strengthens rather than weakens the human collaborative fabric.
