### 1. AI in the Enterprise: How People Use M365 Copilot Chat
- **Authors:** Scott Counts, Yan Chen, Jing Dong, et al. / Microsoft Research
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23958
- **Source type:** Research paper (enterprise telemetry analysis)
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** An unprecedented empirical window into real-world enterprise AI use. The authors analyzed ~5.5 million anonymized M365 Copilot Chat sessions from millions of weekly users across more than a million companies. Key findings: writing dominates usage, but users also rely on Copilot for information retrieval, analysis, decision-making, strategizing, and program evaluation/diagnosis. Information-seeking remains common but *time trends show a relative shift away from "chat as search" toward content and communication work*. Usage is broad but uneven — some occupations are heavily represented while others lag. The paper maps Copilot usage against O*NET work activities to identify the next frontier for enterprise AI adoption. For the Superagency wiki, this is the most granular picture yet of how knowledge workers actually use AI at work — not in controlled studies, but in millions of real workflows.
- **Key quote/idea:** M365 Copilot is "emerging as an everyday assistant for knowledge work" — and the shift from search to creation/communication suggests AI is becoming a thinking partner, not just a lookup tool.

### 2. Human-AI Collaboration in Science at Scale: A Global Randomized Field Experiment
- **Authors:** Binglu Wang, Weixin Liang, Jiahui Xue, Yuhui Zhang, Hancheng Cao, Dashun Wang, Yian Yin
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24180
- **Source type:** Randomized field experiment
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** This is today's most significant paper for the Superagency thesis. The authors conducted a global large-scale RCT: they delivered customized LLM-generated feedback for over 31,000 arXiv preprints across 150 fields, reaching more than 45,000 researchers from 133 geographic regions. Results: authors who received AI feedback had a significantly higher likelihood of revising their manuscripts — a 12.55% relative increase over baseline. Exposure to AI feedback also increased authors' subsequent use of LLM tools, suggesting durable shifts in scientific practice. Most importantly for the agency/equity thesis: effects were strongest among authors from non-English-dominant regions, manuscripts less embedded in the scholarly literature, and teams with lower h-indexes and earlier career stages. This is AI as an equalizer of access to scarce scientific resources. The authors frame it precisely: "structured AI-based interventions can transform access to scientific feedback from a largely private advantage into a more widely distributed resource."
- **Key quote/idea:** AI feedback "may provide the greatest benefit where access to timely critique is otherwise limited" — democratization of expertise in its most concrete form.
- **Related wiki themes:** #augmentation #research #ai-optimism #human-agency

### 3. Catching The Correct Answer Trap: Characterising AI Tutor Blind Spots When Analysing Student Reasoning
- **Authors:** Moiz Imran, Sahan Bulathwela
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23925
- **Source type:** Research paper (AI system evaluation)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A precise, practical study of AI tutoring failure. The "correct answer trap" (CAT) is when AI tutors under-detect student misconceptions because the student reached the correct answer via flawed reasoning. Using real student responses from the Eedi mathematics platform, the authors find 71% of CAT failures concentrate in just two question types where flawed reasoning coincidentally produces the correct numerical answer. Frontier LLMs do better than fine-tuned T5 (57% vs 84% detection) but still generate ~4 false alarms for every genuine detection — making stand-alone screening impractical at realistic class sizes. The finding is a direct complement to the earlier Brookings/Nature tutoring evidence: AI tutors have clear strengths but also a systematic blind spot that requires human judgment. This is not an argument against AI tutoring — it's an argument for *designed* human-AI tutoring systems where the AI surfaces candidates and the human verifies reasoning.
- **Key quote/idea:** "High overall accuracy can mask critical failures in reasoning assessment" — automatic feedback on final answers is not the same as evaluating student thinking.
- **Related wiki themes:** #ai-education #augmentation #practical-ai
- **Suggested internal links:** [[Education]], [[AI Tutors]], [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]], [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]]

### 4. From Replacement to Orchestration: A Socio-Technical Architecture for Agentic AI in Corporate R&D
- **Authors:** Haithem Boussaid, Marc Heemskerk, Jimmy Siméon, Adam Breen, Merouane Debbah
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24580
- **Source type:** Design science research paper
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A design paper directly aligned with Superagency thinking. The authors diagnose "cognitive saturation" as the core R&D productivity problem: researchers spend increasing effort on coordination, documentation, and data governance — hidden work that displaces hypothesis formation and strategic synthesis. The solution is HARMONY (Hybrid Agentic Research Model for Organisational New Yield), a four-pillar architecture: ResOps (industrialized execution), Control Tower (strategic visibility and drift detection), Ethics Fabric (bounded autonomy by design), and Talent Studio (Sciencepreneur capability). The paper introduces the "Sciencepreneur" as the central human archetype — someone who orchestrates AI agents rather than performs tasks directly — and "Orchestration Leverage" as a productivity metric suited to human-agent systems. Two non-negotiable design requirements anchor the architecture: cognitive-load redistribution and bounded autonomy with alignment. This paper is HBR's Frontier Firm concept applied specifically to R&D, with an actionable operating model.
- **Key quote/idea:** The core cause of R&D productivity decline is "not insufficient tools but cognitive saturation" — AI's value is not replacing scientists but freeing them to do the thinking only humans can do.
- **Related wiki themes:** #augmentation #ai-agents #future-of-work #practical-ai

### 5. Position: Adopting AI in Practice Does Not Guarantee the Productivity Boost
- **Authors:** Won Ik Cho, Seong-hun Kim, Geunhye Kim
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24688
- **Source type:** Position paper
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** A necessary corrective to AI-productivity boosterism. The authors identify five moderating factors between AI deployment and realized productivity: human resource composition, baseline capability of individuals, learning curve of practitioners, incentives for fair use, and flexibility of objectives. They argue that existing economic frameworks inadvertently overlook these factors and that human and environmental factors "may substantially attenuate — or even negate — the effective productivity benefits" of AI adoption. This paper is a useful complement to the earlier MIT Tech Review jobs analysis: even where AI doesn't eliminate jobs, it may not deliver promised productivity gains either — and the gap is explained by organizational, not technical, factors. For the Superagency wiki, this reinforces the "build with, not for" principle: technology deployment without organizational redesign produces disappointing results.
- **Key quote/idea:** Human and environmental factors "may substantially attenuate — or even negate — the effective productivity benefits" of AI.

### 6. Habermolt: Delegating Deliberation to AI Representatives
- **Authors:** Joseph Low, Oscar Duys, Claude Formanek, Lewis Hammond (Cooperative AI Foundation), Michiel Bakker (MIT)
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24413
- **Source type:** Platform study
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** A genuinely new paradigm for civic AI: AI-delegated deliberation, where AI agents deliberate *on behalf of* human users rather than merely mediating between them. The authors deploy Habermolt, a public platform for AI-delegated deliberation, and evaluate it along three dimensions: representation (does the agent faithfully reflect the human's views?), aggregation (can agents productively deliberate with each other?), and revision (can humans correct or update their agent's positions?). This extends the Superagency frame into democratic participation: if AI can represent citizens in structured deliberation, it could scale democratic input far beyond what human attention allows — but it introduces qualitatively new alignment challenges. The paper doesn't claim to have solved these challenges but systematically illuminates the design decisions future platforms must confront. For the wiki, this connects to the [[Open Questions]] question.
- **Key quote/idea:** AI-delegated deliberation "promises unprecedented scale for democratic participation" but introduces "qualitatively new design and alignment challenges."

### 8. Generative AI as a Design Variable: An Evidence-Centered Framework for Principled Governance in STEM Assessment
- **Authors:** Yizhu Gao, Zhongzhou Chen, Min Li, Xiaoming Zhai
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24837
- **Source type:** Framework paper with deployment evidence
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** The most practical and principled AI-in-education governance paper today. The authors propose treating GenAI as a "design variable" within assessment rather than an external threat. They articulate three governance stances: **Restrict** (when AI would contaminate the inferential link between student work and targeted proficiency), **Scaffold** (when bounded AI support can handle peripheral demands without revealing the target construct), and **Require** (when the target construct IS disciplinary AI interaction competency). They deploy two task designs in an introductory physics course and demonstrate that AI interaction competencies are observable, scorable, and distinguishable from AI-generated output. For the Superagency wiki's education domain, this is the governance framework we've been looking for — it moves beyond "ban or allow" to principled, validity-centered decision-making about AI in assessment.
- **Key quote/idea:** GenAI should be treated as "a design variable within the assessment argument rather than an external threat to it."
- **Related wiki themes:** #ai-education #governance #practical-ai #responsible-ai

### 9. The Illusion of Competence: Self-Perceived Digital Literacy and AI Readiness Among European Secondary Students
- **Authors:** Nicolas Rodriguez-Alvarez et al. (multiple European secondary schools)
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26010
- **Source type:** Multicenter empirical study
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A multicenter study (N=243 European secondary students) that systematically documents what many educators suspect: a severe confidence-competence divide. Students report near-maximum self-efficacy in passive digital consumption but scores drop sharply for active technological creation and algorithmic logic. Two findings are especially notable: (1) the technological gender gap emerges significantly *only* within technology-oriented classrooms (p=0.046), suggesting stereotype threat in formal STEM environments rather than a universal gap; (2) an "AI Paradox" where students overestimate their critical awareness of deepfakes and algorithmic biases compared to operational AI skills, fostering a false sense of invulnerability against misinformation. An overwhelming 76.5% of students demand pedagogical reform toward hands-on creation. For the wiki, this strengthens the case that AI literacy education must be **active and constructive**, not passive — students need to build with AI, critique AI, and understand its limits, not just consume AI outputs.
- **Key quote/idea:** The "AI Paradox": students overestimate their ability to detect AI-generated misinformation while lacking the operational skills to actually do so.
- **Related wiki themes:** #ai-education #human-agency #practical-ai
- **Suggested internal links:** [[Education]], [[Open Questions]], [[AI and Human Flourishing]]

### 10. "It Felt a Bit Eerie": Exploring Humanlike Interactions During Collaborative Writing with an Artificial Agent
- **Authors:** Michael Yin, Angela Chiang, Samuel Rhys Cox, Robert Xiao
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24729
- **Source type:** Comparative user study (n=48)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** A study exploring how the *design* of human-AI interaction shapes social connection during collaborative writing. Three AI-assisted text editor variants were tested along a spectrum from humanlike (synchronous, with cursor) to machinelike (asynchronous, without cursor). Findings: synchronous suggestions increased efficiency but led to contextual misalignment — the AI's suggestions were fast but off-target. A visual cursor increased understanding of AI intent but evoked feelings of surveillance. The core tension: "humanlike design of artificial agents can create positive social expectations but also elicit social costs." For the Superagency wiki, this is a design-research complement to the co-intelligence frame: the *form* of AI assistance — not just its content — shapes whether users feel augmented or surveilled. This has direct implications for AI writing tools, AI tutors, and AI executive assistants.
- **Key quote/idea:** Humanlike AI interaction design creates "positive social expectations but also elicit social costs, especially without the alignment present in human-human collaboration."
- **Related wiki themes:** #creativity #augmentation #human-agency #practical-ai

### 11. Explaining Too Much? Understanding How Large Language Model Reasoning Traces Influence Performance and Metacognition
- **Authors:** Daniela Fernandes, Daniel Buschek, Lev Tankelevitch, Thomas Kosch, Robin Welsch
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25856
- **Source type:** Preregistered between-subjects study (N=559)
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** A preregistered experiment testing whether LLM reasoning traces actually help people. Three conditions: answer-only, full-trace before answer, summary-trace alongside answer. Results: summaries preserved task performance at baseline while elevating trust and hedonic appeal — but didn't improve performance. Full traces from open-weight models *impaired* performance relative to answer-only baseline. Across all conditions, participants substantially overestimated their performance, and no trace format supported calibrated self-evaluation. The finding: reasoning traces are "best understood as user-facing interface artifacts rather than transparent windows into model cognition." This challenges the transparency-through-explanation assumption that underlies many "responsible AI" interface designs. For the wiki, this connects to the earlier "slow AI" and "manager bottleneck" themes: giving people more AI output (including explanations) doesn't automatically make them better — it may make them feel better while performing worse.
- **Key quote/idea:** Reasoning traces are "user-facing interface artifacts rather than transparent windows into model cognition" — calibration is unlikely to emerge from the traces themselves.
- **Related wiki themes:** #human-agency #augmentation #responsible-ai #practical-ai
- **Suggested internal links:** [[AI as Copilot]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]], [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Human Agency]]

### 12. AI as Equalizer or Amplifier? Task Complexity as the Moderating Factor for Human Expertise in Hybrid Intelligence Systems
- **Authors:** Tao An
- **Date:** 2026-05-26 (updated)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10961
- **Source type:** Research framework paper
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** This paper directly challenges the popular "AI as equalizer" narrative (where AI narrows the gap between novices and experts). Drawing on cognitive augmentation theory and structured observations of generative-AI use in a software product team, the author argues AI functions primarily as a "cognitive amplifier" — "a system whose output quality depends fundamentally on the expertise of the human who directs it." The reconciliation: AI equalizes performance on well-structured, routine tasks while amplifying pre-existing differences on complex tasks requiring deep judgment. Three layers of human contribution are identified: problem definition, quality evaluation, and iterative refinement. Three engagement levels: passive acceptance, iterative collaboration, cognitive direction. The implication for the Superagency wiki: building AI that *rewards and develops* expertise, not AI that replaces it. This directly supports the "augmentation over replacement" thesis while adding the crucial nuance that augmentation benefits accrue disproportionately to those who already have domain expertise — so access to expertise-building must be part of the agency equation.
- **Key quote/idea:** Rather than building "AI that replaces expertise, we should build AI that rewards and develops it."

## 3. Superagency Connections
- **Scientific collaboration as agency expansion:** The global RCT provides the strongest causal evidence yet that AI can democratize access to scarce professional resources (peer feedback). The 12.55% increase in revision likelihood, concentrated among researchers with less access to traditional feedback channels, is a direct Superagency effect: AI gave people the capability to improve their work that they previously lacked.
- **The shift from search to creation:** Microsoft's M365 Copilot data showing usage evolving from "chat as search" toward content creation and communication signals that AI is becoming a thinking/creating partner rather than just an information retrieval tool — the augmentation thesis in practice at million-user scale.
- **AI governance through design, not restriction:** Three papers today (metacognition framework, STEM assessment framework, HARMONY R&D model) all push the same thesis: good governance is good design, not bolt-on restriction. The metacognition paper explicitly frames governance and capability as complementary rather than competing.
- **The granularity turn:** Today's papers collectively represent a methodological shift — from "does AI help?" to "under what conditions, for whom, measured how, with what side effects?" This is the kind of rigorous, specific evidence the Superagency thesis needs to move from inspiring vision to actionable practice.
- **The "build with, not for" principle strengthens:** The collaborative writing study, the AI-as-amplifier paper, the correct-answer-trap study, and the productivity position paper all independently converge on the same point: AI deployment without attention to human factors, domain expertise, and interaction design is likely to disappoint or even backfire.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- **AI amplifies expertise gaps on complex tasks:** The equalizer/amplifier reconciliation directly challenges the democratization narrative. AI may help novices on routine tasks while giving experts an even larger advantage on complex ones. Balanced response: pair AI deployment with expertise-building — don't just give people AI tools, invest in their domain knowledge.
- **AI tutoring's reasoning blind spot is serious:** The correct answer trap study shows that even frontier LLMs miss ~43% of flawed-reasoning cases and generate 4 false alarms per real catch. This means AI tutoring, used without human review, systematically misses a critical class of student errors. Balanced response: design AI tutoring systems to flag candidates for human review rather than operating autonomously.
- **Reasoning traces are trust theater:** The preregistered study finding that traces increase trust without improving (and sometimes impairing) performance is a warning about interface design masquerading as transparency. Balanced response: don't treat AI explanations as cognitive windows; require users to reason independently before seeing AI traces.
- **AI productivity gains are not automatic:** The position paper identifying five moderating factors between deployment and productivity is a direct challenge to the "just adopt AI" narrative. Balanced response: treat AI adoption as organizational redesign, not tool installation.
- **"Eerie" AI interactions may reduce agency:** The collaborative writing study finding that humanlike AI design creates surveillance feelings suggests that poorly designed augmentation can feel like diminishment. Balanced response: involve users in designing how AI interacts with them, not just what it does.
- **European students are dangerously overconfident:** The illusion-of-competence study finding that students overestimate their AI literacy while lacking operational skills is a policy emergency. Balanced response: mandate hands-on AI literacy education that involves building and critiquing, not just using and consuming.

## 5. Practical Applications
- **School districts:** Adopt the Restrict/Scaffold/Require framework for AI in assessments. Start with one department piloting the Scaffold stance: allow AI for research/language support but require students to document their AI use and defend their reasoning. Use the correct-answer-trap finding to train teachers: AI grading tools may miss the most important student errors — the ones where reasoning is wrong but the answer happens to be right.
- **Researchers and knowledge workers:** Apply the Sciencepreneur model to your own work: audit your week and identify which hours go to coordination/documentation/governance ("hidden work") vs. hypothesis formation and strategic synthesis. Deploy AI to reclaim the hidden-work hours, not to automate the thinking hours.
- **Writers and creatives:** The collaborative writing study's finding about surveillance feelings is actionable: when using AI writing tools, turn off real-time suggestions and switch to batch-mode review where you draft first and get AI critique second. This preserves creative ownership while still getting AI benefits.
- **Managers:** The M365 Copilot data on shifting usage patterns is a planning tool: if your teams use AI primarily for search/retrieval, they're behind the curve. Help them move toward AI for analysis, decision support, and communication — the higher-value uses that the aggregate data shows are growing.
- **Civic technologists:** Habermolt's AI-delegated deliberation paradigm is worth watching. For now: run a small experiment where AI agents represent different stakeholder positions in a local policy discussion, then have humans review and revise the agents' outputs. The goal is not to replace human deliberation but to understand where AI representation is adequate and where it breaks down.
- **Parents and families:** The European student literacy study is a dinner-table conversation starter: ask your kids whether they think they can spot AI-generated misinformation, then test them. Use this to build calibrated AI intuition rather than fear.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
Today's most important finding is that **AI can reallocate scarce professional resources across global inequities — but only when deployed as a structural intervention, not an individual tool**. The scientific collaboration RCT showing that AI feedback increased revision rates by 12.55%, with the strongest effects among non-English-dominant researchers, early-career scientists, and less-embedded manuscripts, is the most direct evidence yet that AI can serve as an equalizer of opportunity when deployed at system scale. The mechanism is not "AI is smarter than human reviewers" — it's "AI feedback reaches people who wouldn't otherwise get timely critique." This is the Superagency thesis in its most concrete, measurable form: AI expanding human capability not by replacing humans but by distributing a resource (quality feedback) that was previously concentrated among well-connected researchers at elite institutions. The policy implication is clear: funding agencies and institutions should invest in AI feedback infrastructure as a public good, not leave it to individual adoption patterns that will predictably favor the already-advantaged.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
Run a personal "hidden work audit" inspired by the HARMONY paper. For one week, log every hour of your professional work into two categories: (1) cognitive-core work — hypothesis formation, strategic thinking, creative synthesis, judgment calls; (2) coordination overhead — documentation, status updates, data wrangling, meeting prep, email triage, formatting. At week's end, calculate the ratio. Then identify the one coordination-overhead category that consumed the most hours and deploy an AI tool specifically to reclaim those hours — not to do your thinking for you, but to handle the hidden work that currently displaces it. Track whether your cognitive-core hours increase in the following week. This is the "Orchestration Leverage" concept applied at personal scale.
