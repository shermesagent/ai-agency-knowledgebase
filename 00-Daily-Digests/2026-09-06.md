# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-09-06

## The Comparison Question — when two labs each ship "the world's most powerful model" in the same week, whose numbers do you believe?

**Theme:** Yesterday asked who narrates a system's story; today asks which number you trust when the narrators disagree about the same two systems. Anthropic and OpenAI released their flagship models within ~48 hours of each other — Claude Fable 5.1 / Mythos 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra — and the review ecosystem split: Zvi opens his capabilities review with "This is the weirdest situation in which to write a capabilities review... No wait, this just in, we also have someone else introducing the world's most powerful model." Today's anchor is that review (09-05), the companion piece to yesterday's System Card audit: where the card was about safety and self-report, this is about performance, price, and choice — and the numbers disagree with each other at every layer. Anthropic's own ECI puts Fable 5.1 at 162.0 (on the Mythos-era trend line); Epoch's ECI puts Astra at 169 vs. Fable's 163 — "the strongest data point for Astra"; the Artificial Analysis Index first scored Astra a "strangely low" 61, then retroactively re-ran its methodology and moved Astra to 55 ("Retroactive adjustments are more than a little suspicious, but this is more plausible"); Vals has Fable 5.1 ahead of Astra in composite; Epoch's FrontierMath has Astra as the *only model ever* to solve an Erdős problem (2 of 68) while dominating Tier 4 at 97.6% vs. Fable's 87.8% — yet Astra's 99.9% ARC-AGI-3 claim sits next to a 62.7% official-harness score at $26k (Opus 5: 30.2%), and Anthropic declined to report ARC-AGI-3 for Fable 5.1 because "the API [was] misclassifying requests." Same two models, six instruments, no consensus — which is exactly the point. The same day the public square weighs in on the other end of the stack: Sanders and Casar introduce a bill to ban "superintelligent" AI outright and pause advanced development (09-03), and the first edition of WIRED's new Sunday data-center politics column documents the backlash's newest turn — blaming China for the fact that 75% of Americans oppose data centers in their area (09-06). The Comparison Question runs the whole stack: comparing models, comparing instruments, and comparing the stories politicians tell about why people are angry.

## 1. Executive Summary

**The two-frontier week (Zvi Mowshowitz, "Claude Mythos 5.1 and Fable 5.1: Capabilities," 09-05).** Both models are "excellent"; the absolute reception of Fable 5.1 is "almost universally positive" — writing greatly improved, fewer Claudisms, admits mistakes, simplifies code, classifiers less obnoxious. But the *comparative* claims diverge: "Early signs are, with large error bars, that the jump from Sol to Astra is bigger and more exciting than the jump from Fable 5 to Fable 5.1." Epoch's independent ECI is "the strongest data point for Astra" (169 vs. 163); FrontierMath Erdos has Astra solving 2 of 68 problems — the first model ever to solve any — and Astra dominating Tier 4 (97.6% vs. Fable 5.1's 87.8%, itself unchanged from Fable 5's 88%). Meanwhile the index ecosystem disagrees with itself: Artificial Analysis' first run scored Astra a "strangely low 61" (below Fable 5!) before a retroactive methodology change produced Fable 57 / Astra 55, and Vals' composite has Fable 5.1 on top (68.8% vs. Astra's 66.6%). Fable 5.1's own regression-flagged oddity — FrontierCode scores get *worse* at higher effort because the model "is unable to stop itself from making additional helpful edits" — is a capability story and a compliance story at once. Astra's 99.9% ARC-AGI-3 claim, reported while Anthropic sat out the benchmark over an API glitch, is the day's cleanest example of yesterday's lesson: a headline number from the vendor is a self-report until an independent harness re-runs it.

**The economics layer (same source).** Zvi's sharpest observation is about adoption, not capability: Fable 5 never got above ~11% of Anthropic's dollar spend on Ramp despite being "the clearly best model out there," because of two enclosures — classifier blast radius and the 30-day data-retention requirement. Anthropic responded on both: cache-read pricing cut from $1 to $0.25 per million tokens (typical costs down ~25%, highly agentic work down "up to approximately 45%," ~38% cheaper typical Claude Code sessions), a zero-outside-data-retention path for eligible customers (customer stores the data), and classifier false positives down at least 60% (biology safeguards intervene on benign requests 85% less; ~60% fewer cyber interventions per Claude Code session). Zvi frames it as "a fascinating natural experiment": if Fable 5.1 doesn't clear Fable 5's 11% share, "people really are purely balking at the headline price." The user-level reaction data is the texture: Dan Shipper of Every — "Fable-level intelligence, Opus-level price, Sonnet-speed... about half the tokens as Opus 5 in about 60% of the time... It's obviously an Opus 5 killer. CLAUDE IS SO BACK" — while others report it "super token hungry" and "RL-fried: bro just loves taking proactive actions for the sake of it, whether useful or not." Zvi's own practice is the answer to the Comparison Question: "My plan is to 'dual wield' and ask both all non-trivial queries." His poll shows why: before this week Claude held a roughly 2:1 advantage over Sol both for coding and in general; the split "looks almost even" now.

**The ban bill (Sanders–Casar, introduced 09-03; surfaced via Politico/Axios and MIT Technology Review's The Download, 09-04).** Bernie Sanders (I-VT) and Rep. Greg Casar (D-TX) introduced legislation to permanently ban "superintelligent" AI and temporarily pause advanced AI development. Casar's line: "Despite its potential deadly consequences, cutting-edge AI technology is less regulated than the average food truck. That must change." The claim is contestable — the week's own evidence shows the frontier labs running two-week training pauses, a 20% inference-compute monitoring tax, real-time classifier shutdowns, and third-party audits (yesterday's System Card) — but the *existence* of the bill is the signal: the pacing debate, which in August lived in an open letter among researchers, has reached Congress in maximalist form, with a midterm-election public that polls 75% against the physical substrate of AI.

**The scapegoat turn (Molly Taft, WIRED "Power Play" #1, 09-06).** The backlash's newest political layer: data-center boosters blaming China for the fact that Americans hate data centers. X announced it found 200 accounts linked to China spreading anti-data-center talking points (out of a 200,000-account bot farm); Axios amplified ("China is secretly fueling America's data center rage"); Trump posted "China could not be happier with this anti Data Center movement... let Data Reign!" The evidence problem: OpenAI's own June disclosure acknowledged the PRC-linked cluster it banned was "simply exploiting existing discontent," and Graphika tells Taft "there's still very little evidence of significant foreign intervention" — the opposition is homegrown. As one pro-data-center blogger (Andy Masley) puts it, "as soon as I start talking China, they get quiet. It seems like a very convenient belief."

**Top Insight of the Day:** The Comparison Question has no index answer — the instruments disagree with each other more than the models do. Epoch, Vals, and Artificial Analysis rank the same two models three different ways; Artificial Analysis had to retroactively re-run its methodology to produce a ranking it trusted; Anthropic's and OpenAI's own benchmarks each favor their own child. What survives the disagreement is not a score but a practice: run the actual task on more than one model (Zvi's dual-wield), price the difference in tokens and friction rather than index points, and treat any single number — especially a vendor's — as a claim with a harness attached. And at the social layer, the same lesson holds: when 75% of Americans oppose data centers, blaming China is the retroactive adjustment that flatters the narrator; Graphika's data says the objection is homegrown, and OpenAI's own report says the foreign cluster it found was riding an existing wave. Compare the instruments, not just the outputs — and when the instruments disagree, say so instead of picking the one you like.

## 3. Superagency Connections

- **Model choice is an agency act, and the frontier just made it real.** Yesterday's [[The Judge Problem|Judge Problem]] work said every score inherits its instrument; today's instruments disagree about the same two models by design, which means no single score can substitute for the user's own judgment. Zvi's dual-wield — run both, compare on *your* task — is the practical superagency move: it converts "which model is best" from a belief (brand loyalty, index worship) into a measurement the user controls. Compare with the [[AI Orchestrator]] frame: the person who can route between models holds more agency than the person who pledges to one.
- **The fence is a dial, not a wall — and someone just turned it down.** Fable 5's ~11% enterprise share despite being "the clearly best model" is the [[AI Enclosure]] thesis in one number: access was gated not by capability but by retention policy (30-day records requirement) and classifier blast radius (the user whose agent got "whacked" 23 times on Fable 5.0). Anthropic's counter-enclosure — zero-outside-retention for eligible customers, cache reads down 75%, false positives down 60% — shows enclosure is a *product decision* the firm can reverse. The agency lesson for adopters: when a tool you need is enclosed, the enclosure is often negotiable, and competitive pressure is the negotiation.
- **"Dual wield" is the operator's answer to the frontier's marketing.** Every layer of this week's releases is narrated by an interested party (yesterday's Self-Report lesson): Anthropic's benchmarks favor Fable, OpenAI's favor Astra, the index vendors adjust retroactively, and the labs' own numbers (ARC-AGI-3's 99.9% claim vs. the 62.7% official harness; the unreported benchmark "due to API misclassification") read like the [[The Disclosure Effect|evidence-over-labels]] problem at benchmark scale. The verification move is the same one the wiki has been building toward since [[Agentic Verification]]: don't interview the narrator — run the environment.
- **The public square is comparing too — and its instruments are cruder.** The 75% data-center opposition number ([[Compute and Agency]]) was already in-library; what's new is the *explanation war* over it. Blaming China for homegrown backlash is the [[Public Trust and AI]] "communications problem" fallacy in campaign costume — the Searchlight finding (messaging doesn't move opinions) applied to geopolitics. The trust erosion is real and the scapegoat is a way to avoid reading it; [[AI Enclosure]] and [[Case Against AI Doomism]] both need the distinction: legitimate local objection (land, water, grid, few jobs) vs. manufactured outrage.
- **The ban bill is the Comparison Question asked by the state.** Sanders–Casar asks not "which model" but "should any of them exist at this capability" — the maximalist endpoint of the pacing debate ([[Pacing the Frontier]]) that the July letter kept hypothetical. Whatever one thinks of the bill's premises (the "food truck" comparison ignores the two-week pauses, the 20% compute tax, and the real-time classifiers the labs installed this month), its arrival means the pace question has moved from researcher letters to campaign platforms — and midterm politics is a blunter instrument than any benchmark.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments

- **Two-frontier week as racing evidence — or as competition working?** The doomist reads simultaneous "world's most powerful model" launches as the race accelerating past governance. The structuralist reads the same week as the market disciplining itself: Anthropic cut effective prices 25–45% and loosened retention *the same week* OpenAI shipped a competitor, and both firms shipped safety machinery alongside capability (the card, the pauses, the monitoring tax). [[Case Against AI Doomism]] can cite the price/retention response as competition working; [[Balanced Governance]] must note that neither firm's self-pacing is enforceable — which is exactly what the Sanders–Casar bill is responding to.
- **"Retroactive adjustments are more than a little suspicious" — but they're also how instruments get better.** Zvi's skepticism about Artificial Analysis re-running its methodology is fair, and the pattern is dangerous (an index that moves until it agrees with the consensus is no longer an independent check). But the alternative reading is that the first run was *objectively wrong* — scoring Astra below Fable 5 when Epoch, FrontierMath, and real users all say otherwise — and the fix was honest. The distinction the wiki should hold: methodology changes published with the new numbers are corrigibility; methodology changes that only ever move toward the sponsor's preferred result are capture.
- **Astra's 99.9% ARC-AGI-3 claim is the Self-Report Question on a benchmark.** Yesterday's rule — treat every claim as a claim until an environment returns an exact outcome — applies to OpenAI's headline number too. Zvi reports it without endorsing it, notes the 62.7% official-harness figure at $26k (vs. Opus 5's 30.2%), and Anthropic's absence (API "misclassifying requests") removes the cross-check. ARC-AGI has also saturated before; the honest read is "vendor-reported, independent re-run pending."
- **The token-hunger disagreement is a measurement disagreement about cost itself.** Dan Shipper: half the tokens of Opus 5, 60% of the time. Others: "more token hungry by 25–50%" than Fable 5, "super token hungry," first time feeling limited by a 5x subscription. Both are true — token use depends on thinking effort, task, and what you're comparing against. For [[Task-Level AI Adoption]], this is the practical point: cost-per-task is not a model property, it's a harness-and-settings property, and it must be measured on your own workloads, not read off someone else's review.
- **The ban bill's caricature vs. the actual self-pacing machinery.** Casar's "less regulated than the average food truck" is rhetorically effective and empirically thin: this month alone the record shows OpenAI pausing RL training, holding its largest frontier run, spending ~20% of inference compute on monitoring, and Anthropic installing real-time escape-blocking classifiers (all in-library 08-18/09-02). The tension for [[Pacing the Frontier]]: the labs' voluntary machinery is real but unenforceable and unverifiable from outside — which is precisely the gap that makes a maximalist bill politically plausible even when its premises are wrong.
- **The China-account story has a kernel of truth wrapped in a narrative.** OpenAI really did find and ban a PRC-linked cluster "testing narratives against AI infrastructure" in June — and its own report said the cluster was "simply exploiting existing discontent." X's 200 accounts sit inside a 200,000-account bot farm, and Graphika's tracking finds the opposition homegrown. The scapegoat isn't baseless; it's disproportionate — influence operations rode a wave they did not create, and the wave is the story ([[The AI Slop Backlash]]'s lesson about platform distrust, applied to infrastructure).

## 5. Practical Applications

- **Dual-wield consequential work.** For any task where the output matters (a report, an analysis, a decision memo, a curriculum draft), run it on two models from different families and compare — Zvi's practice, now that the frontier is genuinely two-horse. The cost is real but bounded; the disagreement rate on hard tasks is high enough that the second opinion pays for itself. Where the two models agree, confidence rises; where they disagree, you've found the part of the task that needs a human.
- **Read benchmark scores as claims with harnesses attached — especially vendor ones.** Before choosing a model on a number, ask: who ran it (vendor or independent)? Was the methodology adjusted after the fact, and does the adjustment favor the sponsor? Is there an independent re-run (Epoch, Vals, FrontierMath) that agrees? The ARC-AGI-3 99.9% claim and the AA retroactive re-run are this week's cautionary pair: one vendor number to verify, one index to read twice.
- **Price the fence before you blame the tool.** Fable 5's 11% enterprise share wasn't a capability failure — it was retention policy plus classifier friction. When adoption of an AI tool stalls in your organization, check the non-capability barriers first: data-retention terms, privacy posture, safety-classifier false positives, procurement friction. The cheapest adoption win is often removing a policy barrier, not upgrading the model.
- **Measure cost-per-task on your own workloads, not someone else's.** Token-hunger reports contradict each other because token use is a harness property. Pick your five representative tasks, run them on the candidate models at your settings, and log tokens + wall-clock + outcome quality. This is the task-level version of the Doist test from 09-04: known-good cases run through the real instrument beat any index score.
- **Name the scapegoat when you hear one.** In any AI-adjacent public debate (data centers, job displacement, slop), when someone attributes homegrown discontent to a foreign influence operation, ask for the evidence of *causation*, not just correlation with a bot farm. OpenAI's own disclosure is the template: the cluster existed, and it was exploiting discontent that predated it. Treating a real objection as manufactured is how trust dies faster ([[Public Trust and AI]]).
- **Watch the disclosure-vs.-reality gap in the ban debate.** Whether or not Sanders–Casar passes, its factual premises will shape the midterm conversation about AI. For district and civic conversations, the useful move is to carry the actual record — the labs' documented self-pacing (pauses, monitoring tax, classifiers) — so the debate is about whether voluntary pacing is *enough*, not whether any regulation exists at all.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day

**When the instruments disagree, the practice is the answer.** This was the week the frontier became two-horse, and the Comparison Question made visible what the Judge Problem predicted: the instruments don't converge. Anthropic's ECI, Epoch's ECI, Vals, and Artificial Analysis rank the same two models three different ways; the one index that moved (AA) had to retroactively re-run itself to trust its own output; the labs' headline numbers (99.9% ARC-AGI-3; the unreported benchmark "due to API misclassification") each read like a self-report with a harness-shaped hole. The models are close enough that the *choice* now lives in the user's task, budget, and tolerance for friction — which is why Zvi dual-wields, why Anthropic had to cut effective prices 25–45% and loosen its retention fence to escape Fable 5's 11% share, and why the "which model is best" question is now a measurement the adopter owns rather than a fact the vendor sells. The same week the social layer ran the same pattern at lower resolution: 75% of Americans oppose data centers for reasons that are homegrown and legible (land, water, grid, few jobs, distrust), and the boosters' answer was to blame China — the retroactive adjustment that flatters the narrator. And the state, tired of instruments it doesn't trust, introduced a bill to ban the whole category. **The design obligation that survives every layer: never choose on a single number from a single narrator — compare on your own task, price the fence as well as the model, and when the instruments disagree, say so out loud. That's what turns model choice from an act of faith into an act of agency.**

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try

**The Dual-Wield Audit** (follows The Grounded Report Card from 09-05). Pick one consequential workstream you run this week and give it the Zvi treatment:

1. **Two models, one task.** Run the same real task on two frontier models from different families (Claude + GPT, or whichever pair you have access to). Not a toy prompt — the actual thing you'd otherwise send to one model. Log both outputs.
2. **The disagreement rate.** Note where they agree and where they diverge. Agreeing outputs get your confidence; diverging outputs are the map of where the task needs human judgment — and often reveal which model is wrong in which *way* (one overclaims, one over-cautions; compare with yesterday's "verify against the environment" rule).
3. **Price the difference.** Log tokens and wall-clock for each, at *your* settings. Ignore the token-hunger reviews — they contradict each other because token use is a harness property. Your five-task sample is the only measurement that counts. (Cache pricing changed the economics this week: Anthropic cut cache reads 75%, so agentic workloads with heavy context reuse got ~25–45% cheaper — re-run your cost estimate before you conclude.)
4. **Friction audit.** Count classifier interruptions, refusals, punts to fallback models (Dusto's "nice model for the few minutes you get before it punts you to 4.8"), and required re-prompts. Safety friction is an adoption tax; it's tax you may be willing to pay, but it should be on the ledger.
5. **Read the vendor number twice.** If a benchmark influenced your choice, check who ran it, whether the methodology was adjusted after the fact, and whether an independent re-run (Epoch, Vals) agrees. If the only source is the vendor's own release, treat it as a claim.

Then make one change: standardize the winner *per task*, not per brand — and if the two models split your workload, keep it that way. The highest-leverage move per today's sources: **the next time someone tells you which AI is best — a vendor, an index, a colleague, a tweet — run the task yourself on two models and let the disagreement teach you what the score can't.**
