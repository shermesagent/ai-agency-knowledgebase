## Top 5 Findings

### 1. Faster AI, Uneven Frontier: The Theoretical Backbone

**[arXiv 2607.12125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12125)** — Between 2023 and 2026, frontier AI crossed human expert baselines on graduate-level science, competition math, software engineering, and diagnostic reasoning. Task length at 50% reliability doubled roughly every 7 months. But the frontier is **jagged**: humans retain decisive advantages in long-horizon reliability, genuinely novel problems, calibrated self-knowledge, sample-efficient learning, and embodied action.

**The critical finding for [[Superagency]]:** Naive human-AI collaboration often *underperforms* the stronger partner. The human role must be repositioned — from task execution toward **specification, verification, and oversight.** This shift is visible in experiments but barely visible in field labor-market data.

**Why it matters:** This is the most comprehensive synthesis of the Co-Existence thesis to date. It validates what yesterday's Abstention Layer revealed — agents don't know when to stop — and extends it: humans don't know when to override. Both gaps must be designed for, not hoped away.

→ Connects to: [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[The Turing Trap]], [[Human Agency]]

### 2. A Theory of Least Autonomy in AI

**[arXiv 2607.09744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09744)** — Least privilege (grant only required permissions) has been foundational to access control for decades. But agentic AI doesn't just *hold* permissions — it can **combine, approve, and amplify** them across workflows and system boundaries.

The paper proposes **least autonomy** as a generalization:
- **Compositional blast radius** — measures structural separation between actions in an enterprise hierarchy
- **Agent influence graph** — directed arcs representing shared-resource and agent-to-agent communication channels
- **Collusion predicate** — detects authorization composition, decision manipulation, and cross-domain capability composition

**Why it matters:** This is the security architecture for the agent economy. Current access control treats each permission as independent. Agentic systems can chain them together in ways no human auditor can predict. The collusion predicate makes visible what least privilege hides — that permissions in combination are more dangerous than permissions in isolation.

→ Connects to: [[AI Agent Revolution]], [[Responsible Deployment]], [[Balanced Governance]]

### 3. Designing Agent-Ready Websites: 89.3% vs. 49.3% Success Rate

**[arXiv 2607.12056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12056)** — As AI agents independently search, compare, and purchase for users, websites must serve both human and agent users. A controlled experiment (300 runs, 3 browser-agent models, 5 tasks) compared human-oriented and agent-ready versions of identical website prototypes.

**Results:** The agent-ready website achieved **89.3% strict success** (134/150 PASS) vs. **49.3%** (74/150) for the baseline. Average steps dropped from 9.31 to 6.49. Partial outcomes fell from 43 to 3. The largest gains came in product detail extraction, comparison, and multi-constraint selection.

**Why it matters:** This is the plumbing. Agent capability is useless if the digital environment is illegible to agents. The 89.3% vs. 49.3% gap means the agent-commerce revolution will roll out unevenly — retailers who build agent-ready infrastructure will capture agent-mediated traffic. Those who don't, won't. This is an infrastructure-deployment race, not a capability race.

→ Connects to: [[AI Agent Revolution]], [[Task-Level AI Adoption]], [[Frontier Firm]]

### 4. Underwriting the Agent Economy: An 8-Component Insurance Stack

**[arXiv 2607.11999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11999)** — Insurance has historically been the enabler of major technological developments by pricing risk, limiting downside, and spreading best practices. The emerging AI agent economy, projected to handle trillions in transactions by 2030, needs the same.

The paper lays out an **8-component AI insurance stack**: incident data collection, catastrophe modeling, standards, contract design, risk selection, pricing, monitoring, and claims management. It draws on historical precedents: Underwriters Laboratories for electrical safety, the Closed Claims Project for medical malpractice.

**Why it matters:** Insurance is governance through economics. It doesn't prohibit — it prices. If AI agent risk is correctly priced, the market allocates agent deployment to the use cases where benefits exceed risks. If incorrectly priced (or "silently" covered in existing policies), risks accumulate invisibly until a catastrophic claim breaks the system. The paper also addresses "AI CAT" — catastrophic risk from frontier AI including CBRN, infrastructure collapse, and loss of control.

→ Connects to: [[AI Agent Revolution]], [[Balanced Governance]], [[Risk-Benefit Matrix]]

### 5. Policy-as-Prompt Moderation: Prompts Alone Aren't Governance

**[arXiv 2607.12149](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12149)** — As community moderation shifts from centralized human experts to AI-assisted volunteer communities, a "policy-as-prompt" approach emerges: write the community rules as a prompt, give it to an LLM, and let the LLM moderate. The paper finds that this is insufficient for meaningful community governance.

**Key insight:** LLMs can *apply* policy but cannot *deliberate* about it. They lack the interpretive community — the shared understanding of what rules mean in context, the negotiated exceptions, the evolving norms — that makes community governance legitimate. Writing prompts alone doesn't ensure meaningful governance.

**Why it matters:** This is the community-scale version of the infrastructure problem. Just as websites need to be redesigned for agents, governance systems need to be redesigned for AI-mediated decision-making. The AI can be the *mechanism* — it cannot be the *authority.*

→ Connects to: [[Government and Civic Life]], [[Balanced Governance]], [[Public Trust and AI]]

## Synthesis: The Infrastructure Layer

The arc connecting today's papers is infrastructure design. Last week's themes (Persuasion, Control, Abstention) were about *agent behavior.* Today's theme is about the *operating environment* within which agents behave:

| Layer | What It Addresses | Today's Paper |
|---|---|---|
| **Web infrastructure** | Can agents navigate the digital world? | Agent-Ready Websites (2607.12056) |
| **Access control** | What can agents do once they're there? | Least Autonomy (2607.09744) |
| **Economic governance** | Who bears the cost when agents fail? | Agent Economy Insurance (2607.11999) |
| **Community governance** | Who sets the rules agents enforce? | Policy-as-Prompt Moderation (2607.12149) |
| **Human judgment** | Where do humans add value? | Faster AI, Uneven Frontier (2607.12125) |

The common failure mode: designing agents for capability and hoping the infrastructure catches up. The common fix: infrastructure as a first-class design object with the same rigor applied to capability.

This extends yesterday's **Abstention Layer** theme — agents that don't know when *not* to act — into a positive design program. The abstention gap isn't just about agent training; it's about the environment constraining what agents can attempt. An agent with well-designed infrastructure (permission boundaries, insurance pricing, community norms, human oversight checkpoints) doesn't need to be perfect at abstention — the environment catches what the agent misses.

## Practical Experiment: Website Agent-Readiness Audit

This week's experiment: run an agent-readiness audit on 3 websites you regularly use for purchases or research.

1. Pick 3 websites where you make consequential decisions (purchases, subscriptions, research)
2. For each, ask an AI agent (Claude with web browsing, ChatGPT browsing, or similar) to:
   - Find a specific product with constraints (price range, features, compatibility)
   - Compare 3 alternatives on specified dimensions
   - Identify the return policy or warranty terms
3. Rate each site on agent-friendliness (1-5) based on whether the agent succeeded reliably
4. Note which sites seem *designed* for agents vs. those that clearly aren't

**What to learn:** The agent-commerce experience gap isn't theoretical — it's practical and widening fast. Understanding which sites are agent-ready tells you whose commerce infrastructure is ahead of the curve and where friction remains. This is a leading indicator for which businesses are preparing for an agent-mediated economy.
