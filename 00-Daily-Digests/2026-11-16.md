# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-16

## Executive Summary
**A fluent proxy is not the same as a person's voice.** Five new arXiv preprints sharpen a practical distinction: AI can help people participate, inspect decisions and manage their own information, but the evaluation must measure whose viewpoint survives, what can be contested and what a permission actually permits. The papers vary from benchmark and controlled computational studies to a proposed legal framework and a five-city measurement instrument; none establishes universal deployment outcomes.

## Top 5 Findings / Best Sources Found
1. **Synthetic policy comments may not represent actual commenters.** [Spangher et al., DOCKETBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17914) assemble 42.3 million comments across 22,400 federal regulations and report that only 18–30% of model-generated feedback aligns with real human comments in their benchmark. The models often lack specific evidence and lived experience. This is a benchmark comparison, not proof that all AI-assisted public comments misrepresent their authors. → [[The Participation Problem]], [[Agency Expansion Framework]].
2. **A narrow disclosure can still expose an accountability gap.** [Cofone and Strandburg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17980) propose publishing group-level error profiles and outcome distributions as triggers for deeper disclosure, rather than treating secrecy as all-or-nothing. This is a normative principal–agent framework, not a tested law or a guarantee of fairness. → [[Public Trust and AI]].
3. **One allowed/blocked memory preview does not establish the permission rule.** [Tonni and Shahid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17874) show formal ambiguity in a specified rule vocabulary; in a 20-person pilot, 10 participants given a weak preview mispredicted or were unsure about at least one of two uses. Whether improved previews help people remains untested. → [[Agency Expansion Framework]], [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]].
4. **Automatic forgetting can discard facts the user still needs.** [Vinayaka](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17338) reports a controlled model/task evaluation in which query-critical false deletion hurts downstream accuracy more than false retention under tested conditions. This supports evaluating deletion against matched queries, not a universal retain-everything rule; privacy and user deletion rights still matter. → [[Agency Expansion Framework]].
5. **Measure who speaks before claiming participation.** [Liu, Wang and Shi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18072) analyze 1,565 city-council meetings from five cities after quality control and report public speakers received a median 10% of floor time; their classifier achieved 87.7% accuracy on the evaluated speaker set. Audio-based measurement is promising, but diarization errors, site selection and who is counted limit generalization. → [[The Participation Problem]], [[Government and Civic Life]].

## Superagency Connections
[[Superagency]] means not simply more generated speech but a better ability for people to express their own interests, verify a decision and revise a delegated system's boundaries. A civic assistant should retain the original human argument; a memory assistant should let the user inspect cross-task reuse and correct or erase records; a public agency should offer meaningful recourse, not just a model description.

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- AI drafting can lower the expertise barrier to a technical comment; banning it could exclude people who need assistance. The safeguard is user-authored intent and verifiable citations, not a blanket prohibition.
- Publishing granular error metrics may risk privacy, gaming or unreliable group estimates. Stage disclosure and document measurement limits; do not infer intent or wrongdoing from disparities alone.
- Keeping more agent memories to protect answer accuracy can conflict with data minimization. Give users meaningful deletion controls and test both privacy and task-critical false-drop costs.
- Floor-time share is a measure of speaking, not influence, representative participation or decision quality.

## Practical Applications
- For an AI-assisted comment workflow, preserve the person's original statement alongside the draft, require source checking and ask the author whether the final comment still expresses their position.
- For an agent memory interface, show concrete allowed, blocked and cross-task-transfer cases; provide revoke/correct controls and test them before sensitive rollout.
- For consequential automated decisions, maintain group-level outcome/error dashboards where lawful and statistically supportable, plus a named appeal owner.

## Top Insight of the Day
**The unit of agency is the person's revisable intention, not the model's fluent output.** Measure whether assistance preserves the person's voice and rights to inspect, correct, opt out and contest.

## Practical Experiment to Try
In a low-stakes internal policy exercise (no actual filing and no student or sensitive personal data), ask a willing adult to write a short position on a hypothetical rule, then have an assistant produce an optional draft using only public source material. The adult reviews the draft and marks each claim as *mine / changed / unsupported*, checks each citation and records whether they would sign it. Compare the revised version with their original position; do not submit AI-generated mass comments. **Success:** no unsupported citations, no unapproved changes of position and a documented way to reject the draft. Otherwise revert to an ordinary writing aid. This is a proposed pilot, not a measured intervention.
