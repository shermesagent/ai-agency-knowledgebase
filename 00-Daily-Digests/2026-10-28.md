**The Full-Cost-and-Authority Test** · Wednesday curator pass · Four distinct sources (two essays/articles, two preprints); five findings are not five independent studies.

## Executive Summary
AI assistance can look successful at launch and still fail the people who must trust, check and defend its work. Today's reported publishing rollout, research on the cost of missed errors, scientific-delegation framework and external-evaluator debate point to one useful rule: count the *whole* cost of assistance, and keep human authority over both the consequential work and the evidence used to judge it.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **A festive launch is not a useful workflow.** WIRED reports that Macmillan introduced NOA in 2024 and sunset it in 2025; several staff called it unreliable. Macmillan disputes aspects of the rollout description. Measure opt-in use, time spent checking, and whether the person doing the work can reject the tool. → [[Work]], [[AI Use Case Evaluation Rubric]].
2. **A reported hallucinated quotation can cost someone else.** An author interviewed by WIRED says marketing copy included invented quotations; that is an account, not a measured incident rate. Approval of manuscript use and a source-to-copy check belong upstream of publication. → [[AI and Creator Rights]], [[Work]].
3. **Time saved can hide errors released.** Lijo's abstract reports that in its small AI-reviewer experiment a low-error-rate claim reduced checking by seven of nine figures and let a wrong number through in 22.5% of briefs versus none under the comparison condition. The METR reanalysis gives different reported slowdown estimates depending on the estimand. Neither result licenses a general productivity claim. → [[AI Use Case Evaluation Rubric]], [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]].
4. **A proof is not the same as a scientific community that can use it.** Heidari and Mohseni argue that humans must retain capacity to explain, defend and contest results and authority to direct research questions. This is a design proposal, not a trial. → [[AI-Augmented Scientific Collaboration]], [[The Comprehension Bottleneck]].
5. **An embedded evaluator needs usable independence.** Anthropic says six METR staff have employee-like access and METR can publish without company veto, subject to defined redactions that Anthropic ultimately judges; Zvi welcomes the access while challenging whether methods and institutional powers suffice. External observation is a step forward, but it does not itself confer stop authority. → [[Balanced Governance]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]].

## Superagency Connections
Capacity: assistance can draft, check and generate more research. Freedom: workers, creators and researchers must be able to decline, challenge and explain the result. Platforms: the tool owner sets data, publication and evaluator-access terms. Value: count saved effort against review, repair and credibility costs. Wisdom: preserve the human right to define the important question and interrupt a poor process. See [[Superagency]] and [[Human Agency]].

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- Worker interviews can expose a failure hidden by launch metrics, but one contested case cannot estimate enterprise-AI effectiveness. The same tool might work in a different workflow with clearer data permissions and staff choice.
- Defect-cost ledgers require assumptions about downstream harms; estimating a break-even cost is more honest than declaring zero, but uncertain costs must be reported as ranges, not spurious precision.
- A human-understanding requirement can become a blanket veto on useful machine-aided discovery. The proposal's value is task-by-task delegation with public correction, not pretending people must perform every calculation themselves.
- Embedded assessors can see more than outsiders. Confidentiality and legitimate IP protection matter; without independently checkable access, redaction and action rights, however, a favorable review should not be mistaken for a binding safety certificate.

## Practical Applications
For one **low-risk, nonconfidential, volunteer** workflow, compare an unaided draft with an AI-assisted draft on elapsed time, verification time, source-grounded errors, corrections, participant preference and a named human sign-off. Use public-source material and teacher-authored non-identifiable test tasks only—not student records, author manuscripts or employee performance data. For any external evaluation, publish a short terms card: information the assessor can inspect, right to publish, who decides redactions and who can pause the system.

## Top Insight of the Day
**The advertised unit of value is output; the real unit is an outcome that someone can check, defend, correct and choose to stop.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
Run a **full-cost-and-authority trial** on five publicly sourced, low-stakes briefs. Randomly assign the first draft to human-only or AI-assisted work; keep one consistent source-check rubric. Track drafting and checking minutes, unsupported claims that survive review, corrections required after sign-off, and whether the reviewer could reject the AI draft without penalty. Have a second person blind-check consequential claims. Stop if sensitive material would be needed. Do not treat five briefs as an efficacy estimate; use failures to revise the workflow before any broader pilot.
