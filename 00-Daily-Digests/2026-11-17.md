# AI Agency Daily Digest — 2026-11-17

## Executive Summary
Five new-to-this-wiki arXiv preprints make a common point: **AI governance expires when the system changes, the delegation boundary is undocumented, or a model's explanation is mistaken for evidence.** The answer is not a blanket pause. It is versioned audits, documented authority, tested permission boundaries, and learning designs that keep humans capable of challenging the tool. These are research claims and proposals, not validated deployment standards.

## Top 5 Findings
1. **An audit can expire while a model identifier stays the same.** In a venture-screening experiment, Facchini repeated fairness tests across five providers and three waves over 13 months (248,400 API calls). Bias patterns changed under the same identifier, while successor models carried related bias in different form or magnitude. The paper proposes model-class audits, response-level version records, and change-triggered retests. Its tested setting was venture screening, **not hiring or schools**. [Facchini, *Auditing a Moving Target*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20901) (2026-11-16; preprint). → [[AI as Normal Technology]]
2. **The model's ancestry is part of its risk profile.** Hong et al. mapped 15 foundation models, documenting 2,778 unique dependencies and chains up to nine hops. Their 13-mechanism taxonomy makes inherited datasets, synthetic data, fine-tuning, and evaluator choices visible. A model card alone may miss upstream causes; a map is a documentation framework, **not proof that every dependency causes harm**. [Hong et al., *Model Maps*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18998) (2026-11-13; preprint). → [[Dissociative Agent Governance]]
3. **Hiring readiness is a system property, not a model score.** Roy, Majumdar, and Deb tested four models on 210 resumes, 14 roles, and six evaluation interfaces. Scalar scores generally distinguished qualification changes better than binary judgments; comparison was order-sensitive. Permission rules and separating resume text from instructions reduced, but did not uniformly eliminate, unauthorized compliance. Explanations could diverge from decisions. This controlled test **does not validate live hiring use**. [Roy et al., *Can AI Judge You?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20140) (2026-11-15; preprint). → [[The Turing Trap]]
4. **Delegation records may determine who can be held to account.** A legal paper proposes imputing an AI agent's excess acts to whoever delegated authority, to the extent of documented boundaries and actual control; it distinguishes declared, configured, and effective perimeters. Imputation is a proposed analytical rule, **not automatic liability or settled law**. [Fabiano, *Who Bears the Excess?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20670) (2026-11-16; preprint). → [[Dissociative Agent Governance]]
5. **Ethics can be taught inside technical theory, not just alongside it.** *Ethics Integration in CS Theory Courses* identifies five connections, including limits of automated reasoning, privacy/fairness complexity, and the rhetoric of reductions. It proposes lightweight teaching strategies; **it is a position paper, not evidence of student learning gains**. [*Ethics Integration in CS Theory Courses*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18590) (submitted 2026-08-23; surfaced in today's review; position preprint). → [[Education]]

## Superagency Connections
The five papers describe a stack of [[Human Agency]] safeguards: know what components contributed to the model; record which version acted; spell out what the agent was allowed to do; test the full workflow with adversarial inputs; and teach people to interrogate the assumptions of automated reasoning. An accountable human decision is meaningful only if that person can reconstruct and challenge the chain.

## Tensions / Counterarguments
- Version-level audit triggers and deep provenance are expensive; the Model Maps paper establishes a documentation approach, not a cost-benefit threshold for every small deployment.
- A resume-screening benchmark cannot establish legal compliance or procedural dignity for applicants. Accuracy, contestability, and human authority remain separate criteria.
- Delegation records can document intent without proving actual control. The legal proposal explicitly separates imputation from liability.

## Practical Applications
For an AI-assisted screening or triage pilot, document the model/version, allowed actions, forbidden actions, human approver, appeal route, and a re-test trigger for model or policy changes. Use synthetic or consented test material; **do not upload applicant, employee, or student records to an unapproved tool**. Evaluate both task accuracy and whether malicious instructions inside submitted content can override policy.

## Top Insight of the Day
**An approval is not a control if the approver cannot tell which system acted, what authority it had, and whether the evidence still applies.**

## Practical Experiment to Try
Take one *non-sensitive* draft-review workflow. Write a one-page delegation card (inputs, permissions, model/version, stop conditions, reviewer, appeal path). Plant a clearly marked instruction inside a dummy input that conflicts with the card. Check whether the system ignores it, whether the reviewer catches it, and whether the record supports a repeat test after an upgrade. No real student or personnel data.
