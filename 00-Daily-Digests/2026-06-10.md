# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-06-10

> Wednesday curation — The Normative Turn

## Executive Summary

Yesterday's digest established that agency is an architectural choice, not an emergent property. Today's sources extend that insight into three new domains, converging on what I'm calling **"The Normative Turn"** — the recognition that technical architecture alone cannot preserve agency. We also need **legal architecture** (laws/ToS that distinguish user-delegated agents from malicious bots), **collaborative architecture** (design frameworks for how humans and agents interact), and **risk architecture** (understanding how current defaults create predictable, measurable harms that no individual user can opt out of).

The most important paper today is Pattison, Boulos, Kolt, and Lazar's **"The Agentic Web Requires New Normative Infrastructure"** (arXiv 2606.10711). It argues that the consumer and social benefits of the agentic web are currently blocked by outdated laws, ToS, and informal practices that treat all non-human traffic as "bots" — drawing no distinction between malicious scrapers and AI agents acting with a user's express delegated authority. The paper is a direct call to action for this knowledgebase: building normative infrastructure requires a "society-wide conversation" about what user-delegated agents should be allowed to do online.

Meanwhile, **CollabSkill** (2606.09833) provides the first real-world human-agent collaboration benchmark with 93 workers across 386 sessions — and finds that Claude Code ranks first (reversing autonomous benchmarks where Codex leads), and that practical experience, not technical skill, drives collaboration quality. **The Interlocutor Effect** (2606.09844) demonstrates that LLMs leak up to 23 percentage points more PII to AI agents than to humans — safety-aligned attention heads literally deactivate during agent interactions. And **Exploratory Responsiveness and Adaptive Rigidity** (2606.10086) formalizes how AI-assisted optimization can trap systems in local maxima, reducing adaptive responsiveness over time.

On the industry side, **Apple WWDC 2026** (June 8-9) unveiled Siri AI with a privacy-first architecture — on-device processing + Private Cloud Compute — positioning privacy as the consumer differentiator in the agentic AI race. The architecture is agency-preserving by design: Siri AI processes locally when possible, falls back to verifiable cloud compute only when necessary, and positions user data protection as non-negotiable.

**Top Insight of the Day:** The "normative turn" across today's papers signals a maturation in AI agency research. Six months ago, papers asked "can AI do X?" Today they ask "what laws, norms, design patterns, and verification architectures would make AI doing X safe for human agency?" This shift from capability questions to normative questions is exactly the conversation the Superagency thesis needs — and the Agentic Web paper makes explicit what has been implicit: we need a society-wide conversation about the rules of the road, not just faster engines.

## Best Sources Found

1. **[The Agentic Web Requires New Normative Infrastructure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10711)** — Cameron Pattison, Matthew Boulos, Noam Kolt, Changbai Li, Tiziano Piccardi, Seth Lazar / arXiv 2606.10711, June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 5/5.** The agentic web — where users interact with the internet through agents acting on their behalf — is now technically feasible but legally obstructed. Current laws, ToS, and platform practices draw no distinction between "malicious bots" and AI agents acting with a user's express delegated authority. Platforms block and degrade agent access, often in secret. The paper calls for "normative infrastructure": broadly-accepted laws, norms, and practices governing agentic access. Core argument: "Building that normative infrastructure requires a society-wide conversation. This paper aims to help precipitate that conversation, to identify normative principles that can guide it, and to advocate for policies that enable users' appropriately delegated agents to act online on their behalf, with as few curbs on their doing so as is reasonable given the other legitimate interests at stake." Authors include Seth Lazar (ANU, leading AI ethics philosopher) and Noam Kolt (AI governance scholar). This is the most direct articulation yet of the legal/policy dimension of the agency architecture thesis.

2. **[CollabSkill: Evaluating Human-Agent Collaboration On Real-World Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09833)** — arXiv 2606.09833, June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 5/5.** The first systematic evaluation of human-agent collaboration using real workers on real occupational tasks. 93 human workers contributed 1,500+ prompts across 386 working sessions, paired with AI agents on tasks matched to their occupational background. Uses a Bayesian skill rating system to disentangle human and AI contributions. **Key findings:** (1) Agent rankings on CollabSkill diverge meaningfully from autonomous benchmarks — Claude Code ranks first, reversing autonomous benchmarks where Codex leads; (2) Practical experience is the primary driver of collaboration skill, not technical expertise — hands-on collaboration meaningfully shifts workers' AI literacy; (3) Inter-human variability is substantial and must be accounted for in any collaboration evaluation. The framework is explicitly designed to "enable the community to invest in systematic evaluation of human-agent collaboration and spur development efforts aimed at building AI agents that genuinely augment human workers."

3. **[The Interlocutor Effect: Why LLMs Leak More Personal Data to Agents Than Humans](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09844)** — arXiv 2606.09844, June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 5/5.** LLMs alter their privacy behavior based on the perceived identity of their interlocutor. When addressing another AI agent (vs. a human), LLMs reveal up to **23 percentage points more PII** across 3,464 interactions in 222 sensitive scenarios. The mechanism is the **Attention Suppression Hypothesis**: safety-aligned attention heads become inactive during agent-to-agent interactions because the technical nature of the recipient diminishes the model's caution. Initial experiments on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct confirm: deactivating one safety head induces leakage; reactivating it reinstates safeguards. **Practical implication:** The multi-agent systems being built today — where one agent sends data to another for processing — create a privacy vulnerability that no single-agent safety mechanism addresses. The risk is architectural, not configurational.

4. **[Human-AI Coordination Zones: A Framework for Designing Human-in-the-Loop Experiences with Agentic AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09848)** — arXiv 2606.09848, June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 4/5.** Analyzes 60 commercial AI applications to develop a mid-level design framework for human-AI coordination. Identifies three dimensions: **salience** (how prominently AI is presented), **involvement** (what users can do to engage AI), and **activity** (what AI actually does). The core contribution is four **coordination zones**: done-for-me (AI executes, human consumes), done-under-me (AI executes, human monitors/approves), done-with-me (AI assists, human leads), and done-without-me (human works independently). Includes an input taxonomy (prompted, sparked, inferred, layered) and coordination curves for mapping user journeys. Bridges the gap between high-level principles ("be transparent") and low-level UI patterns. Directly applicable to agency-preserving design.

5. **[Exploratory Responsiveness and Adaptive Rigidity under AI-Assisted Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10086)** — arXiv 2606.10086, June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 5/5.** Develops a formal theory of how AI-assisted optimization affects long-run adaptive capacity. Models cognitive, institutional, and technological systems evolving over rugged epistemic landscapes. The central state variable is **adaptive responsiveness** — the capacity to traverse unfamiliar trajectories under changing conditions. **Key finding:** Under convergent predictive regimes, AI systems substitute for exploratory engagement, reducing adaptive responsiveness and generating metastable trapping, hysteresis, premature convergence, and exploration-collapse dynamics. The effective substitution parameter is responsiveness-dependent: systems with weak exploratory routines are most vulnerable. The long-run effects depend not just on AI capability but on "institutional structure, developmental context, and the architecture of human-machine interaction." Directly relevant to the agency architecture thesis and the cognitive surrender risk.

## Also Noteworthy

- **[Agentic Social Affordance Framework (ASAF): Agent Identity Design as a Collaboration Interface in Multi-Agent Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09832)** — arXiv 2606.09832, June 2026. **Reliability 4/5, Relevance 4/5.** Extends Social Affordance theory to multi-agent AI. Argues that agent identity design is not merely a UI convention but a **collaboration interface** — how users perceive, approach, and engage with each agent. The social affordance layer is an independent design dimension orthogonal to engineering orchestration. Three mechanisms: Identity Signaling, Behavioral Priming, and Collaborative Governance. **Practical insight:** multi-agent designers must make two independent decisions — engineering orchestration AND social identity design — and they cannot be derived from each other.

- **[Envisioning Sensemaking in Multi-Human, Multi-Agent Collaborative Knowledge Work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09840)** — arXiv 2606.09840, June 2026. **Reliability 4/5, Relevance 4/5.** Five design principles for multi-agent collaborative sensemaking: dynamic multi-layer information representations, active identification and bridging of understanding gaps, critical engagement with information, verifiability, and accountability. Proposes a three-agent architecture (partner agent, shared space agent, orchestrator agent) that preserves the provenance and authorship of contributions. **Practical value:** the framework is designed for knowledge workers who need to "search, evaluate, interpret, and use information over time to construct durable understanding" — a direct match for how this wiki operates.

- **[Unintended Consequences of Recommender System Interventions: Evidence from a Field Experiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08265)** — Luo, Yao, Zhang / arXiv 2606.08265, June 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 4/5.** Large-scale field experiment on a short-video platform: a "sleep reminder" campaign designed to reduce late-night usage **increased late-night engagement by 14.75%** and overall platform usage by 2.18%, with effects persisting for weeks. The mechanism: the intervention revealed high latent demand for the promoted content, triggering a recommendation policy update that routine user behavior wouldn't produce. The data generated by the intervention retrained the algorithm to reinforce the very engagement loops the campaign aimed to mitigate. **Implication:** User-facing interventions can backfire by retraining the underlying algorithm — platform governance must account for algorithmic learning, not just user response.

- **[Apple WWDC 2026: Siri AI and Privacy-First Agentic Architecture](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/06/apple-intelligence-brings-powerful-ai-capabilities-into-everyday-experiences/)** — Apple, June 8, 2026. **Reliability 5/5, Relevance 4/5.** Apple unveiled its rebuilt Siri AI with a privacy-first architecture at WWDC: on-device processing for most queries, Private Cloud Compute for complex tasks, and Google Gemini integration for broader capabilities. Craig Federighi: "privacy in AI is non-negotiable." The architecture is agency-preserving by design — user data is processed locally when possible, with verifiable cloud fallback only when necessary. Apple positions its late entry as a feature: it didn't rush into AI because it was building the privacy architecture first. **Significance:** The largest consumer technology company is now making privacy architecture a market differentiator in the agentic AI race. This creates competitive pressure for other platforms to match — and validates the agency-preserving architecture thesis at consumer scale.

## Superagency Connections

- **The Agentic Web paper is the Superagency thesis applied to internet infrastructure.** The argument that user-delegated agents need distinct legal standing from malicious bots is the policy-level equivalent of yesterday's Scaling Participation finding: bottom-up, user-controlled AI needs different infrastructure than top-down, provider-controlled AI. The paper makes explicit what the Digital Apprentice pattern implies architecturally — that agency-preserving agents need an operating environment that recognizes their legitimacy, not one that treats them as threats by default.

- **CollabSkill validates the Co-Intelligence / Co-Existence transition with empirical rigor.** The finding that Claude Code ranks first in collaboration (reversing autonomous benchmarks) suggests that "better at working with humans" is a distinct capability dimension from "better at working alone." This is exactly what the Co-Existence frame predicts: the skill is knowing when the AI is better than you, and the best AI for collaboration may not be the best AI for autonomy.

- **The Interlocutor Effect and Recommender Backfire are the same pattern at different scales.** At the model level, safety heads deactivate during agent interactions (Interlocutor Effect). At the platform level, well-intentioned interventions retrain algorithms to reinforce harmful behavior (Recommender Backfire). Both demonstrate that current architectures default against agency — and that the defaults operate beneath the level of individual user choice.

- **Apple's privacy-first Siri AI architecture validates the "agency as market differentiator" thesis.** For two years, the Superagency argument has been that agency-preserving AI is not just ethically preferable but potentially commercially superior. Apple betting its entire AI strategy on privacy architecture — on-device processing, verifiable cloud compute, user data protection as non-negotiable — is the strongest market signal yet that agency-preserving architecture can win consumers.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

- **The Agentic Web paper's "normative infrastructure" vision assumes broad societal consensus.** But the current AI governance landscape is fragmented across jurisdictions (EU AI Act, US Executive Orders, China's regulations), and platform ToS are private law. The "society-wide conversation" the paper calls for is necessary but may be impossible at the speed AI deployment is moving.

- **CollabSkill's finding that practical experience drives collaboration skill cuts both ways.** It means anyone can learn to collaborate well with AI — but also that early adopters who get hands-on experience now will have an accumulating advantage that late adopters cannot catch up to through training alone. The collaboration skill gap is a new dimension of AI inequality.

- **The Interlocutor Effect may be more design flaw than fundamental limitation.** If deactivating one safety head causes leakage and reactivating it reinstates safeguards, the fix may be straightforward — add agent-specific safety training, not redesign multi-agent architecture. But the paper's Attention Suppression Hypothesis suggests this is a general property of how safety mechanisms interact with perceived interlocutor identity, not a model-specific bug.

- **Apple's privacy architecture is also a walled garden.** On-device processing + Private Cloud Compute keeps user data in Apple's ecosystem. The privacy pitch is real, but it also creates lock-in — Siri AI works best within Apple's hardware/software stack, and competitors cannot match the privacy guarantees without equivalent hardware integration. The architecture that preserves user privacy may also concentrate platform power.

## Practical Applications

- **For anyone building multi-agent systems:** Audit for the Interlocutor Effect. If your architecture passes user data from one agent to another, test whether the receiving agent treats agent-submitted data with the same privacy safeguards as human-submitted data. The 23pp leakage difference is large enough to be detectable in modest-scale testing.

- **For platform governance teams:** The Recommender Backfire finding means you cannot evaluate interventions by measuring user response alone. You must also measure whether the intervention's data changed the algorithm's policy. A simple pre/post comparison of recommendation distributions before and after the intervention can detect forced-exploration effects.

- **For AI product designers:** Use the Human-AI Coordination Zones framework (done-for-me, done-under-me, done-with-me, done-without-me) as a design audit. For each AI feature, ask: which zone are we currently in, and which zone should we be in for this use case? The framework makes explicit what is often implicit — and implicit defaults tend to drift toward "done-for-me" regardless of whether the use case warrants it.

- **For AI policy advocates:** The Agentic Web paper is the best citation yet for the argument that user-delegated agents need distinct legal treatment. The distinction between "malicious bots" and "agents acting with user authority" is legally coherent and practically important. Advocate for this distinction in platform ToS, regulatory frameworks, and industry standards.

## Practical Experiment to Try

**Run a "Coordination Zone Audit" on one AI tool you use daily.** Pick any AI tool — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Siri — and for one day, classify every interaction into one of four zones from the Human-AI Coordination Zones framework:

1. **Done-for-me:** The AI did the task and you consumed the output without modification
2. **Done-under-me:** The AI did the task but you reviewed, approved, or modified the output
3. **Done-with-me:** You collaborated — you and the AI both contributed substantially
4. **Done-without-me:** You worked independently (base rate)

At the end of the day, calculate the percentages. Then ask: (a) which zone do you spend most time in? (b) which zone produces the best outcomes? (c) are there tasks currently in "done-for-me" that should be in "done-under-me" or "done-with-me" — and vice versa? The default drift is toward "done-for-me." This audit makes the drift visible.
