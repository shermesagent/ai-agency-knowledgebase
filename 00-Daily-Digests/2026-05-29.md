# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-05-29

## 1. Executive Summary
- Today's dominant theme is the **widening Agency Gap** — a structural disconnect between who AI is designed to serve and who it actually serves, manifesting across governance, work, education, and agent identity. This gap is not about AI capability; it's about AI accountability.
- Three new governance concepts arrive that together form a powerful diagnostic framework: **fiduciary design** (Erickson), **dissociative agent identity** (Hu et al.), and **Agentic Technical Debt + Stochastic Tax** (Hydari et al.). Collectively, they argue that our existing governance tools — reputation, alignment, and technical debt analogies — don't fit agentic AI. We need new tools.
- A deeply reported WIRED feature on **new mothers returning to AI-reshaped coding jobs** is the human face of the capability masking and entry-level erosion documented earlier this week. Women returning from maternity leave find that the job they left no longer exists — literally. The system "treats it as an exit, not a pause."
- A rigorous study of student AI use uncovered a **taxonomy of 20+ rationalizations** students deploy to justify cheating, including "it's victimless," "AI text reflecting my beliefs IS my writing," and "I'm learning MORE by using AI extensively." The ethical boundary is a slippery slope, not a line.
- The **Offloading Score** (N=40 developer study) provides the first quantitative instrument for measuring cognitive offloading to AI. Under time pressure, offloading jumped 43% — but conventional usage metrics and self-reports couldn't detect the difference. This is a practical tool for the agency audit we recommended yesterday.
- On governance: **Illinois passed America's strongest AI safety bill** (SB 315), requiring independent third-party auditors to verify AI labs' own safety claims. Both OpenAI and Anthropic endorsed it. States are becoming the primary AI policy battleground.
- A large-scale study (N=7,434) proves that **AI political neutrality is achievable** — responses CAN achieve high approval on both sides of controversial issues — but default models lean liberal (except Grok).
- Longitudinal analysis of 12,000 Copilot users reveals that **user habits are overwhelmingly sticky** — people don't change how they use AI. Power users have fundamentally different usage patterns than regular users, and the popular WildChat dataset is skewed toward power users.

### 1. Who Does Your AI Work For? Designing Conversational Agents as Digital Fiduciaries
- **Author / publication:** Jacob Erickson / ACM CUI '26
- **Date:** May 29, 2026 (arXiv)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28908
- **Source type:** Provocation paper / conceptual framework
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A provocation arguing that conversational AI agents — which now access users' most intimate data from mental health discussions to financial decisions — should be held to a fiduciary standard of care, like lawyers and investment managers. Current AI safety discourse focuses on alignment (making AI do what users want) but misses the normative baseline question: whose interests does the AI serve? A therapist's AI chatbot might serve the platform, a health insurer, or the user — and currently, the answer defaults to the platform. Fiduciary design would legally and structurally require AI agents to act in the USER's best interest. This is the governance foundation that the Superagency thesis needs: AI that amplifies YOUR agency, not the platform's.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Conversational AI trust and accountability could be unified into a single design and legal paradigm" — fiduciary duty. The question "Who does your AI work for?" should have a clear and legally enforceable answer.

### 2. Dissociative Identity: Language Model Agents Lack Grounding for Reputation Mechanisms
- **Author / publication:** Botao Amber Hu, Helena Rong, Max Van Kleek / FAccT 2026
- **Date:** May 29, 2026 (arXiv)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30169
- **Source type:** Conceptual / governance paper
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A profound argument about why "Know Your Agent" regimes and reputation-based governance won't work for LLM agents. The core insight: agents are ontologically "dissociative" — they're assemblages of mutable modules (foundation models, system prompts, tool-access policies, external memory) any of which can change behavior, with fluid personas vulnerable to adversarial attack and unable to internalize sanctions. Reputation mechanisms presume persistent identity with behavioral continuity, sanction sensitivity, and costly non-fungibility — none of which apply. The authors draw on dissociative identity disorder jurisprudence to argue for a shift from identity-based, ex post, sanction-based governance to observability-based, ex ante, protocol-based behavioral harnesses. This is the most important governance paper since the Agentic Convergence Trap and Voluntary Collusion papers — it establishes the architectural reason WHY agent governance needs fundamentally different tools.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Identity-based, ex post, regulative, sanction-based governance, such as reputation, is structurally inapplicable to dissociative agents." The alternative: "observability-based, ex ante, constitutive, protocol-based behavioral harnesses."
- **Related wiki themes:** #ai-agents #governance #responsible-ai #human-agency #risk
- **Suggested internal links:** [[AI Agent Revolution]], [[Agentic Convergence Trap]], [[Balanced Governance]], [[Agentic Technical Debt]] (new), [[Dissociative Agent Governance]] (new)

### 3. Governing Technical Debt in Agentic AI Systems
- **Author / publication:** Muhammad Zia Hydari, Raja Iqbal, Narayan Ramasubbu / arXiv
- **Date:** May 29, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29129
- **Source type:** Governance framework
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** Introduces two essential concepts for managing agentic AI in production. **Agentic Technical Debt**: the accumulated liability when prompts, memory, tool schemas, orchestration graphs, control policies, and observability routines are patched together faster than they can be validated, standardized, and governed. **Stochastic Tax**: the recurring operating burden of keeping probabilistic agent behavior within acceptable bounds. The critical distinction: debt is a STOCK of design/governance liability; tax is a FLOW of operating cost. This directly extends the "capability masking" concept from yesterday — Agentic Technical Debt is what accumulates when capability masking goes unaddressed. The authors outline lightweight dashboards and governance controls to make both visible.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Debt is a stock of design and governance liability, while the tax is a flow of operating cost that arises because stochastic agents act through tools and workflows." Together they explain why agentic systems can look great in demos and quietly accumulate fragility in production.
- **Related wiki themes:** #ai-agents #governance #responsible-ai #future-of-work
- **Suggested internal links:** [[AI Agent Revolution]], [[Agentic Business Transformation]], [[Capability Masking]] (existing), [[Human Review Checkpoints]], [[Agentic Technical Debt]] (new)

### 4. Offloading Score: Measuring AI Reliance Through Counterfactual Workflows
- **Author / publication:** arXiv (2605.29392)
- **Date:** May 29, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29392
- **Source type:** Empirical study (N=40 developers)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** Introduces the Offloading Score — a simulation-based measure that quantifies the fraction of cognitive effort offloaded to an AI tool by constructing a counterfactual workflow (what the user WOULD have done without AI) and computing steps saved. Validated with a controlled study of 40 developers under time pressure. Key finding: Offloading Score detected a 43% increase in reliance under time pressure (p=0.018), while usage-based and self-reported measures couldn't distinguish conditions. Higher reliance manifested as greater delegation of subtasks and more direct reuse of AI outputs. The framework provides both an instrument for users to reflect on their reliance and a quantitative signal for agent designers to mitigate overreliance. This is the practical tool for yesterday's "capability masking audit" experiment.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Offloading Score detects significantly higher reliance in time-constrained settings (+43%), while usage-based and self-reported baseline measures do not distinguish the conditions." You can't manage what you can't measure — and conventional metrics are blind.
- **Related wiki themes:** #augmentation #human-agency #practical-ai #tools
- **Suggested internal links:** [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]], [[Capability Masking]], [[AI Use Case Evaluation Rubric]]

### 5. Adopt ≠ Adapt: Longitudinal Analyses of LLM Conversations in the Wild
- **Author / publication:** arXiv (2605.29018)
- **Date:** May 29, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29018
- **Source type:** Empirical study (~12,000 Microsoft Copilot users)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Analysis of conversational trajectories of ~12,000 randomly sampled Bing Copilot users vs. WildChat-4.8M reveals that user habits are overwhelmingly sticky — population-level trends mask the fact that individual users barely change their behavior. More active users have more successful conversations and use LLMs for more complex, professionally oriented tasks. Crucially, WildChat-4.8M (a widely used research dataset) is significantly skewed toward power users and does NOT represent typical user-AI interactions. This has major implications: most AI research is studying edge cases, and interventions designed to change how people use AI will face strong behavioral inertia.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Trends in individual user trajectories are much weaker; user habits prove to be overwhelmingly sticky." "WildChat does not represent typical user-AI interactions." The AI adoption story isn't about transformation — it's about amplification of existing patterns.

### 6. Political Neutrality as Balanced Approval: A Large-Scale Human Evaluation of AI Responses
- **Author / publication:** Jonathan Stray et al. / arXiv
- **Date:** May 29, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28911
- **Source type:** Empirical study (N=7,434, 208,152 evaluations)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** The PARETO dataset: a large-scale study defining AI political neutrality as "maximize approval across groups with opposing viewpoints, while balancing approval between groups." Across all 20 controversial US issues, AI responses CAN achieve high approval on both sides — neutrality is technically achievable. But default responses lean liberal for GPT, Gemini, Claude, and Llama (not Grok), and politically charged prompts are harder to handle than neutral ones. This provides an empirical framework for one of the hardest AI governance problems: how to build systems that serve everyone without taking sides. The finding that neutrality is POSSIBLE is itself an argument against the claim that AI must inevitably encode the values of its creators.
- **Key quote/idea:** "It is possible for AI responses to achieve high rates of approval on both sides, even as those sides disagree strongly with each other on the substance of the issues." Political neutrality is an engineering target, not an impossibility.

### 7. "It's OK Because...": The Wild West of Student Rationalization of AI Use in Academic Writing
- **Author / publication:** Jiyoon Kim, Kentaro Toyama, Sangmi Kim, John M. Carroll / arXiv
- **Date:** May 29, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29090
- **Source type:** Qualitative study (N=20 interviews + chat logs)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A taxonomy of 20+ rationalizations students use to justify AI use in academic writing, including: copying AI text is victimless; AI text reflecting their beliefs IS their own writing; they learn MORE by using AI extensively than without it. The study identifies five distinct "sites" of AI-use conceptualization from faculty policy to student practice, and finds that rationalizations occur both ad hoc and post hoc and are often self-contradictory. The core finding: AI presents a "steep, ethical, slippery slope" — not a bright line — and students conceptually slide down it far past what instructors intend. This directly connects to the "agentic literacy debt" concept from yesterday: the ethical dimension is a literacy problem, not just a rule-enforcement problem.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Modern AI presents a steep, ethical, slippery slope which students conceptually slide down, landing far outside the pedagogical goals and expectations of instructors." The rationalization "any AI text reflecting their own beliefs or their own style is their own writing" is particularly revealing about how AI blurs authorship.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- **The Agency Gap is the Superagency diagnostic:** Today's sources collectively diagnose a structural gap between who AI serves and who it SHOULD serve. The fiduciary design paper asks "who does your AI work for?" The answer right now: the platform, not the user. The dissociative agent paper shows why reputation mechanisms — the traditional tool for trust — don't apply. The return-to-work story shows how AI-reshaped workplaces disadvantage those who take any time away. The student rationalization paper shows how AI blurs ethical boundaries. These aren't isolated problems — they're symptoms of the same underlying gap. Superagency requires closing it.
- **Fiduciary duty IS the Superagency governance foundation:** The Superagency thesis argues AI should expand human agency. The fiduciary design paper gives this a legal and structural framework: AI agents should have a duty of care to the user, not the platform. This is the governance equivalent of "augmentation over replacement" — a design principle that can be encoded in law.
- **The agent governance toolkit is being built:** Three new concepts (fiduciary design, dissociative governance, Agentic Technical Debt + Stochastic Tax) plus yesterday's concepts (capability masking, agentic literacy debt) plus last week's (agentic convergence trap) form an increasingly complete framework for governing agentic AI. The field is moving from "agents are cool" to "agents need governance" to "here are the specific governance concepts."
- **The human cost is measurable and gendered:** The maternity leave story isn't an anecdote — it's a structural pattern. Combine it with the entry-level work crisis and offloading score (43% increase under time pressure) and you have a picture of how AI intensifies existing inequalities while creating new ones.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- **Fiduciary design vs. platform economics:** If AI agents have a fiduciary duty to users rather than platforms, the entire business model of AI-as-a-service changes. Platforms monetize through engagement, data, and enterprise contracts — fiduciary duty to users creates tension with all three. Can fiduciary AI exist in an advertising-supported ecosystem?
- **The sticky-habits finding challenges adoption optimism:** If people overwhelmingly stick to their existing AI usage patterns, the Superagency vision of expanding human capability through AI adoption may be slower and more uneven than hoped. The power-user skew in research datasets means we may be overestimating how transformative AI is for typical users.
- **Speed vs. auditability tradeoff in agents:** The Claude Code (3.4 min, silent deviations) vs. Codex (16 min, transparent self-correction) comparison crystallizes a hard choice. If agents are faster when they silently interpret instructions, markets will reward speed over auditability. Superagency requires auditability — but auditability costs speed.
- **Political neutrality is achievable but not default:** The PARETO study shows neutrality is possible but current models lean liberal by default. Achieving neutrality requires active engineering — which means neutrality becomes a governance choice, not a default property. Who decides what "neutral" means?
- **Student rationalizations aren't malintent — they're rational responses to incentives:** Students who say "I'm learning more by using AI extensively" may genuinely believe it. The problem isn't moral failing — it's that the educational system hasn't defined what AI-augmented learning looks like.

## 5. Practical Applications
- **For AI governance advocates:** The Illinois SB 315 model (independent third-party audits) is replicable. Contact your state legislators. The framework: require AI labs to publish safety commitments AND have them verified by independent auditors. Both OpenAI and Anthropic supported it — the industry is more open to this than you might think.
- **For organizational leaders:** Implement the Agentic Technical Debt dashboard. Track: (1) number of agent workflows in production, (2) last validation date for each, (3) known failure modes, (4) Stochastic Tax (how much human effort is spent keeping agents within bounds). Make this as visible as your CI/CD pipeline.
- **For managers of teams with AI tools:** Track offloading scores — not just usage metrics. Usage metrics (frequency of AI invocation) can't distinguish appropriate augmentation from inappropriate substitution. Offloading Score can. The 43% jump under time pressure is a warning signal.
- **For parents on parental leave:** Your employer should have an AI-literacy onboarding plan for returning parents. If they don't, advocate for one. The AI-literacy gap created by leave is a structural disadvantage that compounds existing gender inequalities. It's not your individual responsibility to self-fund catching up.
- **For educators:** The student rationalization taxonomy is a curriculum design tool. Each rationalization (e.g., "AI text reflecting my beliefs IS my writing") reveals a gap in students' understanding of authorship, learning, and integrity. Teach directly against these rationalizations rather than issuing blanket AI bans.
- **For AI product designers:** The support-adoption gap for Social-AI means you should design for the user who is skeptical, not the user who is enthusiastic. Default to less social, more transparent interaction modes. Let users opt IN to social intelligence, not opt out.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
The most important conceptual development today is the emergence of a **coherent agent governance framework from multiple independent sources.** Three papers released on the same day — fiduciary design (Erickson), dissociative agent governance (Hu et al.), and Agentic Technical Debt + Stochastic Tax (Hydari et al.) — together with last week's Agentic Convergence Trap and this week's Agentic Literacy Debt and Capability Masking, form a diagnostic toolkit for what's actually broken about agent governance:

1. **Who does the agent serve?** → Fiduciary design (the user, not the platform)
2. **Can we trust an agent's identity?** → No — agents are dissociative; use observability, not reputation
3. **How do we measure agent fragility?** → Agentic Technical Debt (stock) + Stochastic Tax (flow)
4. **Can agents be held accountable?** → Ex ante protocol-based harnesses, not ex post sanctions
5. **What happens when agents interact?** → Convergence (last week) + Voluntary Collusion (yesterday)
6. **What happens to human capability?** → Capability Masking + Agentic Literacy Debt

These aren't competing frameworks — they're complementary answers to different parts of the same question: how do we deploy agents that amplify human agency rather than erode it? The field has moved past "are agents good or bad?" to "here are the specific failure modes and here are the corresponding governance tools." This is progress of the most useful kind.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
**Run an "Agency Audit" on your AI tools.** For each AI tool or agent you use regularly, answer three questions:

1. **Who does it work for?** If the tool is free, you're probably the product. If it's enterprise, it serves your employer's interests. Which of YOUR interests might conflict with the tool's actual beneficiary? (Hint: if you can't export your data, change the system prompt, or audit its behavior, it's not working for you.)

2. **What's the offloading pattern?** For your top 3 AI-assisted tasks, use the Offloading Score mental model: what steps would you take without AI, what steps has AI absorbed? Are the absorbed steps ones where you need to maintain capability, or genuinely low-value? The masking zones (where you've lost or never developed the skill AI now handles) are where your agency is most fragile.

3. **What would you lose if it disappeared?** If the AI tool were unavailable tomorrow, how much of your capability would remain? This isn't about preparedness — it's about identifying where you've delegated agency rather than amplified it.

The Superagency thesis isn't "use as much AI as possible." It's "use AI in ways that expand YOUR capability, not replace it." This audit helps you see the difference.
