# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-06-02

## 1. Executive Summary

Today's sources converge on a single revealing pattern: **people don't actually penalize AI-authored creative work — but they think they do, and that belief shapes everything downstream.** Three studies from different angles — a preregistered experiment on short fiction, an ethnographic analysis of student writing patterns, and a high-profile creator-platform dispute — all point to the same finding: the question isn't whether AI CAN produce creative work. It can. The question is what humans choose to value, protect, and stay intentional about.

The headline paper ("Know Your Author") found that AI authorship labels produced **no reliable main effects** on creativity, enjoyment, recommendation, or originality ratings for short fiction. Effect sizes were uniformly small. But labels dramatically shaped **perceived effort**: participants estimated human authors took 148 minutes vs. 6 minutes for AI — a 25x gap — and higher inferred effort predicted greater enjoyment **even within the AI-labeled condition**. This is the "effort heuristic" at work: we don't actually judge AI writing as worse, but we believe we should, and that belief drives our downstream evaluations.

Ethan Mollick's latest essay ("Choosing to Stay Human") provides the practical corollary: AI-generated writing is everywhere — social media, academic papers, New York Times opinion pieces, award-winning short stories — and much of it is what he calls "meaning-shaped attention vampires": text that seems intellectual but delivers little real understanding. His argument is for **intentionality**: not reflexive avoidance of AI or reflexive dependence, but conscious choice about when to use it and when to do the hard work yourself. The default settings being set NOW — by AI companies (frictionless design), employers (what counts as "using AI well"), and educators (AI literacy curricula) — will be hard to reverse.

The WIRED piece on the Good Advice Cupcake dispute completes the picture: BuzzFeed licensed a creator's character for an AI-animated Amazon series without her consent or compensation, arguing that standard employment IP agreements give them the right to "bring a dormant library series off the shelf." The creator calls it "an assault on artists everywhere." This is the structural counterpart to the individual-choice question Mollick raises: even if individual creators choose to stay intentional, the platforms that own their IP can choose otherwise.

The through-line: **creative agency in the AI era is determined less by what AI can produce than by what structures and norms humans build around its use.** The absence of an AI penalty in fiction is, paradoxically, more threatening than its presence would be — if people can't tell the difference and don't care when they can, what incentive do platforms have to pay creators?

### 1. Know Your Author: Does the AI Penalty Hold in Short Fiction? (Morris, Brubaker, Garrett / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 2, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00006
- **Source type:** Preregistered experiment
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A preregistered experiment (N=254 per protocol) testing whether labeling short fiction as AI-written changes how readers evaluate it. Participants read 200-word vignettes randomly labeled as Human-written, AI-written, or with no author line. **The key finding: AI labels produced no reliable main effects on creativity, enjoyment, recommendation, or originality.** Effect sizes were uniformly small. However, labels powerfully shaped perceived effort: participants estimated human authors took ~148 minutes vs. ~6 minutes for AI (a 25x gap). Higher inferred effort predicted greater enjoyment even within the AI-labeled condition, and participants' prior attitudes toward AI moderated recommendation judgments — pro-AI participants rated AI-labeled stories higher, while AI-skeptical participants did the opposite. **Implication for the wiki: the "AI penalty" in creative evaluation may not exist in the way public discourse assumes.** But the effort heuristic suggests a deeper dynamic: readers judge creative work partly by how hard they imagine it was to make, and AI short-circuits that heuristic. This is consistent with the "meaning-shaped attention vampire" thesis — the work seems fine on the surface but carries no sense of human craft behind it.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Authorship labels did not produce reliable main effects on creativity, enjoyment, recommendation, or originality; observed effect sizes were uniformly small. However, labels strongly influenced inferred effort: participants estimated that Human-labeled stories took far longer to create than AI-labeled stories (148 vs. 6 minutes)."

### 2. Choosing to Stay Human (Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing)
- **Date:** May 26, 2026
- **URL:** https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/choosing-to-stay-human
- **Source type:** Essay / newsletter
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** Comprehensive essay on intentional AI use in creative and cognitive work. Three core arguments: (1) AI-generated content is now pervasive — social media, academic papers, NYT opinion articles, award-winning short stories — and much of it is "meaning-shaped attention vampires": text that looks intellectual but delivers nothing. (2) Default AI use for writing risks losing the craft of personal style, which takes years to develop and involves self-discovery as much as technique. AI can be a fantastic aid (checking writing, simulating reader perspectives, helping those who struggle to communicate), but mindless over-reliance is the problem. (3) The concept of "cognitive surrender" — stopping thinking and letting AI do the work even when it's wrong — is the core risk. Two education studies make this concrete: a Turkish study found AI-as-answer-giver hurt learning (homework improved, exams worsened), while a Taipei study found AI-as-personalized-tutor helped (5-month Python course, +0.15 SD on final exam, equivalent to 6-9 months of additional schooling). The essay ends with a call for **intentionality**: "The most important thing we can do is keep asking what to hand over and what to keep for ourselves… and not expect anyone, including the AI, to answer that for us."
- **Key quote/idea:** "There is often no human meaning there, these posts are just meaning-shaped attention vampires that take mental effort to decode and give you no equivalent understanding in return." And: "The point isn't to avoid AI but to be intentional about it by making a conscious choice about AI use, rather than reflexive dependence or reflexive avoidance."
- **Related wiki themes:** #creativity #human-agency #augmentation #practical-ai #ai-education
- **Suggested internal links:** [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Human Agency]], [[AI Writing Partners]], [[Creativity]], [[Education]]

### 3. Amazon Is Making an AI-Animated 'Good Advice Cupcake' TV Show. Its Original Creator Is Furious (Miles Klee / WIRED)

- **Source type:** Investigative reporting
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** Detailed reporting on the dispute between Loryn Brantz, creator of the Good Advice Cupcake (Cuppy) character, and BuzzFeed/Amazon. Brantz created Cuppy while employed at BuzzFeed; the character became a viral sensation (2M+ Instagram followers). After she left in 2023, BuzzFeed — which retained IP ownership through her employment agreement — licensed Cuppy to Amazon for an AI-animated series called "Cupcake & Friends," funded through Amazon's GenAI Creators' Fund. Brantz was not consulted, did not consent, and receives no revenue share. She calls it "an assault on artists everywhere" and a "soulless AI puppet." The case illustrates a pattern likely to become common: creators sign standard work-for-hire agreements early in their careers, platforms accumulate character libraries, and AI enables those libraries to be scaled into content without ongoing creator involvement.
- **Key quote/idea:** Brantz: "Nothing said in good faith by managers and executives was followed through with." Peretti: "Her personal opposition to AI cannot determine how BuzzFeed develops IP that it owns, or deny the many other talented creators involved in this project the opportunity to do their work."

### 4. Tracing GenAI Literacy: Uncovering Student-AI Interaction Patterns in Academic Writing through Epistemic Network Analysis (Bae et al. / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 2, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00040
- **Source type:** Empirical study (learning analytics)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Collected interaction logs from 162 university students engaged in a GenAI-assisted abstract writing task. Using Epistemic Network Analysis, the study modeled questioning strategies by GenAI literacy level. The key distinction: **high-literacy students engage in iterative refinement and strategic questioning** (using AI to test ideas, request alternatives, probe weaknesses), while **low-literacy students rely on direct generation commands** (asking AI to produce output with minimal guidance and accepting it as-is). This provides process-level evidence for what Mollick describes at the outcome level: mindless AI use produces fluent-but-shallow results, while intentional engagement preserves learning and craft. The study contributes a methodology for data-driven GenAI literacy assessment that moves beyond self-report scales to actual interaction patterns. **Directly relevant to the wiki's focus on augmentation over replacement: the difference between AI-as-co-pilot and AI-as-replacement shows up in keystroke-level interaction patterns.**
- **Key quote/idea:** "High-literacy students engage in iterative refinement and strategic questioning, while low-literacy students rely on direct generation commands."

### 5. AI-conformity: How Algorithms Change Human Moral Decision-Making (Kowalczuk, Nowak, Biecek / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 2, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00013
- **Source type:** Experiment (Asch paradigm adaptation)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Adapted the classic Asch conformity paradigm for AI influence (n=165). Participants completed moral dilemmas under three conditions: social majority pressure, AI providing brief answers, and AI providing answers with explanations. **Finding: an AI model with a reasoning component influenced participants' moral judgments to a degree comparable to a human majority.** This challenges the assumption that moral decision-making lies in an "AI inadmissibility zone" — a sphere where only human-made decisions are acceptable. The mechanism of algorithmic conformity appears to differ from social conformity: social conformity was driven by group pressure, while algorithmic conformity was driven by the perceived competence of the AI's reasoning. **This has direct implications for creative work: if AI can shift moral judgments, it can certainly shift aesthetic and creative judgments through similar mechanisms of perceived competence.**
- **Key quote/idea:** "An AI model with a reasoning component affected the opinion of participants to a degree comparable to that of a human majority." The mechanism appears to be perceived competence, not social pressure.
- **Related wiki themes:** #human-agency #counterarguments #responsible-ai #augmentation

### 6. The New Social Image: How AI Competency and AI Proactivity Influence Self- and Peer-Perceptions in the Workplace (Gulati et al. / Interacting with Computers)
- **Date:** June 2, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00182
- **Source type:** Vignette experiment (n=50, 2×2×2 design)
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** A 2×2×2 vignette study examining how AI competency (low/high) and proactivity (low/high) shape workplace perceptions. The counterintuitive finding: **highly competent and proactive AI systems undermine perceptions of ownership, job meaningfulness, satisfaction, and positive team dynamics.** Low-competency or low-proactivity AI generally improved these perceptions. Crucially, effects were often moderated by point-of-view: low AI proactivity improved job satisfaction from self-perception more than from peer perception. The authors argue that designing AI for the future of work solely around performance metrics is inadequate — "highly competent and proactive AI-driven systems can have undesirable impacts on perceptions of ownership, job identity, social image and team dynamics, and consequently, job meaningfulness." **This is directly parallel to the creativity findings: what makes AI "good" from a performance standpoint can make it "bad" from a human experience standpoint.**
- **Key quote/idea:** "Designing AI for the future of work solely around performance metrics may not be adequate. Highly competent and proactive AI-driven systems can have undesirable impacts on perceptions of ownership, job identity, social image and team dynamics."

### 7. Beyond Tool Adoption: A Practical Five-Stage Developmental Continuum for AI Literacy in Higher Education (Anagnostopoulos et al. / arXiv)
- **Date:** June 2, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00038
- **Source type:** Framework paper + implementation case study
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Proposes a five-stage AI Literacy Continuum: (1) Not Yet Engaged, (2) Uncritical Use, (3) Informed Use, (4) Critical Evaluation, and (5) Improvement. Based on implementation at NC State University engaging 330+ participants (Fall 2024–Spring 2026). The continuum addresses the two problematic extremes in student AI use: avoidance driven by fear or mistrust, and uncritical reliance that produces fluent output while masking misunderstanding. Key insight: AI literacy is a **developmental capacity**, not a binary threshold — students move through stages, and discipline-embedded experiences produce stronger movement toward critical evaluation and improvement-oriented practice. The framework complements dimensional AI literacy models (UNESCO, OECD) with a practical diagnostic pathway. **Directly relevant to the wiki: fills the gap between "AI literacy is important" (everyone agrees) and "here's how to actually assess and develop it" (few models exist).**
- **Key quote/idea:** "AI literacy should be understood not as tool adoption alone but as a developmental capacity to understand, evaluate, and responsibly apply AI systems in disciplinary and societal contexts."

### 8. The Deterministic Horizon: When Extended Reasoning Fails and Tool Delegation Becomes Necessary (Stolfo, Piantadosi, McCoy, et al. / ICML 2026)
- **Date:** June 2, 2026
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00376
- **Source type:** Research paper (ICML 2026)
- **Reliability score:** 5/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** Establishes a formal Attention Bottleneck Theorem showing that decoder-only attention has a fundamental information-theoretic limit on state-tracking capacity: O(H · log(L/H) · √d_h). This creates a "Deterministic Horizon" d* ∈ [19, 31] beyond which neural chain-of-thought reasoning fails and tool delegation becomes necessary. Across 12 models and 8 task domains (SWE-Bench, WebArena, SQL-Multi), tool-integrated reasoning achieved 86-94% accuracy vs. 24-42% for neural chain-of-thought. Fine-tuning on optimal-length traces yielded <5% improvement, confirming an **architectural ceiling** rather than a training deficiency. High cross-model correlation (r = 0.81-0.91) indicates these failures are fundamental to the architecture, not specific to any model. **Implication for creative work with AI: there are structural limits to what pure reasoning can do. Agentic systems that combine neural reasoning with external tools inherit the benefits of both. This is the technical counterpart to the human-agency argument that AI should augment, not replace — the architecture itself says tool use beats pure reasoning beyond a defined horizon.**
- **Key quote/idea:** "Tool-integrated reasoning consistently outperforms neural chain-of-thought; on the primary model suite it reaches 86-94% accuracy versus 24-42% for neural chain-of-thought." The ceiling is architectural, not training-specific.
- **Related wiki themes:** #ai-agents #research #augmentation #tools

## 3. Superagency Connections

- **The "no AI penalty" finding is a Superagency inflection point.** If readers genuinely don't penalize AI-authored fiction, then the primary constraint on AI-produced creative content isn't audience acceptance — it's the choices of platforms and creators about what to produce and how to credit it. This shifts the Superagency question from "can AI produce good creative work?" (yes) to "who gets to decide what gets produced and who benefits?" (unresolved, and deeply contested in the Good Advice Cupcake case).

- **Mollick's intentionality framework is the practical Superagency posture.** His argument that we should consciously choose what to hand over and what to keep — and not expect anyone, including the AI, to answer that for us — is exactly the human-agency-through-design position this wiki advocates. The Superagency thesis isn't "use AI for everything." It's "design AI systems and norms so that humans remain the ones making the big choices about what matters."

- **The AI-conformity study extends the Superagency risk map into moral and aesthetic domains.** If AI can shift moral judgments as effectively as peer pressure, then the design choices embedded in AI systems — which models to deploy, what reasoning they show, how they frame options — carry agency-shaping power that extends beyond simple task assistance. The "Digital Fiduciary Duty" concept (already in this wiki) becomes more urgent: an AI system that shapes what people believe is right carries fiduciary-level responsibility.

- **The Deterministic Horizon paper is a Superagency design principle from the architecture level up.** If pure neural reasoning has a hard ceiling around 19-31 steps, then the future of capable AI systems is inherently hybrid — tool-using, delegation-capable, architecturally plural. This is good news for the augmentation thesis: the architecture itself pushes toward systems that combine reasoning with external action, which is the pattern of human-AI collaboration, not AI autonomy.

- **The five-stage AI literacy continuum operationalizes the Superagency promise.** The difference between "uncritical use" (Stage 2) and "critical evaluation" (Stage 4) is the difference between being shaped by AI and shaping your relationship with AI. This continuum provides a measurable pathway from passive consumption to active agency — exactly what the Superagency thesis claims is possible.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments

- **The "no AI penalty" finding could accelerate creator displacement.** If readers genuinely don't care whether fiction is AI-written, the economic case for paying human writers collapses. Why hire a writer when an AI produces equally well-received work at near-zero marginal cost? The Good Advice Cupcake case shows this logic already operating: BuzzFeed explicitly frames AI as a way to "bring dormant IP off the shelf" without the original creator. The absence of an audience penalty for AI content is, perversely, worse for creators than the presence of one would be.

- **Mollick's intentionality framing may be too individualistic.** His call for "conscious choice" puts the burden on individuals to resist systems designed to encourage dependence. AI interfaces are engineered for seamlessness (frictionless design); employers measure "using AI well" by output, not by learning; educators' AI literacy curricula often teach tool use without teaching resistance. Individual intentionality in the face of systemic incentives toward surrender is a thin reed. The wiki's governance pages ([[Balanced Governance]], [[Digital Fiduciary Duty]]) address the structural side, but the tension between individual agency and systemic design remains unresolved.

- **The AI-conformity study has a critical contextual limitation.** The Asch paradigm tests conformity to a single presented judgment, not ongoing interaction with an AI over time. Real-world AI influence operates through repeated, varied interactions that build trust and dependence gradually. The study shows vulnerability in the moment; the larger question is whether repeated exposure deepens or reduces this effect.

- **The Deterministic Horizon's 19-31 step ceiling is about state-tracking tasks, not creative reasoning.** Creative work (writing fiction, generating ideas, evaluating aesthetic quality) may not map cleanly onto the state-tracking problems the paper studies. The architectural limits it identifies are real, but their relevance to creative domains specifically needs to be established through further research.

## 5. Practical Applications

- **For writers and creators:** The "Know Your Author" findings suggest a practical strategy — **lead with your process, not just your output.** If readers enjoy work more when they perceive higher effort behind it, then sharing your creative process (drafts, revisions, decision points) may increase audience engagement even if AI was involved in the workflow. The value is in the human craft story, not just the final text.

- **For educators teaching writing:** The GenAI literacy interaction study provides a concrete diagnostic: **watch the interaction pattern, not the output.** Students who ask AI strategic questions, request alternatives, and probe weaknesses are developing literacy. Students who paste prompts and accept output are not, regardless of how good that output looks. Use this distinction to design writing assignments that require visible process, not just polished product.

- **For AI product designers building creative tools:** The New Social Image study suggests that **competent, proactive AI in creative contexts should be deliberately designed to be less seamless.** Add friction that signals "you're in control." Let the human make choices the AI could make automatically. Show the AI's reasoning but don't act on it without human confirmation. The performance cost is real but may be worth the ownership and meaning gain — especially in creative domains where ownership IS the value proposition.

- **For platform governance and creator contracts:** The Good Advice Cupcake case is a preview of coming conflicts. Creators negotiating employment or licensing agreements should consider AI-specific clauses: does the agreement permit AI-driven derivative works without the creator's involvement? Does it require consent, compensation, or credit? Standard work-for-hire language that predates AI capabilities may not reflect what either party understood they were agreeing to.

- **For the five-stage AI literacy framework:** Educators and trainers can use this as a self-assessment tool. Ask: "At what stage am I using AI in my domain?" Stage 2 (uncritical use) is the default — moving to Stage 4 (critical evaluation) requires deliberate practice and institutional support. The continuum also provides a rubric for evaluating AI literacy programs: are they moving students toward Stage 4-5, or just teaching Stage 2 more efficiently?

## 8. Top Insight of the Day

**The absence of an AI penalty in creative evaluation is more dangerous for human creative agency than its presence would be.** If readers genuinely don't distinguish between human and AI-authored fiction — and the preregistered experiment shows they don't — then the only remaining constraints on AI-produced creative content are the choices of platforms and creators about what to produce, how to credit it, and who benefits. The Good Advice Cupcake case shows platforms are already choosing "produce without the creator, credit nobody, keep the revenue." The counterforce isn't audience preference — it's governance, contracts, norms, and individual intentionality. Creative agency in the AI era will be won or lost not in the hearts of readers but in the fine print of IP agreements and the design defaults of creative AI tools.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try

**Do your own "Know Your Author" test with your own writing.** Take a paragraph or short piece you've written. Create an AI-generated version of similar length on the same topic (use a good prompt — don't just ask for generic output). Now share both with 3-5 people you trust, without labels, and ask them: (a) which do they prefer, (b) which do they think was written by a human, and (c) how long do they think each took to create?

Then share the results. The point isn't to "prove" anything — it's to experience firsthand the gap between what we think we can detect about AI writing and what we actually can. Mollick's "meaning-shaped attention vampires" thrive in that gap. Closing it — becoming able to distinguish substance from shape — is a learnable skill, but only if you practice it.
