# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-11-19

## Executive summary
The same question ties today's research together: **does the person held responsible actually control the decision and have evidence that the system is doing the right task?** Four newly reviewed preprints offer concrete checks for assessment writing, human calibration, predictive modeling, and high-risk governance. A WIRED report puts the distributional cost of frontier infrastructure in public view. None of the preprints is proof of a successful Texas school or institutional deployment.

## Top 5 findings

1. **Curriculum-checked drafts still need teacher acceptance.** [VeriMath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23456) (submitted Nov 17) generated Vietnamese Grade 12 mathematics assessment drafts from 4,555 reference items and 50 generation requirements. Its abstract reports verifier-accepted drafts on 47/50 requirements per run across two model backbones, but strict teacher-readiness acceptance reached *up to* 56.8%. The gap between machine verification and teacher acceptance is the point: a locally aligned item must also be mathematically sound, readable, accessible, and instructionally appropriate. Neither Vietnamese curriculum alignment nor automated acceptance establishes Texas TEKS alignment. Connects to [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]] and [[Education]].

2. **A display that helps a learner need not best describe the model.** [Miscalibrated AI improves human calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23618) (Nov 17) reports a preregistered study of 300 novices interpreting white-blood-cell images. One model's accuracy was held at 94% while its uncertainty display varied. In later unaided judgments, the conservative, poorly model-calibrated probabilities improved human calibration; the model-calibrated probabilities and categorical predictions did not show the same condition-specific improvement. This is an image-judgment experiment, not a clinical or K–12 intervention; do **not** infer that misleading users is good practice. It asks teams to test both *model calibration* and *what users learn* from a display. Connects to [[Risk-Benefit Matrix]] and [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]].

3. **A valid-looking model can predict the wrong outcome.** [When Do LLMs Model the Right Target?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23638) (Nov 18) tested 11 OpenAI and Anthropic models across seven prompt conditions in child-welfare, healthcare, and employment problem formulations. The authors found that without guidance models did not reliably account for historical decisions that shape observed outcomes; explicitly specifying target, assumptions, and estimation procedure produced 99–100% target-aligned implementations **in the tested conditions**. Some explanations named the problem while their code still failed to solve it. High implementation scores under prescribed prompts are not deployment validation or evidence that the target itself is fair. Connects to [[Risk-Benefit Matrix]] and [[Case Against AI Doomism]].

4. **Accountability without control is a governance failure.** [ALIGN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24609) (submitted Nov 18) describes a scenario-based method developed with expert interviews and observation in a high-risk railway use case. It asks which stakeholder can see, change, halt, or remedy an AI-mediated decision versus who is blamed if it causes harm. This is a method-development and evaluation paper, not evidence of broad safety outcomes or a railway deployment certification. Connects to [[Risk-Benefit Matrix]], [[Human Review Checkpoints]], and [[Balanced Governance]].

5. **Infrastructure benefits and burdens land on different people.** [WIRED's report on Anthropic's IPO](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropics-record-breaking-ipo-highlights-the-ai-giants-worst-enemy-itself/) (Nov 19) pairs the firm's financing and safety commitments with opposition to data-center development in West Virginia. Local residents' land-use and electricity concerns are governance inputs, not proof that every data center imposes the same costs. Financial figures and corporate claims are reported by WIRED and have not been independently audited here. The agency question is who can consent, influence siting, receive benefits, and challenge costs. Connects to [[AI Enclosure]] and [[Compute and Agency]].

## Top insight of the day
**A human in the loop is only meaningful if the human has a valid target, inspectable evidence, and a usable control lever.** Teacher review, model cards, an accountability chart, and local hearings are not interchangeable; each has to change a decision before a costly action is locked in.

## Practical experiment to try
Use a **fictional, non-student assessment item** generated for one explicitly identified TEKS expectation (verify the exact code in the current TEKS text first). Before showing the draft to another reviewer, record the expected reasoning, answer, likely misconception, and acceptance criteria. Then have a teacher independently check mathematical correctness, TEKS fit, language/accessibility, and whether the proposed solution actually tests the intended skill. Log machine-verifier pass separately from teacher acceptance, revise once, and stop short of classroom use. No student work or identifiable records in an unapproved AI service. This is a workflow test, not a claim about item quality or student learning.

## Tensions / counterarguments
Automated checks can speed drafting but do not certify teaching quality; the uncertainty-display result may not generalize beyond its image task; expert-informed governance methods may not change field outcomes; local infrastructure burdens cannot be inferred from one site.

## Practical applications
Run the non-student assessment dry run below before considering any approved pilot. For predictive tools, record the target and historical decisions that affect labels; for deployment, name the person who can stop the action and what evidence they can inspect.
