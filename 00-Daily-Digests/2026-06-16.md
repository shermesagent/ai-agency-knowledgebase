## Executive Summary

Today's research converges on three interlocking ideas about invisible accumulation: (1) **Cognitive Debt** is formalized as the stock of unverified reasoning obligations that accumulates when AI is used as a substitute rather than a complement for first-principles cognition — complete with a cognitive Minsky moment where subjective risk falls while true systemic fragility rises. (2) **Developer risk perception** reveals exactly where agency-preserving design falls in the priority stack: developers prioritize product and business risks before considering societal risks like job displacement and end-user privacy, and lack mature controls for containing agentic risks without constraining the very capabilities that make agents useful. (3) **Governance infrastructure** is cracking — Zvi's definitive analysis of the Fable takedown shows ad-hoc export controls being used as a vibes-based enforcement mechanism, while a new Ostrom-based taxonomy maps the institutional alternatives we're not building.

The Mollick counterpoint: "Using AI Right Now: A Quick Guide" (published today) provides practical scaffolding for AI adoption — a reminder that the infrastructure for healthy human-AI relationships exists and can be taught. Cukurova et al. provide the diagnostic: most current human-AI "collaboration" is better described as consultation, governance, delegation, or instruction — not collaboration at all.

The synthesis: We are accumulating three kinds of debt simultaneously — cognitive debt (individual), agency debt (organizational), and governance debt (institutional) — and the settlement mechanisms for the first two don't yet exist while the third is being dismantled in real time.

## Best Sources Found

### 1. Cognitive Debt: AI as Intellectual Leverage and the Dynamics of Systemic Fragility (Meng, 2606.15078)
**Type:** arXiv paper | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 4/5

A formal theory that will define the vocabulary for the next phase of agency research — and provides the language for what the Surrender Threshold model (yesterday's anchor paper) was reaching toward. Six propositions:

- **Rational agents incur positive cognitive debt** because costs are deferred, partially external, and masked by short-run productivity gains
- **The cognitive Minsky moment**: Tranquil periods lower subjective risk assessments, raise AI substitution intensity, and compound leverage — subjective risk falls while true systemic fragility rises
- **Expected crisis losses are convex** in aggregate leverage
- **The false-correction loop**: Post-crisis, output-target pressure can produce a trap where agents patch AI failures with more AI rather than rebuilding cognitive capital
- **The decentralized equilibrium over-adopts** substitutive AI relative to the social optimum due to systemic risk externalities, cognitive public goods, and arms-race dynamics
- **The high-capital paradox**: High-cognitive-capital agents adopt AI more intensively and may eventually erode their unaided cognitive capital below that of initially lower-skilled agents

**Superagency connection:** Cognitive Debt is to the individual what Agentic Technical Debt is to systems and what the Surrender Threshold is to autonomy — the formal model of invisible accumulation that the Superagency thesis must prevent. When cognitive debt compounds through RSI, the recovery mechanism becomes not just a design feature but a survival requirement.

### 2. Using AI Right Now: A Quick Guide (Ethan Mollick, June 16, 2026)
**Type:** Newsletter / Practitioner guide | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 5/5

Mollick's latest — published today — is a practical entry point for AI adoption. Key structure:
- **Choose a model**: GPT-5.5 for broad capability, Claude Fable/Opus 4.8 for nuanced reasoning, Gemini for Deep Research and video generation
- **Try three things immediately**: Switch to the powerful model for a complex task, use Deep Research, experiment with voice mode
- **Voice mode's killer feature** isn't natural conversation — it's screen sharing and real-time camera access for cooking, repairs, navigation
- **Deep Research** (all three platforms) produces cited, structured reports that are far more accurate than raw model outputs
- **Canvas mode** (ChatGPT, Gemini) or Claude's native output generation can create interactive tools, simulations, and games

The difference between casual users and power users isn't prompting skill — it's knowing these features exist and using them on real work.

**Superagency connection:** This guide operationalizes the Co-Intelligence framework. Every feature Mollick recommends — context provision, branching, Deep Research, voice mode with screen sharing — is an agency-expanding capability. The guide is a reminder that the infrastructure for healthy human-AI relationships exists and can be taught.

### 3. The Perils of Agency: How Developers Perceive, Prioritize, and Address Risks in Agentic AI Products (Lee et al., 2606.15485)
**Type:** arXiv paper | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 5/5

N=35 industry developer study. Three key findings with direct Superagency implications:

- **Risk perception is tied to agentic qualities**: Developers identified risks specifically from autonomy, tool use, and real-world deployment — the same characteristics that make agents useful
- **Priority stacking works against agency preservation**: Product and business risks were prioritized before downstream societal risks (job displacement, end-user privacy). This means agency-preservation features are systematically deprioritized unless they also serve product metrics
- **No mature controls for agentic risk containment**: Developers lack tools for containing agentic risks without constraining agentic capabilities — the capability vs. risk control tension persists at the engineering level

**Superagency connection:** This is the organizational mechanism behind cognitive debt accumulation. When developers prioritize product risk over societal risk, and lack mature controls for agency preservation, the system accumulates agency debt at the same rate it accumulates technical debt — silently, invisibly, and with a Minsky moment waiting.

### 4. Commons-Governed Artificial Intelligence: A Taxonomy of Collective Governance (Garrido-Merchán, 2606.15466)
**Type:** arXiv paper | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 4/5

An Ostrom-based taxonomy of AI governance that maps the institutional alternatives to both market and state frames. Key contributions:

- **Two-dimensional taxonomy**: Resource layer (data, compute, models, knowledge/evaluation, energy) × governance function (Ostrom design principles)
- **Ten institutional archetypes** identified across published evidence: data trusts, federated learning consortia, public compute initiatives, open-weight collaborations, community data sovereignty regimes, and more
- **Energy and sustainability as first-class governance problem**, not an externality
- **Maturity matrix** and comparative reading against Ostrom's eight design principles
- **Tensions identified**: openwashing, the compute bottleneck, free-riding, scale vs. sustainability

**Superagency connection:** This fills a gap in the knowledgebase's governance section. The Balanced Governance page currently focuses on state/market/hybrid frames — the commons frame is the missing third institutional approach, and it's the one most aligned with the Superagency thesis of distributed capability.

### 5. What Do You Mean by Human-AI Collaboration? Prerequisite Functions and Affordances (Cukurova, 2606.15509)
**Type:** arXiv paper | **Relevance:** 5/5 | **Reliability:** 5/5

A systematic reconstruction of what "collaboration" actually requires versus what current human-AI interactions deliver. Key contributions:

- **Five-level diagnostic taxonomy of human-AI teaming**: Transactional → Situational → Operational → Praxical → Synergistic — only the highest level satisfies traditional collaboration conditions
- **Most current human-AI interaction is not collaboration** but consultation, governance, delegation, or instruction
- **Collaboration requires**: partly symmetric and negotiated relationship, shared and negotiable goals, low and shifting division of labor, interactive and synchronous exchange, mutual modeling, grounding, and socially shared regulation
- **These are mostly engineering choices, not capabilities to be awaited**

**Superagency connection:** This directly refines the Co-Intelligence concept. Co-Intelligence currently covers the broad spectrum of human-AI cooperation — Cukurova provides the diagnostic precision to distinguish genuine collaboration (which preserves and expands agency) from delegation masquerading as collaboration (which transfers agency).

## Superagency Connections

Today's sources validate and extend the Superagency thesis along three axes:

1. **Cognitive Debt formalizes the Surrender Threshold model from yesterday.** The Minsky moment dynamic — where subjective risk falls while true fragility rises — is the mechanism by which cognitive surrender becomes preference inversion. The false-correction loop (patching AI failures with more AI) is the behavioral pattern that makes recovery mechanisms individually irrational even when collectively necessary.

2. **Developer priorities are a structural barrier to agency-preserving AI.** The Perils of Agency study shows that even when developers recognize agentic risks, they systematically deprioritize them below product and business concerns. This means agency preservation cannot be left to developer goodwill — it must be designed into the incentive structure, the evaluation frameworks, and the governance requirements.

3. **The governance infrastructure is simultaneously being built (Commons-Governed AI) and dismantled (Fable takedown).** The Ostrom-based taxonomy maps the institutional alternatives we're not building; the Fable takedown demonstrates the costs of not building them. The convergence point: ad-hoc governance is the governance equivalent of cognitive debt — invisible accumulation that looks stable until the Minsky moment arrives.

## Tensions / Counterarguments

1. **Mollick's practical optimism vs. Meng's formal pessimism.** Mollick's guide says "just try three things" — the path to agency expansion is practical, accessible, and immediate. Meng's model says the same path accumulates cognitive debt with every delegation. Both can be true: the practical guide can work AND accumulate debt. The question is whether the guide's practitioners are building recovery mechanisms or just delegating faster.

2. **Commons governance vs. the Fable precedent.** Garrido-Merchán's taxonomy shows that commons-governed AI is proliferating in practice — data trusts, federated learning consortia, open-weight collaborations. But the Fable takedown shows that the state frame can override all of these with a single export control. The tension: commons governance grows from below; state power strikes from above. Which one determines the trajectory?

3. **Collaboration taxonomy vs. the Mollick guide.** Cukurova argues that genuine collaboration requires symmetric, negotiated relationships that current AI can't deliver. Mollick's guide recommends practical use that looks more like consultation/delegation. Is Mollick teaching people to delegate well (good), or teaching them to call delegation "collaboration" (the accountability gap again)?

## Practical Applications

1. **The Cognitive Debt Diagnostic:** Before adopting AI for a recurring task, ask: (a) What unverified reasoning obligations am I accumulating? (b) Is there a false-correction loop risk — where AI failures get patched with more AI? (c) What is my recovery mechanism when the debt comes due?

2. **The Collaboration Level Audit:** Use Cukurova's five-level taxonomy to audit any human-AI workflow. If you're at Transactional or Situational, that's fine — but don't call it collaboration. If you want Synergistic, you need symmetric negotiation, shared goals, low division of labor, and mutual modeling.

3. **The Governance Debt Inventory:** For your organization: (a) What governance mechanisms exist for AI deployment decisions? (b) Are they institutional (rules, review processes) or ad-hoc (executive judgment, vibes)? (c) What's the Minsky risk — where does subjective confidence exceed true institutional capacity?

## Practical Experiment to Try

**The Debt Audit:** Pick one recurring AI-assisted task. For one session, do it entirely without AI. Note: (a) How much longer does it take? (b) What reasoning steps have you been skipping? (c) Would you have caught the last AI error without the recovery check? This is your personal cognitive debt measurement. If the gap between AI-assisted and unaided performance is wider than expected, you're accumulating debt faster than you're building recovery mechanisms.
