## The Evidence-and-Credit Test

## 1. Executive Summary
Four directly inspected sources (two October 16 arXiv abstracts, one October 16 WIRED report, one October 15 Zvi newsletter essay) point to two separate questions: **can a user challenge what the AI says, and can the human who supplied its apparent creativity claim credit?** Five findings below are drawn from four sources; #5 is a second implication from WIRED, not an additional study. `web_search` returned empty on two distinct queries, so primary RSS, accessible publisher text and arXiv feed abstracts supplied the evidence. The papers are preliminary/preprint evidence, not Texas K–12 effectiveness trials.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- [[AI Tutor Evaluation Checklist]]: agency grows when a student can request evidence, challenge a hint and explain the answer *without* the system, not merely when a conversation feels helpful.
- [[AI Field Experiment Evidence]]: an inspectable benchmark is useful screening infrastructure; observed human benefit still needs a separate test.
- [[The AI Slop Backlash]]: synthetic-performer disclosure and underlying creator attribution are distinct obligations. An audience can know the face is AI while still not knowing who wrote the joke.
- [[Human Review Checkpoints]]: before an agent spends money or transmits payment credentials, the owner must be able to inspect the item, price, merchant, data destination and final authorization.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- Asking children to verify every hint can add cognitive load; sometimes a tool-like interface may support checking better than a social persona, but this small preliminary study does not establish the best classroom design.
- ImpactBench's automated-judge agreement with majority expert ratings was 83.3% in reviewed cases (Cohen's kappa 0.667); that leaves nontrivial disagreement and does not validate a product for real teens.
- Copyright law does not automatically protect every comic premise or delivery style. A fair review process must distinguish wholesale copying from common material, legitimate quotation and remix.
- Automated purchasing can save time, but convenience does not justify casually sharing card images in chat or bypassing authorization and purchase review.

## 5. Practical Applications
1. In a teacher-controlled demonstration on **public, non-identifiable material**, ask the learner to challenge one plausible but flawed AI hint; score evidence-seeking, correction and a later unaided explanation separately. Do not upload identifiable student work to an unapproved service.
2. Before approving a teen-facing assistant, inspect metric definitions and a sample of underlying conversations, then specify the real outcome and human-review plan. A simulated safety score is not a deployment pass.
3. For creator-facing publishing, capture a source/permission record and a human-reviewed complaint/appeal route. For transaction agents, require an explicit pre-purchase review screen and a safe payment mechanism rather than a card photo in chat.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**A fluent output needs two receipts:** evidence the user can challenge and credit the underlying human contributor can reclaim. Neither a pleasant interface nor a synthetic-content label supplies both.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
Run a 20-minute tabletop review with three adults using a public AI-generated example, not student or employee data. One person plays the user and asks for evidence; one traces the origin/permission of any borrowed material; one identifies the exact action that would require explicit approval before publication or purchase. Record whether each person can reach the relevant evidence and reverse a mistaken action. Treat the result as a workflow test, not proof of learning or legal compliance.
