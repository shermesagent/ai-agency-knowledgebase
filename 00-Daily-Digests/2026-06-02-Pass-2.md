# Daily AI Agency Digest — 2026-06-02 (Curator Pass 2: Work & Labor)

> Complementary to the morning digest (Creativity & Writing rotation). This pass covers the Work/Labor rotation with new data-driven sources on AI's actual labor market impact, the invisible AI economy, automated AI R&D risks, and a sharp counterargument from Cory Doctorow.

## 1. Executive Summary

Today's second-pass sources converge on a single tension: **the hard data says AI's labor impact is still small and manageable — yet every intuition from inside the AI industry screams that something immense is coming, and society is barely measuring it.** David Rotman's MIT Technology Review piece is the most thorough data-driven reality check on AI jobs hysteria we've seen: only 1 in 5 companies use AI in any business function, unemployment in AI-exposed occupations is actually lower than in less-exposed ones, wages are rising in AI-exposed sectors, and the 16% decline in entry-level jobs for young workers is concentrated in automation-use cases while augmentation-use cases show growth. Former BLS head Erika McEntarfer's verdict: "It could be disruptive, but the data is telling us right now that disruption is not yet here, and we have time to plan."

Jack Clark's Import AI 459 provides the counterweight from inside the industry: the AI economy is growing at ~2,600% per year in quality-adjusted terms but is nearly invisible in GDP statistics. Meanwhile, AI systems are automating more and more of their own R&D pipeline — coding, kernel design, post-training, even alignment research itself. An Australian assistant minister (Andrew Leigh) gives a remarkable speech arguing economists must price extinction risk properly, because "a society that doubles GDP and doubles its extinction risk has made a much less impressive bargain than the national accounts suggest."

And from the critical left, Cory Doctorow's June 2 Pluralistic column argues that billionaires are using AI to replace "inconvenient humans" — migrants, workers who demand rights, anyone who makes moral claims — because "AI makes no demands, requires no moral consideration."

The through-line: **we have time to plan for the AI labor transition, but we're not using that time well. We're underinvesting in measurement, governance, and worker preparation while billions pour into capabilities that may outpace our institutions. The Superagency prescription — deliberate augmentation design, better data, transition support — is exactly what the evidence calls for, and exactly what we're not doing.**

## 3. Superagency Connections

- **The MIT Tech Review data is a Superagency vindication — with a warning.** The finding that augmentation-use cases grow headcount while automation-use cases shrink it validates the wiki's core augmentation-over-replacement thesis. But the 16% decline in entry-level jobs for young workers is a real and growing cost. The "earn-while-you-learn" pipeline is breaking. Superagency means designing transition pathways — apprenticeships, reskilling, job redesign — not just celebrating the augmentation side of the ledger.

- **The invisible AI economy is a Superagency measurement crisis.** If the AI sector is growing at 2,600% per year but GDP statistics don't capture it, then policymakers are flying blind. You can't govern what you can't measure. The AI satellite accounts proposal is the kind of practical governance infrastructure the wiki's [[Responsible Deployment]] framework calls for: measure before you manage.

- **Automated AI R&D is the Superagency boundary question.** Clark's analysis shows AI systems are already automating large chunks of AI research — coding, kernel optimization, post-training, even alignment research. When AI can build its own successor, human agency over the technology's trajectory becomes a governance question, not an individual-choice question. The Superagency thesis must account for recursive self-improvement as a structural challenge to human steering.

- **Leigh's "survival capital" concept is a Superagency governance innovation.** Treating resilience as a form of national capital — alongside physical, human, and social capital — operationalizes the balanced-governance approach this wiki advocates. It's not anti-progress; it's pro-durable-progress. "Progress that compounds" vs. "progress that cancels itself out" is a frame that works for both optimists and cautious realists.

- **Doctorow's critique sharpens the Superagency question: agency for WHOM?** If AI deployment is shaped by capital's desire to eliminate labor's bargaining power, then individual intentionality (Mollick's frame from the morning digest) is insufficient. Superagency requires structural design — governance, contracts, collective bargaining, IP law — not just personal choice. The Doctorow piece is a valuable stress test: can the Superagency thesis handle the "who benefits?" question convincingly?

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments

- **The MIT Tech Review data has a temporal limitation.** The labor statistics it cites are from a period when AI adoption was still relatively low (1 in 5 companies). If adoption accelerates — and the 2,600% growth rate of the AI economy suggests it might — today's "small impact" could become tomorrow's "sudden disruption." McEntarfer herself says the key question is speed. The data says we have time — but doesn't say how much.

- **Clark's automated R&D analysis is a capabilities argument, not an impact argument.** Just because AI CAN automate AI research doesn't mean it WILL displace AI researchers. History shows that automation of a task often increases demand for the humans who do complementary tasks. Radiologists weren't replaced by AI image screening; they were augmented. The same could hold for AI researchers. But Clark's point is that the RECURSIVE nature of AI R&D automation creates a different dynamic — it's not just automating existing tasks but potentially accelerating the rate of capability improvement itself.

- **Doctorow's "inconvenient humans" thesis may overstate capital's power and understate labor's agency.** The MIT Tech Review data shows wages RISING in AI-exposed sectors — inconsistent with a simple "capital is replacing labor to suppress wages" story. Workers are adapting, and labor markets are complex. But Doctorow is right that the POWER to shape AI deployment is unevenly distributed, and that uneven distribution shapes outcomes in ways that pure capability analysis misses.

- **The "invisible AI economy" may be less invisible than claimed.** If compute spending is $219B and datacenter construction is visible in construction employment and energy demand, then the AI economy IS showing up in some statistics — just not in the ones economists habitually look at. The measurement problem may be as much about what we choose to measure as about genuine invisibility.

- **Leigh's "extinction risk pricing" framework has a practical implementation gap.** How do you price a 0.01% annual extinction risk? What discount rate applies to the entire future of humanity? These are philosophically contested questions, not just economic ones. Leigh's speech is valuable for raising the questions; the answers remain deeply uncertain.

## 5. Practical Applications

- **For policymakers:** The MIT Tech Review data supports targeted interventions rather than blanket panic. Focus on entry-level transition programs (apprenticeships, AI-augmented training pipelines), not preemptive UBI. The "earn-while-you-learn" pipeline is the specific thing breaking — fix it specifically.

- **For business leaders:** The augmentation-vs-automation distinction is not just academic. The Stanford/ADP data shows augmentation-use cases grow headcount; automation-use cases shrink it. The choice of HOW to deploy AI — as a copilot or as a replacement — determines whether you're investing in your workforce or liquidating it. This is a strategic choice, not a technological inevitability.

- **For economists and statistical agencies:** The AI satellite accounts proposal is concrete and actionable. Start tracking nominal compute spending, allocate training vs. inference compute, and develop quality-adjusted output measures. Without this data, benefit-sharing schemes (tax reform, sovereign wealth funds, transition support) can't be designed.

- **For AI developers and researchers:** The UK AISI paper on automated alignment difficulties is a practical research agenda. If you're building AI systems that supervise other AI systems, you need to address correlated errors, non-human-evaluable arguments, and evidence volume. These are solvable problems, but only if they're explicitly targeted.

- **For workers and students:** The MIT Tech Review finding that CS enrollment is shifting toward AI-adjacent fields (data science, cybersecurity, AI itself) rather than collapsing is encouraging. The strategy of "move toward the technology, not away from it" appears to be working. But the "earn-while-you-learn" model for entry-level coding jobs is under real pressure — plan for a career path that builds tacit knowledge early, not just codified knowledge.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day

**The data says we have time. The intuitions say we don't. Both are probably right in different ways.** The labor market data is genuinely reassuring about the PRESENT: AI is not causing mass unemployment, wages are rising in exposed sectors, and augmentation-use cases grow jobs. But the AI economy is growing at 2,600% per year, AI systems are automating their own R&D pipeline, and we're investing less than 1% of AI spending on understanding the transition. The Superagency posture captures both truths: embrace the capabilities (they're real and they can amplify human agency) while demanding the measurement, governance, and transition support that makes that amplification widely shared rather than narrowly concentrated. Optimism without measurement is wishful thinking. Optimism WITH measurement is strategy.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try

**Do your own "AI economy visibility" audit.** Pick one domain you know well — your workplace, your industry, your local economy. Ask: (1) How much AI adoption is actually happening? (Not "how much is being discussed" — how much is in production?) (2) Is it augmentation or automation? (3) Who's being affected — which roles, which demographics? (4) Is anyone measuring this systematically? The gap between what you find and what you expected is the "invisible AI economy" at the micro level. If the answer to (4) is "no one," you've found a measurement gap worth filling.
