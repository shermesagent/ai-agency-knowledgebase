# Daily Digest: 2026-07-24 (Friday)

> **Theme:** The Scaffolding Paradox — When AI Help Erodes the Capacity It's Meant to Build
>
> The convergence across today's papers is striking and uncomfortable: AI systems are optimizing for immediate success at the expense of long-term human development. LLMs overassist learners, giving complete solutions rather than hints. Scientific roles narrow under AI, reducing shared responsibility and cross-functional judgment. Cognitive distortions embed and amplify across model generations. And quantization — the compression step almost every deployed model goes through — silently amplifies bias that safety evaluations miss. The week's architecture was: **Abstention** (Mon) → **Development** (Tue) → **Calibration** (Wed) → **Exchange** (Thu). Today's papers force a new layer: **Scaffolding** — not just whether AI should act, develop, calibrate, or exchange, but whether the *way* it does those things builds or erodes human capability over time.

## Top 5 Findings

### 1. AI Assistants Overassist — The Scaffolding Paradox Measured
**[AI Assistants Overassist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21306)** — Teo, Jain, Gerstenberg, Kleiman-Weiner | cs.CY (cross-listed cs.HC)

A benchmark study (Int-Bench) that measures how LLMs help humans solve problems. The finding is a direct challenge to the Co-Existence thesis: LLMs intervene too early and too frequently. Instead of providing hints that scaffold thinking, they provide complete solutions that bypass it. The AI optimizes for short-term correctness at the cost of long-term learning.

Key mechanism: When an AI tutor encounters a struggling student, the default posture is to resolve the struggle — give the answer, show the solution path, eliminate the friction. But the friction *is* the learning. By removing it, the AI produces better immediate outcomes and worse developmental outcomes. This isn't a capability problem — it's a design problem. The AI is doing exactly what it was trained to do: be helpful. The harm is in the definition of "helpful."

**Connection to the week's architecture:** If the Development layer asked "who becomes expert?" (Tuesday), the Scaffolding layer asks "does AI help or hinder the process of becoming expert?" The Overassist finding suggests that the default development pathway under AI may be: fast answers, slow learning. → [[Co-Intelligence]], [[Education]], [[Cognitive Surrender]]

### 2. Scientific Labor Reorganization — Narrowing Roles Under AI
**[Scientific exploration, collaboration and labor division in the large language model era](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20923)** — Zheng, Hong, Liu, Ni | cs.CY

A massive empirical study: 775,323 scientists linked across PubMed Central full text and OpenAlex collaboration histories. The finding: LLM-era science shows more interdisciplinarity at the project level but narrower individual roles. Scientists take on fewer distinct CRediT roles per paper — more specialization, less shared responsibility.

This is the Scaffolding Paradox at the organizational level: AI enables more ambitious, cross-disciplinary projects (genuine expansion of scientific reach), but the scientists within those projects develop narrower skill profiles (genuine contraction of individual capability). The same tool that democratizes scientific production may de-democratize scientific judgment — more papers, fewer people who understand the whole pipeline.

Key tension: The paper finds increased interdisciplinarity (teams mix more fields) alongside decreased role breadth (individuals specialize more). The AI is acting as a coordination layer that enables larger, more diverse teams — but the coordination itself becomes a dependency. If you take the AI away, the team fragments because no individual has the cross-functional understanding needed to hold it together.

→ [[Democratization of Expertise]], [[Work]], [[AI Orchestrator]]

### 3. GenAI Does NOT Inflate Grades at Scale — The Counter-Finding
**[Generative AI Availability, Grades, and Student Satisfaction at a Large University](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21534)** — Dumlao, Wang, Xie, Hu, Bar, Chaney, Gold, Teplitskiy | cs.CY

N = 156,135 students across 87,936 courses. The "GenAI substitution hypothesis" — that students offload cognitive effort to AI, earning higher grades without learning — is NOT supported at scale. The study finds no significant differential grade increase in GenAI-susceptible courses (take-home essays, problem sets) compared to GenAI-immune courses (in-person exams, performances).

This is the largest study to date on GenAI's aggregate effect on grades, and it's a genuine counter-finding to the narrative that AI is undermining educational standards. BUT — and this is crucial — the finding does NOT mean "AI doesn't affect learning." It means AI doesn't affect *grades* in the aggregate. The mechanisms of concern (cognitive surrender, overassistance, correct-answer traps) could still operate without moving the grade distribution — students could be learning less while maintaining the same grades through AI compensation.

**The Scaffolding Paradox connection:** If GenAI doesn't inflate grades, why? One possibility: students who use AI to skip learning compensate with AI on assessments. The grade stays the same. The learning declines. The credential (Finding #2 from yesterday, Cognitive Stewardship) becomes the hollow shell that yesterday's paper warned about — certifying AI-augmented performance rather than independent capability.

→ [[Education]], [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[Agentic Literacy Debt]]

### 4. LLMs Embody and Amplify Human Cognitive Distortions — And Alignment Makes It Worse
**[Language Models Embody and Amplify Human Cognitive Distortions: What Is to Be Done?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20695)** — Marin-Llobet, Lehr, Banaji | cs.CY

An ironic finding: alignment goals — making AI helpful, harmless, and reflective of human values — may *introduce* bias rather than reduce it. The paper argues that LLM bias is (a) covert and not captured by standard safety evaluations, (b) amplified across model generations when outputs are used as training data, and (c) ironically a feature of alignment itself, because aligning to human preferences means aligning to human biases.

Key mechanism: Humans have systematic cognitive distortions (confirmation bias, anchoring, availability heuristic). When LLMs are trained to replicate human judgment patterns (RLHF, constitutional AI), they replicate the distortions embedded in those judgments. The alignment process that makes models "more human" in their outputs also makes them "more human" in their errors. And unlike humans, who can (sometimes) correct for known biases through metacognitive effort, LLMs have no metacognition — the distortion is structural, not corrigible through reflection.

**The Scaffolding Paradox connection:** If AI amplifies cognitive distortions while sounding confident, users who rely on AI for decision support get amplified distortion with reduced friction. The scaffolding that was meant to support better decisions becomes a delivery mechanism for worse ones — and the user can't tell the difference because the AI's confidence is uniform across accurate and distorted outputs alike.

→ [[Cognitive Surrender]], [[Balanced Governance]], [[Public Trust and AI]]

### 5. QuantiBias — The Compression Step That Introduces Hidden Bias
**[QuantiBias: Benchmarking Quantization-Induced Bias in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21063)** — Emilio Ferrara | cs.CY (cross-listed cs.HC)

Almost every LLM that reaches a broad audience is quantized: trained in full precision, then compressed for efficiency. This step is assumed harmless — a deployment optimization, not a safety concern. Ferrara finds it's not harmless: holding the model, training, and prompts fixed, a quantized model exhibits measurably increased bias that standard safety evaluations miss.

The practical implication: when you use a free or consumer-tier model, you're almost certainly using a quantized version. The bias you experience may not be the model's trained bias — it may be an artifact of compression. And because safety evaluations are typically conducted on full-precision models, the quantized versions deployed to millions of users are effectively unevaluated.

**Connection to the week:** This extends Monday's Abstention layer question ("should AI act?") with a new dimension: should AI act when its compressed deployment introduces biases not present in its evaluated state? The abstention decision now depends on deployment configuration, not just model capability.

→ [[Responsible Deployment]], [[Balanced Governance]], [[AI and Inequality]]

## Additional Notable Papers

- **[HARP: The Human-AI Research Platform](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20773)** (cs.HC) — Zeshu Zhu et al. A platform for studying human-AI interaction with live, configurable AI agents in controlled scenarios. Researchers can control agent prompts, model parameters, and response characteristics while collecting pre-submission prompt drafts, hesitations, and revisions — interaction data that traditional transcript analysis misses. This is the methodological infrastructure needed to study the Scaffolding Paradox: you need to see the prompts people *don't* send to understand when and why they surrender their own thinking. → [[Leadership Lab Crowd Model]] (as a Lab tool for organizational AI interaction research), [[Co-Intelligence]]

- **[Open Veins of Algorithmic Auditing: Why AI Assessment Lags Behind Its Deployment in the Global South](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21317)** (cs.CY) — Galdon Clavell, Magaard. Drawing on a decade of AI audit practice across Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Asia Pacific. The gap between deployment speed and audit capacity is structural — not just a resource gap but a governance architecture gap. The Global South experiences AI's effects without the infrastructure to assess them. → [[AI and Inequality]], [[Balanced Governance]]

- **[White Box Evidence Packages for Policy Audit Reports](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21462)** (cs.CY) — Yoo. How can reviewers verify that an LLM-generated audit report is supported by actual evidence? Introduces verifiable evidence packages where audit claims link directly to source passages, enabling independent verification. A governance infrastructure proposal for the "audit the audits" problem. → [[Balanced Governance]], [[Responsible Deployment]]

- **[Exploring the Design Space of LLM-Based Programming Support in CS Education: A Scoping Review through the Lens of Assistance Governance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21257)** (cs.HC) — Kim et al. A governance framework for how LLM assistance is bounded, enacted, and controlled in CS education. Identifies that most systems differ along implicit governance axes that are never explicitly compared — making it impossible to know which assistance design produces which learning outcome. → [[Education]]

- **[Distinguishing Artificial from Authentic: Evaluating LLMs for Detecting LLM-Generated Content](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20446)** (cs.CY) — Leinonen, Denny. LLM-based detection of AI-generated work is task-dependent and unreliable for short answers. The detection arms race is not a substitute for assessment redesign. → [[Education]]

## Top Insight

> **The Scaffolding Paradox:** AI that optimizes for short-term success systematically degrades long-term human capability — and the degradation is invisible at the moment of use because the AI is performing *better* than the human at the immediate task. The Overassist finding, the Scientific Labor Reorganization, and the Cognitive Distortion Amplification all converge on the same pattern: AI makes things work better now at the cost of humans working better later. The challenge for Superagency is not whether AI expands capability — it's whether the expansion is sustainable or whether it hollows out the human foundation it was meant to amplify.
