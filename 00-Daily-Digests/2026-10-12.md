## The Authorship-and-Approval Test

## 1. Executive Summary
Who authors the intention, who checks the artifact, and who can refuse the deployment? Two new arXiv studies distinguish faster AI-assisted production from the human synthesis and classroom judgment that make it useful. WIRED's publishing investigation shows why disclosure, data permissions and worker participation must be designed into the workflow. A separate language-access report offers an important **non-AI** reminder: representation and usable input precede claims of equitable AI support. Five findings below come from **four sources** (two papers, two reported articles), not five independent studies. `web_search` returned empty on two distinct queries; arXiv RSS and directly read WIRED pages supplied the evidence.

## 3. Superagency Connections
- [[AI for School Districts]]: the teacher's instructional purpose and final classroom-readiness decision cannot be inferred from a polished generated lesson.
- [[AI Writing Partners]]: a rapid research summary can free time, but the writer still needs to construct relationships among claims and sources.
- [[AI and Creator Rights]]: ownership covers manuscript input, representational output and worker-controlled process, not only a copyright clause.

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- A teacher-authored rubric and edit trail cost time; in a thinly staffed district, excessive approval steps can crowd out the time AI saves. Use a bounded pilot rather than a universal form for every sentence.
- Editable AI summaries may help novices gain breadth; richer connections with authored notes are not a universal reason to ban summaries. Assign the tool to the learning objective and test unaided understanding.
- Publishers can use approved AI tools to improve outreach without necessarily replacing creative staff. Worker reports and employer statements should both be recorded; neither is a measured causal employment effect.
- A functioning keyboard does not imply a good language model, and Indigenous-language governance is not interchangeable with bilingual/ESL services. Community speakers and linguistic expertise must have decision rights.

## 5. Practical Applications
1. For a teacher-led lesson pilot, record target skill, relevant adopted TEKS, intended student misconception, AI draft, teacher edits, accessibility/language review and a named classroom-readiness approver. Validate against local curriculum before use; do not upload identifiable student work.
2. For research synthesis, compare AI-summary and human-note conditions on *two* outputs: time to restart and the ability to explain a connection between two sources without the tool.
3. For creative-sector procurement, map separately: which unpublished inputs are allowed, who approves external copy, what workflow telemetry is retained, and who may reject a use.

## 8. Top Insight of the Day
**The artifact is not the authority.** A generated lesson, research summary or publicity pitch expands agency only if the responsible human can revise its substance and withhold approval before it reaches others.

## 9. Practical Experiment to Try
On a public, non-sensitive lesson topic, ask one educator to write an instructional intent and likely misconception *before* any AI draft. Generate one draft, then have a second educator independently point to what changed, which TEKS and local resource actually support it, and who can stop classroom use. If either reviewer cannot show an evidence-backed path from intent to approval, revise the process, not just the prompt. Do not use student-level data or unapproved services.
