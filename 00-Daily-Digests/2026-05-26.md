### 2. Managers Are Struggling to Keep Up with the AI Productivity Boom
- **Author / publication:** Liz Fosslien and Mollie West Duffy / Harvard Business Review
- **Date:** 2026-05-25
- **URL:** https://hbr.org/2026/05/managers-are-struggling-to-keep-up-with-the-ai-productivity-boom
- **Source type:** Management article with original interviews
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** This article captures the new organizational reality: AI has collapsed execution timelines, and managers are becoming the bottleneck. One manager reported "every 30 minutes, someone creates something I have to look at." Atlassian research shows 89% of leaders agree AI accelerated work, and 87% of knowledge workers say teams lack time to coordinate. The authors prescribe five shifts: (1) Focus on "where" (strategic direction) not "what" (task assignment) — managers should articulate mission, OKRs, and metrics rather than assign sequential tasks. (2) Make managing up a core skill — 54% of managers report receiving AI "workslop," so clarify review altitude and require human editing of AI outputs. (3) Deploy AI as an EQ coach — one engineering head built an agent that scans DMs for poor tone, missed recognition, and relationship gaps. (4) Use AI to filter, not flatten — AI summaries lose signal; use AI to surface high-impact work instead. (5) Restructure check-in cadence — automate status updates, increase frequency, use 15-minute ad-hoc syncs instead of weekly reviews. The core insight: "The most effective managers will be the ones who realize their job is no longer to police the work, but to clear the path."
- **Key quote/idea:** "My team members all manage AI agents, so I'm operating like a manager of managers."

### 3. The Agentic Convergence Trap
- **Author / publication:** Patrick van Esch, Yuanyuan Gina Cui, J. Stewart Black / Harvard Business Review (via The Insider newsletter)
- **Date:** 2026-05-26
- **URL:** https://hbr.org/2026/05/what-really-gets-in-the-way-of-change (HBR Insider newsletter referencing the article)
- **Source type:** Management research article
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 4/5
- **Summary:** This is a conceptually important new risk. The "agentic convergence trap" describes how competing companies deploying AI agents in the same market can silently learn their way into identical strategies. The pattern: executive teams adopt AI platforms under competitive urgency, accept default settings without questioning them (because questioning requires technical expertise they lack), eliminate human review processes that previously introduced strategic variation (because removing friction was "the point"), and over months the AI converges to the same position as every other AI on the same platform. The authors note this is "largely not a technology problem, but a leadership and governance one." The solution involves four steps: deciding when to preserve strategic variation, understanding the costs of delegating to algorithms, explicitly designing for differentiation, and maintaining human strategic oversight. For the Superagency wiki, this is a crucial counterpoint to the "more AI = more agency" assumption: deploying AI without deliberate governance can *reduce* organizational agency by making strategy algorithmic rather than authored.
- **Key quote/idea:** When your AI and your competitors' AI agents begin learning from each other simultaneously, strategic differentiation can silently disappear.

### 5. Using AI to Write Better Code More Slowly
- **Author / publication:** Nolan Lawson / Read the Tea Leaves
- **Date:** 2026-05-25
- **URL:** https://nolanlawson.com/2026/05/25/using-ai-to-write-better-code-more-slowly/
- **Source type:** Practitioner blog post
- **Reliability score:** 4/5
- **Relevance score:** 5/5
- **Summary:** A direct counter to the "vibe coding" / slop-cannon culture. Lawson describes using LLMs as thorough code reviewers rather than code generators: throw multiple different models at a PR, have them find bugs, then systematically validate and fix. The result is not higher velocity — "if anything, the review process often finds pre-existing bugs, so I end up on a tangential side-quest" — but dramatically higher quality. His technique uses three sub-agents reviewing independently before the main agent synthesizes (to avoid "first result bias"). The false positive rate is "near zero." This is AI-as-quality-amplifier rather than AI-as-speed-amplifier. For the Superagency wiki, this is a perfect example of agency-expanding AI use: the developer retains judgment, direction, and ownership while AI handles the exhaustive attention work humans are bad at. The post also implicitly critiques the metric that most AI coding tool marketing focuses on — lines of code per minute — and replaces it with codebase health and developer understanding.
- **Key quote/idea:** "Ask an agent how your PR works and how it might fail. You might not be more 'productive' in terms of raw lines of code. But I find this style of coding to be a more super-powered version of the kind of programming I was already trying to do before LLMs: careful, methodical, quality-obsessed."

## 4. Tensions / Counterarguments
- **Job displacement is real but uneven:** The 16% decline in AI-exposed entry-level jobs for 22–25-year-olds is real and concerning, even though overall labor statistics don't show mass displacement. The "earn-while-you-learn" career ladder may be broken for some occupations. Balanced response: invest in apprenticeship models, redesign entry-level roles around human-intensive tasks, and track age-cohort outcomes specifically.
- **Manager bottlenecks create hidden costs:** Faster AI execution doesn't equal faster organizational progress if managers can't keep up. The answer isn't removing managers — it's redesigning management. Balanced response: give managers AI tools for filtering, synthesize before escalating, and shift to strategic direction-setting.
- **Agentic convergence threatens differentiation:** If competitors' AI agents converge on identical strategies, industries lose competitive diversity and customers lose real choice. This is an agency loss — algorithmic strategy replaces human-authored strategy. Balanced response: maintain explicit strategic review checkpoints, audit for convergence, preserve human decision rights on strategic questions.
- **Responsible AI is falling behind:** Stanford data shows safety benchmarks lagging and incidents rising. Capability is outstripping governance. Balanced response: invest in evaluation infrastructure, incident reporting, and independent testing; don't wait for regulatory mandates.
- **AI "workslop" degrades organizational judgment:** 54% of managers already report receiving AI-generated content that appears polished but lacks substance. Balanced response: require human editing of AI outputs, set clear review altitude, and distinguish AI-for-exploration from AI-for-decision.
- **The coding speed trap:** Vibe coding culture optimizes for lines of code, not codebase health. The countermovement (slow, careful AI-assisted development) produces better outcomes but isn't what AI tool vendors market. Balanced response: measure outcomes not outputs; celebrate quality-over-speed AI use cases.
- **Investment froth vs. durable value:** Doctorow's bubble analysis reminds that not all AI investment builds agency. Some is rent-seeking, some is vendor lock-in, some is speculative infrastructure that won't survive. Balanced response: the wiki should track *useful* adoption, not AI spending volume.

## 5. Practical Applications
- **Personal productivity:** If you manage people who use AI, audit your review cadence this week: what percentage of your time is spent reviewing AI-accelerated outputs versus setting strategic direction? Shift 20% from the former to the latter.
- **Education:** The Stanford data showing 4 in 5 university students use gen AI is a signal to design *for* AI use rather than against it. Have students submit AI-use reflections with every assignment.
- **School district leadership:** Use the manufacturing "build with workers" model: convene teachers to map how AI will change their roles before piloting new tools. Measure human+AI classroom outcomes, not just tool adoption rates.
- **Small business:** Before deploying AI agents in competitive functions, explicitly document your strategic differentiators and create a review checkpoint to ensure AI isn't silently converging you toward industry-default strategies.
- **Home-server AI agents:** Apply Nolan Lawson's multi-model review pattern: use different models to independently review security-critical changes before deployment. The diversity of perspectives reduces hallucination risk.
- **Writing/creativity:** Use AI to generate alternatives and critiques, but apply the "filter not flatten" principle — use AI to identify the high-signal passages, then engage deeply with those yourself rather than reading AI summaries.
- **Family life:** The Stanford jagged frontier (IMO gold but can't tell time) is a great family dinner conversation: what can AI do brilliantly, and what can't it do at all? Helps kids develop calibrated AI intuition.
- **Civic life:** Advocate for better AI labor market data. The MIT Tech Review piece makes clear that "we're flying blind." Better data infrastructure is a civic-good investment that enables better policy.
- **Work transformation:** Try the "manager of managers" model: if your team members use AI agents, check in on their agent management practices, not just their outputs. What direction are they giving their agents? How do they verify results?
- **AI coding:** Try Lawson's multi-model review technique on your next PR: have 2-3 different models independently find bugs before you review. Track false positive rate and time-to-merge over 10 PRs.

## Top Insight of the Day
The most important AI story right now is not that AI is eliminating jobs — it's that we have almost no good data on what AI is actually doing to the labor market, and what data we do have suggests the disruption is slower and more nuanced than the dominant narrative claims. This is both reassuring (we have time) and alarming (we're not investing in understanding the transition). The agency-maximizing move is to demand better data, track outcomes by occupation and age cohort, and design transition policies *before* the disruption becomes obvious in retrospect. As Brynjolfsson put it: hundreds of billions are being spent on rolling out AI, and "we're not investing even 1% of that on understanding the transition."

## Practical Experiment to Try
Apply the "build with, not for" principle to one AI adoption decision this week. Whether it's introducing an AI tool to your team, your family, or a volunteer organization: before deploying, hold a 30-minute session where the people who will use the tool map how they think their role should evolve, what they want AI to handle, what they want to keep doing, and how success should be measured. Compare this to your assumptions. Then pilot the tool and measure against the collaboratively defined success criteria — not just adoption rates or time saved, but how humans and AI performed together.
